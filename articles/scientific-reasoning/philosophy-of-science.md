# Philosophy of science

Philosophy of science is the second-order study of the sciences: instead of investigating nature directly, it asks how scientific investigation works and what entitles its results to our trust.[^s2] Its recurring questions concern method (observation, experiment, induction, deduction, modelling and hypothesis testing), the aims those methods serve, and the higher-level values used to justify one method over another.[^s1] For critical thinking it supplies the vocabulary behind everyday judgments such as "that claim is untestable", "one study doesn't prove it" and "the evidence supports the theory".

## Origin

Reflection on scientific method is old. Aristotle joined careful observation to logical reasoning, Francis Bacon warned against leaping from a few particulars to sweeping generalizations, and Newton's rules in the *Principia* became a touchstone for inductivism; Hume's challenge to the justification of induction then pressed later thinkers to find new foundations.[^s1] In the nineteenth century John Stuart Mill treated induction as the core of method, while William Whewell stressed the creative role of ideas in discovery followed by testing through consilience.[^s1]

The field took its modern, professional shape in the 1920s and 1930s. The Vienna Circle, led by Moritz Schlick and including Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath and Hans Hahn, met from 1924, published its manifesto in 1929 and took over the journal *Erkenntnis* in 1930.[^s2] Thomas Uebel's account credits the Circle with providing the blueprint for analytic philosophy of science as meta-theoretical reflection on the sciences; when its members emigrated in the 1930s, largely to the United States and Britain, the programme went with them.[^s2]

## How it works

The field is organised around a handful of standing problems.[^s1][^s4]

- **Confirmation.** How much does a passed test support a hypothesis? On the hypothetico-deductive view, confirmed predictions lend support but never proof.[^s1]
- **Demarcation.** What separates science from pseudo-science? Karl Popper answered with falsifiability: genuine science makes risky predictions it could fail.[^s1]
- **Testing in bundles.** Pierre Duhem observed that an experiment tests a hypothesis together with auxiliary assumptions about instruments and conditions, so a failed prediction does not say which assumption to drop.[^s5]
- **Theory change.** Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) described normal science conducted within a paradigm, accumulating anomalies, crisis and revolution, and argued that shared values such as accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness guide theory choice without dictating it.[^s3]
- **Method and pluralism.** Paul Feyerabend argued that rigid methodological rules hamper progress, while recent work favours a middle view on which methods differ across disciplines yet share common features.[^s1]

A workplace example shows the questions in use. A team's A/B test finds that a redesigned checkout page lifted conversions, and a manager declares the redesign "proven". Philosophy of science suggests the review questions: was the prediction risky enough that it could have failed (demarcation)? Does one passed test confirm the hypothesis or only fail to refute it (confirmation)? If a follow-up test fails, is the redesign wrong, or was the tracking script, the traffic mix or the season at fault (Duhem's point about auxiliaries)? Is the team so committed to its design framework that it is explaining away anomalies (Kuhn's normal science)?[^s1][^s3][^s5]

## Why it matters for critical thinking

Philosophy of science informs what schools teach as the nature of science. Juli Eflin, Stuart Glennan and George Reisch, writing in 1999, argued that philosophers disagree less about the nature of science than survey results suggested, offered a taxonomy of the philosophical issues involved, and proposed roles for philosophy of science in science teaching and teacher education.[^s4] Andersen notes that school curricula often present "the scientific method" as a fixed sequence of steps, whereas working scientists often reject the idea of a single method.[^s1] The field also reaches courtrooms: in the United States the *Daubert* standard for admitting expert testimony draws on philosophy of science, in what Andersen describes as an inconsistent blend of Popper and Hempel.[^s1]

## Limitations

Philosophy of science is a normative and conceptual discipline whose central questions remain open. The Vienna Circle's verification criterion of meaning was abandoned by Hempel around 1950, and Quine's 1951 attack on the analytic/synthetic distinction undercut a pillar of logical empiricism.[^s2] Kuhn's account drew charges of relativism and irrationality because he denied that rules fix theory choice, charges he himself rejected.[^s3] Popper's falsificationism leaves unresolved when to blame an auxiliary assumption rather than abandon a hypothesis.[^s5] Sociologists of knowledge have further argued that social factors, rather than method, explain which beliefs become accepted.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Influenced by logical empiricism.** The Vienna Circle's programme of logical analysis of scientific language set the template for the discipline.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Thomas Kuhn.** *Structure* turned the field toward the history of science and naturalistic approaches and away from a rule-governed, cumulative picture of science.[^s3]
- **Applied in science education.** Eflin, Glennan and Reisch set out how philosophy of science can inform what students and teachers learn about the nature of science.[^s4]
- **Applied in legal reasoning.** Courts weighing scientific expert testimony have borrowed falsifiability and confirmation ideas from the field.[^s1]
- **Related.** It applies epistemology's questions to scientific knowledge; falsifiability, hypothesis testing and the scientific method are its core topics; the problem of induction and the Duhem-Quine thesis are its classic challenges; Popper and Whewell are among its central figures.[^s1][^s5]
