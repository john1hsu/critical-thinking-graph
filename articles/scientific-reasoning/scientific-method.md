# Scientific method

The scientific method is the name for the activities through which science earns its reliability: systematic observation, experiment, inductive and deductive reasoning, building models, and testing hypotheses.[^s1] Andersen's Stanford Encyclopedia entry separates three layers that the phrase often blurs: the aims and products of inquiry (knowledge, prediction, control), the meta-methodological values used to justify a method (such as objectivity, reproducibility and simplicity), and day-to-day practice such as laboratory techniques and reporting conventions.[^s1] At its core, the Berkeley Understanding Science project says, science must test ideas against evidence from the natural world, even though the route by which it does so is iterative and nonlinear.[^s5]

## Origin

There is no single inventor. Aristotle made careful observation the starting point of inquiry and distinguished reasoning upward from observations to principles from reasoning downward from principles to cases, and medieval writers such as Grosseteste and Ockham refined the rules of induction.[^s1] Francis Bacon's *Novum Organum* (1620) attacked both the Aristotelian syllogism, which he thought could not discover anything new, and induction by simple enumeration; in their place he proposed eliminative induction built on tables of presence, absence and degrees, experiments designed to answer specific questions, and a catalogue of systematic errors he called the Idols.[^s4] Bacon's programme inspired the collaborative, experimental ethos of the Royal Society, founded in 1660.[^s4]

Later debates sharpened the picture. Newton's rules of reasoning fed an inductivist tradition; in the nineteenth century Whewell stressed the creative "colligation" of facts and the consilience of inductions while Mill codified rules for finding causes; the logical empiricists tied meaning to observation; Popper made risky, potentially refuting tests the mark of science; and Kuhn argued that the standards guiding research shift with paradigms.[^s1] Feyerabend's *Against Method* went furthest, claiming that any fixed methodological rule would have blocked real scientific progress.[^s1] The familiar four- or five-step school recipe (observe, hypothesise, experiment, analyse, conclude) is a later teaching device that Andersen traces partly to Pearson's *Grammar of Science* (1892) and Dewey's *How We Think* (1910).[^s1]

## How it works

A workplace example shows the moving parts. A product team notices that many customers abandon checkout on mobile phones (observation). One engineer suspects the address form is the problem (hypothesis) and reasons that, if so, a shorter form should lower abandonment while leaving desktop rates unchanged (deduced prediction). The team runs a randomised trial, analyses the data, and shares the result for colleagues to probe; a sceptical analyst points out that a payment-provider outage overlapped the test, so the trial is repeated. This loop matches the Berkeley description of real science: ideas are sorted, the ones that survive testing are kept and built on, and the scientific community's scrutiny is part of the process rather than an afterthought.[^s5] Notice that the work also drew on exploratory data, background theory and judgment that no step list prescribes, which is why philosophers distinguish general method from the specific research strategies scientists actually use.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Treat the method as a set of checks rather than a recipe. State the claim precisely; ask what observable result would count against it; prefer tests that could fail; compare cases where the effect is present, absent and varying in degree, as Bacon's tables recommend; watch for the habits of mind he labelled Idols, such as being swayed by loose words or inherited doctrine; and expose results to criticism and replication before relying on them.[^s4][^s5] Avoid two pitfalls: assuming that a study without a formal hypothesis is unscientific, and assuming that following the steps guarantees a correct answer.[^s3][^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

Whether science has one method is, in Andersen's account, the central methodological debate of the last half century, with positions ranging from unificationism to radical pluralism; she suggests a moderate pluralism fits best, and that diversity of methods across fields helps explain science's reliability rather than undermining it.[^s1] Newer practices such as computer simulation, data-driven "omics" research and machine learning have widened that pluralism further.[^s1] Woodcock argued in 2014 that textbook presentations of "the Scientific Method" disagree widely with one another, have no canonical source in the philosophy or history of science, and should be abandoned rather than kept as a handy approximation.[^s2] The myth persists in classrooms: in a 2021 survey of 56 UK science teachers, 57% agreed there is a universal scientific method, 61% thought hypothesis testing necessary for an inquiry to count as scientific, and more than half held what the authors classed as naive views of scientific methods.[^s3] Reproducibility failures reported since the early 2000s have added practical doubts about how well everyday research lives up to methodological ideals.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Broader: reasoning; requires inductive and deductive reasoning.** The method combines generalising from observations with deriving testable consequences from hypotheses.[^s1]
- **Studied in philosophy of science.** Its aims, rules and justification are the subject of a long philosophical literature.[^s1]
- **Influenced by Francis Bacon.** The *Novum Organum*'s eliminative induction and designed experiments shaped seventeenth-century experimental philosophy.[^s4]
- **Influenced by William Whewell.** Whewell's colligation and consilience of inductions emphasised the creative, hypothesis-forming side of method.[^s1]
- **Challenged by *Against Method*.** Feyerabend argued that no fixed rule survives the history of science.[^s1]
- **Applied in science education.** School curricula are where the single-method picture is most entrenched, and where researchers urge teaching methodological diversity instead.[^s3][^s2]
- **Related.** Hypothesis testing, falsifiability, Mill's methods and randomised controlled trials are components or refinements of the method; the replication crisis tests how well it is practised.[^s1]
