# Rule against conventionalist stratagems

The rule against conventionalist stratagems is Karl Popper's methodological commitment that when evidence contradicts a theory, the theory's defenders will not escape by the standard tricks that make any theory irrefutable: adding ad hoc assumptions, shifting the meaning of key terms, dismissing the observers, or dismissing the critic.[^s1] It does not forbid revising a theory. It forbids revisions whose only effect is to make the contrary evidence stop counting.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Popper set out the rule in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (English edition 1959), in the chapter on falsifiability, as his answer to conventionalism, the view that a theory can always be kept by adjusting other parts of the system.[^s1] As David Merritt's 2017 analysis in *Studies in History and Philosophy of Modern Physics* summarises it, Popper listed four such stratagems: introducing ad hoc hypotheses, modifying the "ostensive definitions" of terms, adopting a sceptical attitude towards the reliability of the experimenter, and, as a last resort, casting doubt on the acumen of the theoretician who raised the problem.[^s1] Popper granted that conventionalism could not be refuted on logical grounds; the only way to avoid it, he wrote, was a decision not to apply its methods.[^s1]

## How it works

The rule has two operational clauses. First, auxiliary hypotheses are acceptable only if their introduction does not lower the degree of falsifiability or testability of the system; in practice the new assumption should make further predictions of its own.[^s1] Second, a change of definitions is permitted, but it must be treated as a modification of the system, which then has to be re-examined as if it were a new theory.[^s1] The old claim does not get credit for surviving a test that only the redefined claim passed.

A workplace example: a manager says the new onboarding programme gets every new hire productive within a month. One hire, who completed the programme, is still struggling after six weeks. The manager replies that this person never *really* went through onboarding, because properly onboarded people become productive. Under the rule, that reply is a redefinition: "onboarded" has changed from "completed the programme" to "became productive". It may be adopted, but the result is a new, near-empty claim that needs its own evidence, and the original claim stands refuted.[^s1][^s5] The same reply would also break the rule's third and fourth clauses if it became "the person who measured productivity was unreliable" or "whoever raised this does not understand onboarding" without any independent reason to think so.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. **Fix the terms before the test.** Write down what would count as a counterexample while the claim is still untested.[^s1]
2. **When a rescue is proposed, ask what else it predicts.** A legitimate auxiliary can be checked independently; an ad hoc one explains away only the anomaly it was invented for.[^s3]
3. **Treat redefinitions as new claims.** If a key word has changed meaning, restate the claim and evaluate it from scratch.[^s1]
4. **Watch for all-purpose escape routes.** Boudry argues that a rescue is especially suspect when it could be applied to any anomaly at all, or is used inconsistently.[^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

The rule is normative, a proposed standard rather than an empirical finding. Its main use has been in diagnosing pseudoscience. Pigliucci's IEP survey lists arbitrary "immunization" from empirical criticism among the recognised epistemic failings of pseudoscience, drawing on Boudry and Braeckman (2011).[^s4] Boudry's 2011 dissertation catalogues such moves, including equivocal "moving target" claims, conspiracy thinking that treats disconfirming evidence as further proof, and changing the rules of evidence when criticised.[^s2]

The rule has limits. Pigliucci notes that falsificationism lacks tools to explain why some ad hoc hypotheses, such as Le Verrier's postulated planet Neptune, turned out to be justified.[^s4] Boudry observes that Lakatos's "protective belt" of auxiliaries shows that genuine science also shields its core claims, so immunizing moves are a matter of degree rather than a clean dividing line.[^s2] Merritt's own application, which argues that dark matter and dark energy work as conventionalist stratagems in modern cosmology, is a contested reading by one author.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Karl Popper in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*.** Merritt traces the four stratagems and the decision against them to that book.[^s1]
- **Counteracts the no true Scotsman.** The no true Scotsman saves a generalisation by narrowing the meaning of the group term; the Fallacy Files classes it as redefinition that buys safety from refutation.[^s5] Popper's clause that a changed definition creates a new system to be re-examined blocks exactly that move.[^s1]
- **Detects pseudoscience.** Resort to immunization is one of the marks used to identify pseudoscientific belief systems.[^s4]
- **Challenged by Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes.** Lakatos's protective belt legitimises some shielding of a theory's core, which blurs the line the rule draws.[^s2]
- **Broader principle and neighbours.** The rule applies the [falsifiability criterion](falsifiability.md) to the defence of theories; [ad hoc hypothesis](ad-hoc-hypothesis.md) covers the first stratagem in detail.[^s1][^s3]
