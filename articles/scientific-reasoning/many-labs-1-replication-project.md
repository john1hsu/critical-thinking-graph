# Many Labs 1 replication project

The Many Labs 1 replication project was a large collaborative study that asked two questions at once: do well-known psychology effects replicate, and does the answer depend on where and with whom they are tested? Thirty-six research teams ran 13 effects from 12 published studies through the same short session, collecting data from 6,344 participants in 27 lab and 9 online samples, 25 in the United States and 11 elsewhere.[^s1] It is often cited as evidence that some judgment-and-decision effects, such as anchoring, are sturdy, while others, notably several social priming effects, are not.[^s3]

## Origin

The project began in 2011 at the Center for Open Science, at a time when published replications were scarce and social psychology faced what commentators called a crisis of confidence.[^s3] The report, led by Richard Klein, Kate Ratliff, Michelangelo Vianello and Brian Nosek, appeared in *Social Psychology* in 2014. The team fixed a confirmatory analysis plan before data collection and posted materials and data openly.[^s1] Pooling many small labs into one semi-anonymous effort was meant to spread the work and soften the professional risk of challenging well-known findings.[^s3]

## How it works

Every participant completed the same standardized web procedure with all 13 tasks in randomized order, so any difference between sites could not come from different materials. The effects included four anchoring questions (for instance, estimating the height of Mount Everest after seeing a low or high number), gain-versus-loss framing, the retrospective gambler's fallacy, sunk costs, the norm of reciprocity, quote attribution, allowed-versus-forbidden question wording, imagined intergroup contact, two math-attitude effects, and two priming effects that used a national flag and images of money.[^s1]

Think of a retail chain that wants to know whether a new checkout layout speeds up queues. Testing it in one flagship store tells you little; running the identical layout in 36 stores of different sizes and regions tells you both whether it works and whether "it depends on the store." Many Labs applied that logic to psychology: the same protocol, many sites, and a direct estimate of how much results vary across them.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

Ten effects replicated, imagined contact received only weak support (significant in 4 of 36 samples), and flag priming and currency priming did not replicate, with pooled effects near zero.[^s1] The anchoring effects came out larger than the original estimate, which the authors read as the original understating anchoring.[^s1] Variation was driven mainly by which effect was studied rather than by sample or setting: lab versus online and U.S. versus international administration made little systematic difference.[^s1]

The authors cautioned that the 13 effects were a small, non-random set and the sites were mostly Western and educated, so the 10-of-13 rate should not be read as psychology's overall replication rate.[^s1] The much larger Many Labs 2 (2018), with 28 effects in 125 samples from 36 countries, found a lower replication rate, with only about half of effects significant and a median effect size well below the originals, yet it confirmed the first project's central finding that effect, not setting, accounts for most variability.[^s2]

## Connections

Many Labs 1 is a landmark of the replication crisis and an early model of open, preregistered multi-site replication that inspired further Many Labs rounds.[^s3] It sits alongside other large replication efforts of the period, such as the 2015 Reproducibility Project. It is studied in social psychology, since most of its effects and its moderator tests come from that field.[^s1] Its main conclusion, that variability depends on the effect more than on the sample, is supported by Many Labs 2.[^s2] Within this graph it serves as replication evidence for anchoring, the gambler's fallacy, framing and the sunk-cost effect, which all reproduced, and as a caution against priming effects, which did not.[^s1]
