# Steelmanning

**Steelmanning** is the practice of restating someone else's argument in the strongest form you can defend, filling in missing premises, swapping a weak reason for a better one and setting aside slips of wording, and only then evaluating it. It inverts the straw man: where a straw man makes an opponent's case weaker than it is, a steelman makes it stronger.[^s1][^s2] Argumentation theorists usually discuss the move under the heading of charity, and R. A. J. Shields describes steelmanning as strengthening another's argument beyond what its author stated or intended, so that one answers the best available version rather than a weak one.[^s3]

## Origin

The ideal of answering the best version of a view is older than the name. Textbook principles of charity from Michael Scriven onward tell the interpreter to seek the strongest or best possible reconstruction of an argument; Shields calls these "maximal" charity principles and contrasts them with weaker, "sub-maximal" ones.[^s3] In the argumentation literature, Scott Aikin and John Casey named the practice of making an opponent's argument stronger than it is the **iron man**, presenting it at the 2013 OSSA and 2014 ISSA conferences and in a 2016 *Topoi* article.[^s2][^s1] "Steelman" is the more common informal name for the same move; Shields uses it while noting that its theory is still largely unworked.[^s3]

## How it works

Aikin and Casey describe the structure as three-sided: a speaker offers an argument that is not defensible as stated, a critic rebuilds it into a more defensible version, and often does so for an audience that wants to know whether the conclusion is true.[^s2] They give three reasons the rebuild can be worthwhile: an epistemic one (understanding comes from facing the most robust case), an ethical one (charitable reading respects the speaker and lets under-heard interests be voiced), and a pedagogical one (showing someone a better version of their own view).[^s2]

A workplace example: in a planning meeting a colleague says the team should drop its weekly status meeting "because nobody reads the notes anyway". Answered as stated, the claim is easy to dismiss, since note-reading is not the meeting's purpose. A steelman reconstructs the case: the meeting costs ten person-hours a week; most of its information already appears in the task tracker; the decisions it exists for could be raised asynchronously or in a shorter call. The critic now answers that version, for example by asking whether cross-team blockers actually surface in the tracker, and the discussion is about what the meeting is for rather than about one weak sentence, which is the payoff Aikin and Casey attribute to engaging the most robust version of a case.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Restate first.** Put the other person's position in your own words and check that they accept the restatement before you add anything.
2. **Repair, then label.** Supply missing premises and stronger evidence, but say openly which parts are your additions, so the improved argument is not passed off as theirs; Aikin and Casey warn that a reconstruction can make the original critics look as if they had attacked a straw man.[^s2]
3. **Answer the strong version.** Direct your objections at the reconstruction; if they still land, they are more likely to be decisive.[^s3]
4. **Know when to stop.** Aikin and Casey call iron manning fallacious when the argument is clearly irrelevant or hopeless, when improving it costs more time than more pressing issues deserve, or when engaging it rewards bad-faith contributions such as trolling.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

Steelmanning is a normative recommendation rather than a tested intervention, and there is little direct experimental work on it. Related evidence comes from Stanley and colleagues (2020), who found that people judged ideological opponents as less likely than allies to have good reasons, linked that judgment to seeing opponents as less intelligent and less moral, and reported that exposure to arguments for the opposing side improved impressions of those opponents.[^s4] That study manipulated exposure to opposing arguments, not participants' own reconstruction of them, so it supports the technique only indirectly. Conceptually, Shields flags open problems: a steelmanned version may be dismissed as no longer the opponent's argument, and in public debate a steelman can itself shade into strawmanning by misrepresenting what was said.[^s3] Aikin and Casey's broader point is that the virtue lies in knowing when to strengthen an opponent's case, not in always doing so.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Counteracts the straw man fallacy.** Aikin and Casey frame iron manning as the mirror image of straw manning, the corrective move of engaging a stronger rather than a weaker version.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with the iron man fallacy.** The same strengthening becomes a fallacy when it drifts from the actual question, wastes the audience's attention or misrepresents the original exchange.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with the principle of charity.** Charity asks for the most reasonable reading of what was said; steelmanning may go beyond the speaker's intent, and Shields suggests it may be a precondition of maximal charity rather than the same thing.[^s3]
- **Supported by evidence on opposing reasons.** Exposure to the other side's arguments reduced derogation of opponents in Stanley and colleagues' experiments, an indirect rationale for seeking out the best case.[^s4]
- It sits alongside **devil's advocacy** and **consider the opposite**, which also bring the strongest counter-case into view, and to **actively open-minded thinking**: Aikin and Casey use the line between apt and fallacious iron manning to mark the limits of open-mindedness as a virtue.[^s1]
