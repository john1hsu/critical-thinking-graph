# Argumentative theory of reasoning

The argumentative theory of reasoning holds that the evolved function of human reasoning is to devise arguments that persuade others and to evaluate the arguments others put forward. On this view reasoning is not mainly a private tool for reaching truer beliefs or better decisions; it is an adaptation to a species that depends heavily on communication and is therefore exposed to misinformation.[^s1] The theory reinterprets many documented reasoning failures as the expected behaviour of a mechanism used outside the social setting it is designed for.[^s1]

## Origin

Hugo Mercier, then at the University of Pennsylvania, and Dan Sperber, of the Institut Jean Nicod in Paris and Central European University, set out the hypothesis in a 2011 target article in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. The journal published it with open peer commentary and the authors' reply, running to page 111.[^s1] They developed it into a book, *The Enigma of Reason* (Harvard University Press, 2017), which presents reason as a means of justifying oneself to others, persuading them and assessing what they say.[^s2]

## How it works

The theory separates two tasks. When **producing** arguments, a reasoner looks for support for a position already held and seldom looks for counterevidence unless expecting to rebut it. Mercier and Sperber treat this one-sidedness, usually called confirmation or myside bias, as a feature of a persuasion device rather than a malfunction.[^s1] When **evaluating** other people's arguments, by contrast, people are expected to be fairly demanding and fairly accurate, because accepting bad arguments is costly.[^s1]

Put those together and reasoning should work best in a group that disagrees and shares an interest in getting the answer right. Each member builds a one-sided case, the others test it, and good arguments tend to win. It should work worst when a person reasons alone, or only with people who already agree, so that nothing they say is challenged.[^s1] Mercier and Sperber also read reason-based choice, belief perseverance and polarisation among like-minded people as effects of looking for reasons to justify a view one already holds.[^s1]

**Workplace example.** An engineer writes a design proposal and, alone, finds every reason it will work while missing a scaling flaw. In review, a colleague who prefers a different design spots the flaw within minutes. The argumentative theory predicts both halves: weak self-critique in production, sharp critique in evaluation.[^s1]

## How to apply it

- Pair or group people on hard problems and build in real disagreement. Discussion that lets the person with the correct answer argue for it can lift accuracy far above individual performance.[^s1]
- Do not rely on asking authors to critique their own work. Have someone other than the author review it, or make the author defend it to a sceptic.[^s3]
- Watch for groups whose members already agree. Discussion among like-minded people tends to strengthen their shared view rather than test it.[^s1]
- Expect people to underestimate how much argument helps. Without evidence, managers and even reasoning researchers judge group discussion less useful than it is.[^s4]

## Evidence

The 2011 paper drew on existing data. On the Wason selection task, individuals averaged about 10% correct while discussion groups reached about 80%, typically because one member who saw the solution persuaded the rest.[^s1] In hypothesis-testing studies, fewer than one in ten participants falsified their own hypothesis, even when told to try.[^s1]

Later tests by Mercier and colleagues were designed around the theory. Trouche and colleagues (2016) used a choice-blindness manipulation to show participants their own earlier argument presented as someone else's. Among those who did not notice the swap, 56% and 58% in two experiments rejected their own argument, and they were more likely to reject it when it was invalid. The authors call this pattern selective laziness: people are lax with their own reasoning and stricter with what looks like someone else's.[^s3] Mercier and colleagues (2015) ran six studies with samples from the United States, India and Japan, European managers and psychologists of reasoning. Every sample underestimated how much group discussion improves Wason-task performance.[^s4]

Independent extensions are more mixed. A 2024 study of 354 Argentine undergraduates found that small groups beat individuals on valid syllogisms, but did no better, and in one study worse, on invalid conclusions that sounded believable. The authors describe belief bias as only slightly reduced in groups.[^s6]

## Limitations

The theory is contested. In a 2020 article in *Episteme*, the philosopher Sinan Dogramaci argued that its key premise, that getting others to adopt one's existing beliefs gives an evolutionary advantage, was not established and is false. He also questioned whether myside bias is good evidence for the theory, and argued that a justificatory or reputation-managing function is not a plausible evolutionary function either.[^s5] Much of the supporting experimental work comes from the theory's proponents, and claims about evolved function are hard to test directly.[^s3][^s5] The safest practical reading is narrower than the evolutionary claim: reasoning that is exposed to motivated critics tends to beat reasoning done alone.[^s1][^s6]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber** in their 2011 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* target article.[^s1]
- **Studied in the psychology of reasoning.** The theory is a reinterpretation of experimental findings about how people reason, such as the Wason selection task, confirmation bias and motivated reasoning.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by the selective-laziness study.** Trouche and colleagues' choice-blindness experiments are a direct test of the claim that people evaluate others' arguments more strictly than their own.[^s3]
- **Supported by the cross-cultural studies of argumentation.** Mercier and colleagues' six studies show that group discussion helps much more than people expect, across several cultures.[^s4]
- **Challenged by Dogramaci (2020)**, who disputes the evolutionary premise and the weight placed on myside bias.[^s5]
- **Related to myside bias, confirmation bias and motivated reasoning**, which the theory recasts as features of argument production; to epistemic vigilance, the evaluation side; to belief bias and groupthink, which show where group reasoning can still fail; and to argumentation theory, the normative study of arguments.[^s1][^s6]
