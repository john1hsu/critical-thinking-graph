# Dialectical relevance

Dialectical relevance is the idea that whether an argument is relevant cannot be read off the sentences alone: it depends on what the argument is being used for inside a particular kind of conversation.[^s1] In Douglas Walton's account, a move is relevant when it contributes to the goal of the dialogue it belongs to, and irrelevant when it heads somewhere that goal does not need, even if it talks about the same subject.[^s1][^s2] It gives critics a working test for fallacies of relevance such as the red herring, instead of a vague sense that a remark is off-topic.[^s1]

## Origin

Informal logic had long treated relevance as one of the basic tests of a good argument. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on informal logic lists it, with acceptability and sufficiency, among the criteria Ralph Johnson and J. Anthony Blair set out from 1977, where a premise is relevant if it gives any support at all to the conclusion.[^s4] Walton's contribution was to make the test dialectical. His 1999 paper in *Informal Logic* argued that relevance is a matter of how an argument is used for a purpose in a type of dialogue, and worked the idea out for persuasion dialogue.[^s1] He expanded it into the book *Relevance in Argumentation*, published in 2004 by Lawrence Erlbaum (now Routledge), which presents a method for evaluating arguments for relevance that draws on argumentation theory, logic and artificial intelligence, with a chapter on fallacies of irrelevance.[^s3] Fabrizio Macagno's 2008 review of Walton's pragmatic theory treats dialogue types and dialectical relevance as central to his whole programme.[^s2]

## How it works

Walton's starting point is that conversations have different goals. Following Walton and Krabbe, Macagno lists persuasion, inquiry, negotiation, information-seeking, deliberation and eristic (quarrel) dialogues.[^s2] A move that is relevant in one type may be irrelevant in another: an offer of a concession fits a negotiation but does nothing to prove a claim in a persuasion dialogue.[^s2]

For persuasion dialogue, Walton lists minimal requirements on a proponent's argument: the respondent must accept the premises, each inference must be structurally correct, and the chain of argument must end in the thesis the proponent has to prove.[^s1] Relevance concerns the third requirement. It is a question of where the reasoning is going, not of whether the premises are true.[^s1] Macagno spells out Walton's distinction between *topical* relevance, where two statements share a subject, and *probative* relevance, where one actually helps prove or disprove the other.[^s2] Walton's own illustration is a parliamentary speech that is clearly on the topic of a bill yet supports nothing that bears on whether the bill should pass, so it is topically relevant but materially irrelevant.[^s1]

A workplace case shows the test. A team is in a persuasion dialogue about whether to replace its ticketing tool, and one member spends the meeting explaining how the current vendor's support team treated her badly last year. The story is about the tool's vendor, so it is topically relevant. Chained forward, though, it leads to "the vendor was rude", which does not connect to the thesis about whether the tool meets the team's needs unless someone adds a premise such as "support quality will matter after renewal". Without that link the move is dialectically irrelevant; with it, it becomes a legitimate, if minor, reason, which is the kind of distinction Walton's forward-chaining test is meant to draw.[^s1][^s2]

Walton also allows for shifts between dialogue types. A shift is acceptable when the embedded dialogue serves the original goal, as when a trial pauses to hear an expert, and illicit when it replaces that goal, as when a deliberation is turned into a quarrel or an unwanted negotiation.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Name the dialogue type and the thesis at issue.** Ask what the conversation is for and what exactly has to be shown, since that fixes what can count as relevant.[^s1][^s2]
2. **Extrapolate.** Walton's method chains the disputed argument forward and the thesis backward and checks whether the two chains meet; argument diagrams make those pathways visible.[^s1]
3. **Separate topic from proof.** Shared subject matter is not enough; look for probative bearing on the thesis.[^s2]
4. **Check for a dialogue shift.** If the move makes sense only in a different kind of exchange, ask whether that shift helps the original goal or derails it.[^s2]
5. **Judge charitably.** Walton notes that the same method can vindicate a move that only looked off-topic.[^s1]

## Limitations

Walton concedes that evidence of where an argument is heading may be incomplete, and a political debate may be partly deliberation rather than persuasion, which changes what counts as relevant.[^s1] Macagno adds that formal dialogue models were not always realistic, that the six-type classification is broad and institutional settings may need subtypes, and that relevance depends on interpreting participants' goals, which can itself be misrepresented.[^s2] The criterion is also only a threshold: in the Johnson-Blair scheme a relevant premise may still fall short of sufficient support.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Detects the red herring.** Walton analyses the red herring as a move that fails the requirement that the chain of argument lead to the thesis, combined with a distracting line of talk, so the extrapolation test is how a critic exposes it.[^s1]
- **Detects *ignoratio elenchi*.** He ties dialectical irrelevance directly to the fallacy of irrelevant conclusion, where an argument proves something other than what had to be proved.[^s1]
- **Introduced by Douglas Walton.** Macagno presents dialectical relevance as one of the central elements of Walton's dialogical theory.[^s2]
- **Introduced in *Relevance in Argumentation*.** The 2004 book sets out the full method for judging moves relevant or irrelevant.[^s3]
- **Influenced by pragma-dialectics.** Walton relates his account to van Eemeren and Grootendorst's rules for critical discussion, and Macagno describes him folding their stage-by-stage view of relevant speech acts into probative relevance.[^s1][^s2]
- **Studied in argumentation theory and informal logic.** Relevance is one of the field's standard evaluation criteria, and the SEP entry describes the dialectical, dialogue-type framework in which Walton's version sits.[^s4]
