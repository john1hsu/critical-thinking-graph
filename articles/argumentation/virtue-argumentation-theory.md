# Virtue argumentation theory

Virtue argumentation theory (VAT) asks not only "is this a good argument?" but also "is this person arguing well?". It borrows the agent-centred approach of virtue ethics and virtue epistemology and applies it to argument, so that the good arguer, and not just the good argument, becomes something to appraise.[^s1] Good arguing is described through argumentational virtues, and bad arguing, including many classic fallacies, through the vices that typically produce it.[^s5]

## Origin

The approach grew out of the "aretaic turn" in ethics and the rise of virtue epistemology, which shifted attention from beliefs to the intellectual character of the knower.[^s1] In argumentation theory the main proposals came from Daniel Cohen, who stressed the social and ethical side of arguing and warned against treating arguments only as finished products, and from Andrew Aberdein.[^s3] Aberdein's 2010 paper "Virtue in argument" in *Argumentation* set out the programme explicitly, adapting Cohen's framework into a tentative catalogue of virtues and drawing on Richard Paul's critical-thinking virtues along the way.[^s1]

## How it works

Aberdein's catalogue groups argumentational virtues under four broad dispositions: willingness to engage in argument, willingness to listen to others, willingness to modify one's own position, and willingness to question the obvious. Each family includes more specific traits, such as intellectual courage, fair-mindedness, intellectual empathy, humility, candour and perseverance.[^s1] In the Aristotelian manner, each virtue sits between two vices. Willingness to listen, for example, lies between Cohen's "deaf dogmatist", who hears nothing, and the "concessionaire", who gives way to everything.[^s1]

Vices then explain fallacies. Because vices are dispositions of people and fallacies are types of argument, the two cannot simply be identified; instead a fallacy is understood as the kind of argument characteristically produced by someone acting on a particular vice, in the proponent, the respondent or both.[^s5] This also offers a test for the ad hominem: on Aberdein's account, criticising an arguer is legitimate when it exposes an argumentational vice, such as a concealed conflict of interest or deception, and illegitimate when it targets traits that have nothing to do with arguing.[^s1][^s5]

A workplace example shows the shift in focus. In a design review, an engineer's proposal rests on one optimistic benchmark. A colleague who replies "that's cherry-picking, so your design is wrong" has found a real weakness but has argued badly. A virtue analysis asks which disposition failed: here, eagerness to win the point and unfairness in judging someone else's case. The virtuous reply names the weak evidence, asks for a broader test, and stays open to the design being sound.[^s4]

## How to apply it

1. **Appraise the exchange as well as the argument.** Ask whether each party listened, engaged with the strongest version of the other side and was willing to revise.[^s1][^s3]
2. **Trace a suspected fallacy to a disposition.** Ask what vice would produce this move, and whether it is in the speaker or in an audience that failed to check it.[^s5]
3. **Use character evidence carefully.** Facts about an arguer can bear on whether to accept their premises on their say-so, but not on whether a deductive argument is valid.[^s2]
4. **Turn it on yourself.** Gascón argues that VAT's main value is practical and self-directed: a way to improve one's own habits of arguing.[^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

VAT is a normative proposal, and its scope is disputed. Bowell and Kingsbury argue that a good argument can come from a vicious arguer, for example someone who recites a valid argument without understanding it, so an agent-based account cannot replace the standard account of argument quality; character matters mainly for accepting premises on testimony.[^s2] Gascón accepts that cogency can be judged without looking at character and argues that VAT should focus on arguers' attitudes and conduct rather than on rating arguments.[^s3] Aberdein himself addresses worries about where virtues get their normative force, about cultural variation in what counts as a virtue, and about separating virtue from mere skill.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Counteracts the argument from fallacy.** Aberdein traces the fallacy fallacy to two argumentational vices, misplaced zeal and unfairness in assessing others' arguments, and argues that reflectively cultivating the opposing virtues, regulated by humility and practical wisdom, is the remedy.[^s4]
- **Influenced by virtue epistemology.** VAT transfers virtue epistemology's focus on the knower's character to the arguer.[^s1]
- **Introduced by Andrew Aberdein and Daniel Cohen.** Gascón identifies them as the authors of the theory's main proposals.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with pragma-dialectics.** Aberdein defends agent-based appraisal against the pragma-dialectical treatment of ad hominem as a breach of discussion rules.[^s1]
- **Related to ad hominem and steelmanning.** Its account of legitimate character criticism refines the ad hominem, and the virtues of listening and fairness underwrite engaging with an opponent's best case.[^s1][^s5]
