# Informal logic

Informal logic is the study of the arguments people actually make: in newspapers and meetings, courtrooms and clinics, classrooms and conversations. Its two core jobs are to pull ordinary arguments out of the context they come in and to supply tools for judging how strong they are, with the aim of improving what people believe and claim to know.[^s1] Ralph Johnson and J. Anthony Blair, who did most to establish the field, define it as the branch of logic that develops non-formal standards and procedures for analysing, interpreting, evaluating, criticising and constructing argumentation in natural language.[^s2] It is "informal" not because it lacks standards but because it does not rely on logical form and deductive validity as its main instruments.[^s2]

## Origin

The field grew out of a teaching problem. At the University of Windsor in Canada, Johnson found in the late 1960s that students taught symbolic logic could not use it on real arguments, and in 1970-71 he began an "Applied Logic" course using Howard Kahane's *Logic and Contemporary Rhetoric* (1971); Blair joined the next year.[^s3] Their handouts, built around Canadian media examples, became the textbook *Logical Self-Defense* (1977).[^s3] In 1978 they hosted the first international symposium on informal logic, whose proceedings (1980) listed open problems that shaped the next decade's research; the follow-up *Informal Logic Newsletter* became a refereed journal, *Informal Logic*, whose first issue appeared in 1984.[^s3] The term itself is older (Gilbert Ryle used it in 1954), and the field claims antecedents in Aristotle, the *Port Royal Logic*, Whately and Mill.[^s1] Its rise was fed by dissatisfaction with formal logic as a guide to everyday reasoning and by the critical thinking movement in education.[^s1]

## How it works

Informal logic gives a reader a sequence of moves. First, *standardize*: list the premises and conclusion, and supply any unstated premise or conclusion the speaker plausibly relies on, often with a diagram showing how the reasons fit together.[^s1] Second, *evaluate*. The most widely used test, from Johnson and Blair, asks whether the premises are acceptable, whether they are relevant to the conclusion, and whether together they are sufficient to support it.[^s1][^s4]

A workplace example: a manager argues, "Our competitor moved to a four-day week and their staff survey scores rose, so we should do the same." Standardized, the argument needs an unstated premise that what worked for the competitor will work here. The premises may be acceptable (the survey result is real), and they are relevant, but they fall short of sufficient: one survey at one firm, with no word on output or cost, cannot carry a company-wide policy change. The informal logician's verdict is not "invalid" but "insufficiently supported", which points to what evidence would close the gap.[^s1]

Beyond these tests the field builds tools for specific patterns. Fallacy theory catalogues recurring errors such as ad hominem and slippery slope, and argument schemes pair common presumptive patterns (appeal to expert opinion, argument from analogy) with critical questions; one standard catalogue lists 96 schemes.[^s1] Dialectical approaches add that a full argument should also answer the objections it faces, a layer Johnson calls the dialectical tier.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

As a normative discipline, informal logic is judged by the quality of its standards rather than by experiments, and those standards are contested. There is no single agreed system; the field is a set of competing approaches.[^s1] The acceptability-relevance-sufficiency criteria draw objections: Hansen argues they predate *Logical Self-Defense*, and Siegel argues that sufficiency already presupposes relevance.[^s4] Johnson's dialectical tier was challenged by Govier, Hitchcock, Tindale, Groarke and others.[^s4] Fallacy theory is often criticised as unsystematic, and standardized critical thinking tests struggle to measure open-ended argument skill.[^s1] The field's boundaries are also unsettled: Johnson and Blair reject the views that informal logic is merely the study of informal fallacies or formal logic with the symbols removed.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Part of logic.** Johnson and Blair place informal logic within logic, as the part concerned with the norms of argument rather than of implication.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with formal logic.** It studies natural-language argument without making form and validity its main tools.[^s2]
- **Introduced by Ralph H. Johnson and J. Anthony Blair.** They created the Windsor course, organised the 1978 symposium and founded the newsletter and journal.[^s3]
- **Introduced in *Logical Self-Defense*.** Blair credits the 1977 textbook with introducing the acceptability, relevance and sufficiency criteria; the attribution of the field to one book is medium-confidence.[^s4]
- **Influenced by Charles Hamblin.** Johnson and Blair tie the field's modern development to Hamblin's *Fallacies* (1970), alongside Kahane's textbook.[^s2]
- **Influenced by the critical thinking movement.** Educational reform and the critical thinking movement were key catalysts.[^s1]
- **Related.** The principle of charity and the Toulmin model are interpretive tools it uses; pragma-dialectics is a sister approach that treats fallacies as violations of discussion rules.[^s1]
