# Argumentation theory

Argumentation theory is the field that studies argumentation: the communicative activity of advancing reasons to resolve a difference of opinion. Its distinctive ambition is to join two jobs that are usually kept apart, describing how people actually argue and judging that practice against standards of reasonableness.[^s1] It is interdisciplinary by design, drawing on philosophy, logic, linguistics, communication studies, rhetoric, cognitive psychology, persuasion research and artificial intelligence.[^s1] A 2024 review describes it as a heterogeneous discipline with a minimal consensus: it studies how to identify, interpret, analyse, evaluate and present arguments, and it treats arguments pragmatically, as tools used for goals in particular contexts.[^s2]

## Origin

The field's roots are classical. Aristotle supplied three of its pillars at once: syllogistic for deductive inference, dialectic for reasoning from plausible premises, rhetoric with its appeals to character, emotion and reason, and the first systematic catalogue of fallacies.[^s2] Later contributors include the Port Royal logicians, Locke, Whately and Mill, who turned attention to practical and inductive reasoning.[^s2]

The modern field took shape in the second half of the twentieth century.[^s2] Two books from 1958 set it going: Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument*, with its layout of claim, data and warrant, and Chaim Perelman and Lucie Olbrechts-Tyteca's *New Rhetoric*, which grounded argument in its audience.[^s1] Charles Hamblin's *Fallacies* (1970) attacked the textbook "standard treatment" of fallacies and revived formal dialectic.[^s1] From the 1970s onward came informal logic (Johnson and Blair, later Walton), pragma-dialectics (van Eemeren and Grootendorst, 1984), French linguistic approaches, communication-studies work and, by the 2000s, computational models of argument.[^s1]

## How it works

Van Eemeren and colleagues divide the field's research programme into five components: a philosophical one that examines what reasonableness is, a theoretical one that builds models of it, an empirical one that describes real argumentative discourse, an analytical one that brings the normative and descriptive together, and a practical one aimed at improving how people analyse, evaluate and produce arguments.[^s1] Across approaches the working vocabulary is shared, even when definitions differ: standpoints, unexpressed premises, argument schemes, argumentation structures and fallacies.[^s1]

A useful way to see the main approaches is Wenzel's threefold distinction, which treats argument as a product (the logical-epistemic view: are the reasons good?), a process (the rhetorical view: does it work for this audience?) and a procedure (the dialectical view: was the disagreement handled by fair rules?).[^s2]

A workplace example shows the three lenses at work. A project lead tells a steering group, "We should switch to Vendor B, because their uptime last year was higher." As a product, the argument rests on an unstated premise that last year's uptime predicts next year's, and it needs more than one metric to be sufficient. As a procedure, the lead carries the burden of proof for proposing a change and owes a reply to the finance manager's objection about migration cost; ignoring it would be a dialectical failure even if the reasons were sound. As a process, the case may persuade engineers but not finance unless it speaks to cost. Argumentation theory gives the group terms to separate these questions rather than collapsing them into who argued most confidently.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

As a largely normative field, argumentation theory is judged by the quality of its standards, and those are contested; one review faults the usual overviews for stressing the field's diversity while offering little shared practical guidance.[^s2] The same review notes that the field's critique of formal logic can overreach, since formal logic was never designed as a theory of everyday argument.[^s2]

The field's empirical side has gained support from psychology. Mercier and Sperber (2011) argue that human reasoning evolved mainly to produce and evaluate arguments in social exchange; on their account people reason better when a problem is framed argumentatively, and confirmation bias is a feature of arguing for one's own side.[^s3] It is a theoretical proposal rather than settled fact; the journal printed it alongside open peer commentary running to nearly forty pages.[^s3]

A live normative debate concerns adversariality. Some critics, including feminist philosophers, argue that combative norms reward aggression and exclude people; defenders such as Govier hold that minimal opposition to a claim is built into argument. Dutilh Novaes proposes a middle view: adversariality should be proportional to how far the parties' interests really conflict.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Influenced by Aristotle.** His syllogistic, dialectic, rhetoric and fallacy theory are the field's main classical source.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Stephen Toulmin and by *The New Rhetoric*.** The two 1958 works are the starting points of the modern field.[^s1]
- **Influenced by Charles Hamblin.** His 1970 critique of the standard treatment of fallacies reshaped fallacy theory and dialectic.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with formal logic.** It studies real, defeasible, natural-language arguments in context instead of abstract logical form.[^s2]
- **Applied in artificial intelligence.** Computational models of argument are a recognised branch of the field.[^s1]
- **Related.** Informal logic and pragma-dialectics are approaches within it; the Toulmin model and argumentation schemes are among its analytic tools; the argumentative theory of reasoning offers a psychological rationale for it.[^s1][^s3]
