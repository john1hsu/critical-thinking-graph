# Dialogue types

Dialogue types are a set of idealised models of conversation, each with its own starting situation, goal and rules, that Douglas Walton and Erik Krabbe use as the background for evaluating arguments.[^s1][^s3] The core idea is that an argument is never assessed in a vacuum: the same remark can be a good move in one kind of exchange and a fallacy in another, so the first job of a critic is to ask what kind of conversation is going on.[^s1][^s2] The typology is the main tool in Walton's "new dialectic" for judging relevance and for explaining many informal fallacies as moves that pull a dialogue away from its goal.[^s1]

## Origin

Walton and Macagno trace the idea back to Aristotle's *Topics* and *Sophistical Refutations*, which already distinguished kinds of dialectical exchange. The modern version was introduced in Walton's work around 1989-1990, developed with the Groningen logician Erik Krabbe in *Commitment in Dialogue* (1995), and extended in Walton's *The New Dialectic* (1998).[^s1] SUNY Press describes *Commitment in Dialogue* as a classification of the major types of dialogue and an account of when an arguer is held to a commitment and when it may be retracted, linking the strict Lorenzen tradition of formal dialogue with the looser Hamblin-style dialogue games.[^s3] An earlier 1993 paper by Walton, based on the joint work with Krabbe, already argued that whether a move is fallacious depends on the type of dialogue and on what the respondent is committed to, not on what anyone privately believes.[^s2]

## How it works

The standard table, taken from Walton and Krabbe, sets out six types:[^s1]

- **Persuasion** starts from conflicting points of view; the goal is to resolve the conflict by convincing the other party.[^s1]
- **Negotiation** starts from a conflict of interests; the goal is a deal.[^s1]
- **Inquiry** starts from shared ignorance; the goal is to prove or disprove a claim and add to common knowledge.[^s1]
- **Deliberation** starts from a need to act; the goal is to decide what to do.[^s1]
- **Information-seeking** starts from one party's ignorance; the goal is to obtain or pass on information.[^s1]
- **Eristic (quarrel)** starts from antagonism; the parties mainly try to win, sometimes reaching a provisional accommodation.[^s1]

Mixed forms such as debates and committee meetings combine several of these.[^s1] Each type carries its own standards. In a negotiation a commitment is a concession rather than a claim to truth, while an inquiry is cumulative and generally does not let participants retract what was established, which is why circular reasoning damages it.[^s2] Walton notes that a quarrel tolerates irrelevant and circular argument, which other types do not.[^s2]

The typology matters most when a conversation **shifts** from one type to another. A shift is licit when it serves the original goal, as when stalled contract talks pause for an information-seeking exchange with an engineer, and illicit when it moves the dialogue away from its goal.[^s1] Many fallacies can be read as illicit shifts: an ad hominem attack turns a critical discussion into a quarrel, and a threat turns a persuasion dialogue into a negotiation.[^s1][^s2] Walton and Macagno assess relevance at two levels: globally, against the goal of the dialogue type, and locally, by asking whether a move answers the question the context calls for. An irrelevant move is one that does not fit the goal of the dialogue it claims to belong to.[^s1]

A workplace example shows the method. A project team meets to decide whether to delay a product launch by two weeks, which is a deliberation. One manager says the launch must not slip because "the last person who asked for a delay here didn't last long". Read against the deliberation's goal, the remark gives no reason about readiness, risk or cost; it changes the conversation into a negotiation backed by a veiled threat. Naming the shift lets the team set the remark aside and return to the question of whether the product is ready.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. Identify the dialogue the participants are supposed to be in: what was the starting situation, and what outcome would count as success?[^s1]
2. For each disputed move, ask whether it contributes to that goal, both globally and as an answer to the immediate question.[^s1]
3. If the move belongs to a different type, decide whether the shift was agreed and helps the original goal, or was imposed and replaces it.[^s1]
4. Check the respondent's commitments: many apparent fallacies, such as a loaded question, are fallacious only if the presupposition was never conceded.[^s2]

A common pitfall is to treat every shift as fallacious. Walton and Macagno stress that the same move can be legitimate or fallacious depending on the institutional or cultural common ground of the exchange.[^s1]

## Evidence

The typology is a normative model, not an empirical claim, but researchers have asked whether people orient to its categories. Cionea and Hample (2014) built self-report scales for the types and tested them with 189 US online workers and 286 undergraduates.[^s4] The scales were reliable; persuasion was the most preferred orientation and eristic the least, and eristic orientation was predicted by anti-social verbal aggressiveness.[^s4] The constructive types overlapped heavily, however, and the authors proposed a new "information giving" type, which suggests the boundaries between categories are less clean in practice than in theory.[^s4] Their measures captured general tendencies, not shifts within real conversations, so they do not test the theory's account of fallacies directly.[^s4]

## Connections

Dialogue types are a framework within **argumentation theory**, the field that studies them empirically and normatively,[^s4][^s1] and they extend **dialectic** as the regulated study of two-party exchange.[^s1] The typology **detects fallacies of relevance** by judging a move irrelevant when it does not fit the goal of its dialogue,[^s1] **detects ad hominem** as an illicit shift from critical discussion to quarrel,[^s2] and **detects ad baculum** when a threat turns persuasion into negotiation.[^s1] It was **introduced by Douglas Walton** and **by Erik Krabbe**,[^s1][^s3] chiefly **in *Commitment in Dialogue***,[^s1] and was **influenced by Aristotle's *Sophistical Refutations***.[^s1] It is the context on which **dialectical relevance** depends, and Walton treats persuasion dialogue as the critical discussion of **pragma-dialectics**.[^s2]
