# Legal reasoning

Legal reasoning is the reasoning used to find out what the law says on a question and to justify a decision that applies it to a dispute. Dickson distinguishes three things the phrase can mean: establishing the current content of the law, reasoning from that law to a court's decision, and deciding what to do all things considered; most philosophical work concerns the first two.[^s2] As a field of study it sits where philosophy of law meets argumentation theory, and it matters to critical thinking because it is the most institutionalised setting in which people must give public reasons, answer counter-arguments and live with the consequences of a ruling.[^s4]

## Origin

Reasoning from earlier decisions is as old as common-law courts, but the modern study of how it works took shape in the twentieth century. Edward Levi's *An Introduction to Legal Reasoning* (1948 article, 1949 book) argued that the basic pattern is reasoning by example, from case to case.[^s3] H.L.A. Hart's 1958 "no vehicles in the park" illustration, and Lon Fuller's reply about a war-memorial truck, set the terms of a long debate over whether rules have a clear core of application or always need their purpose read in.[^s5] From the 1970s, legal theorists such as Neil MacCormick, Ronald Dworkin and Joseph Raz developed accounts of interpretation and coherence,[^s2] and researchers in artificial intelligence and law, starting with systems such as McCarty's TAXMAN, built computational models of legal argument.[^s4]

## How it works

Three moves recur.

**Applying rules to facts.** A statute or settled rule supplies a major premise, the facts supply a minor premise, and a conclusion follows. Bench-Capon, Prakken and Sartor stress that this deductive picture captures only part of the work: lawyers must also choose an interpretation of the client's story and of the rule, and persuade an audience that theirs is the better one.[^s4] Hart's park rule shows why: an ordinary car falls in the clear core of "vehicle", while a bicycle or a child's toy car sits in a penumbra where the words alone do not settle the case.[^s5]

**Reasoning from precedent and analogy.** Under *stare decisis* a court must follow the binding part of an earlier decision, its *ratio decidendi*, or distinguish it by pointing to a material factual difference.[^s1] Levi described a three-step process: a similarity is seen between cases, the rule implicit in the first case is announced, and that rule is applied to the second; because each new judgment of similarity reshapes the rule, the rules change as they are used.[^s3] Lamond adds that, unlike binding precedent, an analogy to an earlier case has no binding force and varies in strength from close to remote.[^s1]

**Weighing defeasible arguments.** Legal conclusions are usually defeasible: a rule can be outweighed by an exception or an overriding principle, and general priority principles such as preferring the more specific or more recent rule settle only a minority of conflicts.[^s4] Courts therefore argue about the purposes and values behind rules, allocate a burden of proof, and rely on presumptions.[^s4]

A workplace example: a company policy says "no personal devices on the factory floor". Last year a manager ruled that a personal smartwatch breached it because it could distract operators. This week an employee wears a fitness band with no screen. The employee distinguishes the earlier ruling (no screen, so the distraction rationale does not apply); the supervisor argues by analogy (it is still a personal electronic device). Each side is reasoning about which facts are relevantly similar, which is exactly the step Levi called the key one.[^s3]

## How to apply it

- **Separate what the rule says from why it exists.** Ask whether a borderline case falls in the rule's core or penumbra and whether its purpose reaches the case.[^s5]
- **State the holding at the right level of generality.** Lamond notes that how broadly a ruling is framed is hard to fix and drives whether a later case falls under it.[^s1]
- **Test an analogy by its rationale.** On reason-based accounts, an analogy holds when the reason for the earlier outcome also applies to the new case.[^s1]
- **Lay out pro and con arguments explicitly.** Argument schemes with critical questions, and diagramming tools descended from Wigmore's evidence charts, make legal arguments easier to inspect.[^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

Legal reasoning is a normative and descriptive field rather than an empirical effect, and its central questions remain disputed. Theorists disagree over why precedent should bind at all; Lamond argues that consistency and predictability can justify it but that consistency alone cannot justify repeating mistakes.[^s1] Dworkin defends one right answer reached through constructive interpretation, while positivists such as Hart and Raz allow judicial discretion where the law runs out.[^s2] Schauer concludes that neither Hart nor Fuller won their debate: Hart's view fits formalist systems better and Fuller's fits more purpose-driven ones.[^s5] Computational models of legal argument are informative but, as their authors note, mostly research prototypes.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** legal reasoning is a specialised form of reasoning carried out under institutional rules of authority.[^s2]
- **Studied in** argumentation theory, which treats legal dispute as a model and test-bed for conflicting, defeasible arguments,[^s4] and in **philosophy of law**, where interpretation and coherence are core topics.[^s2]
- **Formalized by** abstract argumentation frameworks: Bench-Capon applied Dung's framework to case law to explain why jurisdictions reach different outcomes.[^s4]
- **Influenced by** Edward Levi, whose account of reasoning by example remains a standard starting point.[^s3]
- **Related:** the argument from analogy scheme and defeasible reasoning describe its core inference patterns; vagueness, bright-line rules and precising definitions address the penumbra problem Hart raised;[^s5] and burden of proof and presumption govern who must argue for what.[^s4]
