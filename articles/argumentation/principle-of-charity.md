# Principle of charity

The principle of charity is a norm of interpretation: when you reconstruct what someone said or argued, you credit them with the most reasonable claims and reasoning that their words and the context can plausibly support, and only then evaluate it.[^s2] In argument analysis it is usually stated as practical advice to read an argument in its strongest sense before criticising it.[^s1] It is not a rule of kindness or agreement; it constrains how you *understand* a view, and leaves you free to reject it afterwards.[^s4]

## Origin

The term was coined by the philosopher N. L. Wilson in 1959 and was then taken up and reshaped by Quine, Lewis, Dennett and, above all, Donald Davidson.[^s4] Quine used charity as a constraint on translating an unfamiliar language; Davidson made it central to "radical interpretation", arguing that we can only make sense of a speaker at all if we assume their beliefs are mostly true and their reasoning mostly coherent by our own lights.[^s4] On this view some charity is not optional but a precondition of understanding anyone.[^s1]

Argumentation theorists borrowed the idea from philosophy of language. Michael Scriven (1976) presented it as ethical and practical guidance for analysing everyday arguments, and Johnson, Trudy Govier and Douglas Walton later developed logical and dialectical versions within informal logic.[^s1][^s2] Macagno traces older ancestors in Augustine's charitable reading of scripture and in Aristotle's treatment of enthymemes, arguments with unstated premises.[^s2]

## How it works

Most everyday arguments leave premises unstated and use loose wording, so any reconstruction involves choices. Charity says to resolve genuine ambiguity in favour of the more plausible reading: supply the unstated premise the speaker most likely relied on, read quantifiers and qualifications as they were probably meant, and do not pin an extreme claim on someone who made a modest one.[^s2] Mayes treats skills such as identifying a rationale, separating arguments from explanations and stating someone's principle accurately as all serving charitable reconstruction.[^s5]

**Workplace example.** In a planning meeting a colleague says, "We shouldn't launch next month; the onboarding flow isn't ready." An uncharitable listener replies, "So you want to delay until the product is perfect?" A charitable listener first reconstructs the argument: *the onboarding flow has known defects; launching with them would hurt first-week retention; so launch should wait until those defects are fixed.* That version names a specific, checkable premise (how bad the defects are and what they cost), which is where the real disagreement lies. The charitable reading makes the criticism sharper, not softer. The uncharitable reply is the kind of distortion Mayes notes listeners fall into when they are impatient or irritated.[^s5]

## How to apply it

1. Restate the view in terms its holder would accept before responding; if you cannot, you have not understood it yet.[^s5]
2. Where premises are implicit, add the most plausible one the context supports, not the weakest or the most damning.[^s2]
3. Keep qualifications ("some", "usually", "on Fridays") exactly as stated.[^s2]
4. When you suspect you are attacking a weak version, name both the original view and your version and check whether yours is really what was meant.[^s5]

**Pitfalls.** Theorists broadly agree that some basic charity is unavoidable but that maximal charity is unsustainable, and most recommend a moderate version tied to textual evidence.[^s1] Too much charity lets weak reasoning off the hook or builds an "iron man": a stronger argument than the speaker actually gave, which can be its own distortion.[^s2] Stevens argues charity turns "toxic" when the interpreter's own standards of strength replace the arguer's, and recommends a "complex" charity that weighs both perspectives.[^s3] Lewiński's "paradox of charity" adds that in a dispute, being charitable to one side can mean being uncharitable to the other; he proposes applying charity only under real interpretive doubt and favouring the critic in critical settings such as reviews and trials.[^s1] Charitable reconstruction may also be inappropriate where a speaker is strategic or uncooperative, as in cross-examination.[^s2]

## Connections

Charity is the standard remedy for the **straw man fallacy**: Scriven's "negative charity" was framed as a warning against unfair criticism such as attacking straw men, and Mayes defines the straw man as a violation of the principle.[^s2][^s5] Charity is **applied in informal logic**, where it guides the identification and reconstruction of arguments.[^s1] The term was **introduced by N. L. Wilson** and the norm was **influenced by Donald Davidson**'s theory of radical interpretation.[^s4] Its argument-analysis form is credited to **Michael Scriven**.[^s2] It is closely related to **steelmanning**, which deliberately seeks the strongest version of a view, and to the analysis of **enthymemes**, whose missing premises charity helps fill.[^s2]
