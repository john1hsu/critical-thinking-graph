# Stasis theory

Stasis theory is a framework from classical rhetoric for finding the precise point on which a dispute turns. The Greek word *stasis* borrows a medical image of a blocked flow: discussion halts where one party's claim meets the other's denial or doubt, and that point of contradiction becomes the question to be judged.[^s3] Everything the parties agree on is treated as settled, so the framework directs effort to the place where they actually disagree.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

The theory is credited to Hermagoras of Temnos, a Greek rhetorician of the second century BC. Ray Nadeau's 1959 survey describes his classification of main and subordinate issues as decisive in shaping how later ancient writers treated stasis.[^s2] Hermagoras's own writings are lost, so his system is reconstructed from later authors. Malcolm Heath argues that Cicero's early *De Inventione* follows Hermagoras's original model closely, and he prefers Quintilian's report of Hermagoras over a later account attributed to pseudo-Augustine.[^s1] Cicero and Quintilian refined the doctrine in Latin, and Greek theorists through Hermogenes in the second century AD kept reworking it.[^s1][^s4] Heath reconstructs Hermagoras's central idea as follows: the stasis is the defence's first denial, which meets the charge and so creates the dispute.[^s1]

## How it works

The classical system sorts disputes by the structure of the disagreement. In *conjecture* the question is one of fact: did it happen? In *definition* the facts are agreed but their classification is disputed: what should the act be called? In *quality* the act and its name are conceded, but it is defended as justified or excusable.[^s1] Modern teaching versions usually add *policy*: what should be done?[^s4][^s5] Heath stresses why the classification matters. The arguments that suit a question of fact can be irrelevant to a question of evaluation, so a speaker who misreads the stasis argues beside the point.[^s1] Plantin's moped example shows how the choice of reply fixes the debate. An accused rider can deny the act, admit it but reclassify it, plead mitigating circumstances, challenge the court, or even admit it and claim credit, and each move opens a different question.[^s3]

A workplace example: after a service outage, one manager says the vendor broke its contract, and another says the company should stay with the vendor. The first is arguing a definition (does a four-hour slowdown count as an "outage" under the contract?). The second is arguing policy. If the first manager's evidence about the contract wording is offered as a reason to switch vendors, it misses the point unless the policy question has been reached. Working through the stases in order shows that both agree on the facts in the logs, so the meeting can settle the definition before moving to the decision. Edlund describes this kind of talking past each other, where the parties never identify where they actually disagree, as the main failure the stases are meant to prevent.[^s4]

## How to apply it

1. **State each side's claim and find the first denial.** The issue lies where one party's claim is contested, not where both already agree.[^s3]
2. **Walk the questions in order:** fact, definition, quality, then policy. Record which ones are agreed and stop at the first real disagreement.[^s4][^s5]
3. **Decide whether the question is definite or indefinite.** A definite question concerns particular people, places and times. An indefinite one is the general principle behind it. Choose deliberately which one to argue.[^s4]
4. **For future-oriented decisions, use deliberative questions.** Edlund's chapter lists questions on whether an action is legal, expedient and possible, and what effects it would have.[^s4]
5. **Use agreed stases as common ground.** The Purdue OWL describes a student team that agreed on fact, quality and policy despite a lasting disagreement over definition.[^s5]

## Limitations

The framework is a normative and pedagogical tool, not an empirically tested intervention. Its categories were built for courtroom pleading, and their number and subdivisions varied widely among ancient authors.[^s1][^s3] Heath notes that Hermogenes told readers to identify the stasis by inspecting the point the judge must decide: unclear means conjecture, clear but incomplete means definition, complete means quality. Heath also observes that Hermogenes never explains how to identify that point in the first place.[^s1] Applying the scheme to everyday arguments therefore takes judgment. The Purdue OWL also warns that some disagreements will not end amicably even when the stases are mapped.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Hermagoras of Temnos.** Nadeau credits Hermagoras's classification of main and subordinate issues with shaping the whole later tradition.[^s2]
- **Counteracts ignoratio elenchi.** By fixing which kind of question is at issue, stasis analysis exposes arguments built for one kind of question but aimed at another. Heath notes that such arguments may be irrelevant to the actual dispute.[^s1]
- **Studied in argumentation theory.** Plantin's *Dictionnaire de l'argumentation* treats stasis as the organising issue of an argumentative exchange.[^s3]
- **Applied in group decision making.** The Purdue OWL presents the stases as a way for teams to build common ground and keep discussion moving toward agreed action.[^s5]
- **Related to the straw man.** Edlund observes that opposing sides often argue different questions and so never engage. Settling the stasis first leaves less room to attack a position nobody holds.[^s4]
