# Pragma-dialectics

Pragma-dialectics is a theory of argumentation that treats arguing as something two parties do together: one defends a standpoint, the other tests it, and the exchange succeeds when their difference of opinion is settled on the merits rather than by pressure, evasion or luck. The name joins "pragma", from speech act theory, with "dialectics", the study of regulated two-party discussion.[^s1] Its practical payoff is a short code of conduct, the rules for a critical discussion, against which any argumentative move can be checked. A move that breaks a rule is a fallacy, because it obstructs the resolution of the disagreement.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The approach was developed by Frans H. van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst at the University of Amsterdam.[^s1] It combines pragmatics (the speech act work of Austin, Searle and Grice) with formal dialectics and with the critical rationalism of Karl Popper and Hans Albert, which assumes that all human thought is fallible and must be tested against criticism.[^s5] Their mature statement, *A Systematic Theory of Argumentation* (Cambridge University Press, 2003/2004), sets out four meta-theoretical principles: every language act has a function, it creates public commitments, argument is interactive, and differences are to be resolved by norms of reasonableness.[^s5][^s4] The authors argue that the rules should be judged first by whether they actually help settle disputes, and second by whether ordinary arguers accept them, their "conventional validity".[^s4]

## How it works

An ideal critical discussion passes through four stages: confrontation (the disagreement surfaces), opening (the parties agree on roles and shared starting points), argumentation (the protagonist defends the standpoint and the antagonist probes it) and concluding (they decide whether the standpoint survived).[^s1] The same model is used for one-sided texts, where a writer anticipates an imagined critic.[^s1]

Van Eemeren and Garssen give the rules in ten short commandments. In paraphrase: do not stop the other side from advancing or questioning standpoints (freedom); defend your standpoint if asked (burden of proof); attack only standpoints the other side actually holds (standpoint); defend with relevant argument only (relevance); do not misattribute or disown unstated premises; do not misrepresent what is common ground (starting point); reasoning presented as conclusive must be valid; use fitting argument schemes correctly; accept the outcome of the defence (concluding); and avoid unclear or deliberately misread wording (usage).[^s3]

A workplace example shows the rules at work. A team lead proposes that all project hours be logged by Friday; a colleague replies that the lead logs her own hours late, so the rule can be ignored. In pragma-dialectical terms the reply is a tu quoque, which belongs with the abusive and circumstantial ad hominem as a violation of the first rule. It does not show the proposal is wrong. It tries to disqualify the lead from taking part in the discussion at all.[^s1] Recasting the fallacy this way lets an analyst draw a clear line between fallacious personal attacks and relevant ones.[^s1] The right response is to restore the discussion: concede the lead's lapse if it is fair, then return to whether Friday logging keeps invoices accurate.

## How to apply it

Van Eemeren and Garssen describe the analysis as a sequence: identify the standpoints and who plays protagonist and antagonist; locate the four stages in the actual exchange; map the argument structure (single, multiple, coordinative or subordinative); reconstruct unexpressed premises, starting from the logical minimum that would make the step valid; identify the argument schemes and ask their critical questions; and then check each move against the ten rules.[^s3] They stress that the rules are heuristic rather than algorithmic: following them lowers the risk of fallacious arguing and supports evaluation, but cannot guarantee that a dispute will be resolved.[^s3]

## Evidence

Because the theory claims its rules are acceptable to reasonable people, its authors tested that claim. In a series of experiments reported in 2002, Dutch secondary-school students and Spanish respondents rated discussion fragments; fallacious personal attacks were judged clearly less reasonable than legitimate moves (mean 3.75 versus 5.29 on a 7-point scale in the original study).[^s2] Direct abusive attacks were rated least reasonable and tu quoque the least objectionable, though still significantly worse than non-fallacious moves, with a small effect; in scientific discussions tu quoque was consistently judged unreasonable.[^s2] Most written justifications cited argument quality rather than politeness in the Netherlands, while politeness mattered more in Spain.[^s2] The follow-up samples were small (24 to 75 respondents each), and Hitchcock reads the results as support for the standard textbook taxonomy of ad hominem.[^s1][^s2]

The approach also has principled critics. Epistemologists John Biro and Harvey Siegel object that the theory is not genuinely normative and that it rejects the justification of beliefs too quickly. Garssen and van Laar (2010) reply that the goal of resolving differences of opinion can ground real norms, and that the theory defines argument without appeal to justification.[^s6] Hitchcock adds that the later addition of "strategic maneuvering", which lets arguers pursue rhetorical effectiveness within the rules, stays subordinate to critical rationalism and may not satisfy persuasion-oriented theorists.[^s1]

## Connections

Pragma-dialectics is one of the main schools within **argumentation theory**, alongside approaches such as informal logic.[^s1] It **detects tu quoque** and the wider **ad hominem** family by classing them as violations of the freedom rule, and it **detects the straw man fallacy** through the standpoint rule against attacking positions the opponent never held.[^s1][^s3] It was **introduced by Frans van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst**,[^s1] and it was **influenced by critical rationalism**, from which it takes the idea that standpoints earn acceptance only by surviving criticism.[^s5] Its rules are **supported by** the experiments of van Eemeren, Garssen and Meuffels on ordinary arguers' judgments,[^s2] and **challenged by** the epistemic approach to argumentation, which ties good argument to justified belief rather than to resolved disagreement.[^s6] Its argument-scheme rule draws on **argumentation schemes** and their critical questions, and its interpretive care overlaps with the **principle of charity**.[^s3]
