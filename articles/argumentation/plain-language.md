# Plain language

Plain language is a drafting discipline whose goal is that the intended reader can find what they need, understand it, and act on it the first time they read it. The U.S. Federal Plain Language Guidelines turn that goal into concrete rules: express one idea per sentence, keep subjects and objects close to their verbs, put modifiers such as "only" next to the words they modify, use "must" for obligations instead of the imprecise "shall", replace "and/or" with an explicit choice, and break multi-condition sentences into lists.[^s1] For critical thinking its main value is preventive: many of these rules close off the structural ambiguities on which the fallacy of amphiboly depends, before anyone builds an argument on them.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The modern movement grew out of government and legal reform rather than logic. U.S. federal efforts date from the 1970s; in the mid-1990s federal employees formed the Plain English Network, executive orders in 1993 and 1996 reinforced plain drafting of regulations, and a 1998 presidential memorandum extended it to government writing generally.[^s6] In 2000 the network became the Plain Language Action and Information Network (PLAIN), which wrote the Federal Plain Language Guidelines; the Plain Writing Act, signed on 13 October 2010, requires agencies to use plain language in documents about benefits, services and compliance, to name a senior official for plain writing, and to train staff.[^s6] The guidelines themselves say they were first developed in the mid-1990s and are revised every few years.[^s1] In law, Joseph Kimble has argued for decades that plain drafting outperforms traditional legalese.[^s4]

## How it works

Most amphibolies come from grammar that lets a phrase attach in two places: a trailing modifier after a list, a squinting modifier between two possible targets, or "and/or" joining conditions.[^s2][^s5] Kimble's example "men and women who are tall" shows that no grammatical rule settles whether the modifier covers both nouns; only context or redrafting does.[^s5] Courts fall back on interpretive canons such as the rule of the last antecedent, which attaches a trailing modifier to the nearest noun, but Kimble's survey of 39 recent cases found it was rarely the sole reason for a decision, and some judges have openly said they could not tell which competing canon should win.[^s5] Plain drafting removes the need for the canon by making the attachment explicit.[^s1][^s5]

A workplace example: a travel policy reads "Employees must submit receipts for meals and taxi fares over $25." A plain-language rewrite splits the rule into a list: "Submit a receipt for (a) every meal, of any amount; (b) each taxi fare over $25." The rewrite forces the author to decide which reading was meant, and once it is written no employee can argue from the other parse. Wilson's textbook describes the same repair for arguments generally: rewrite the sentence, add punctuation or reorder phrases so that one grammatical structure is clear, and where context cannot settle the meaning, state every plausible reading.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Draft for one reading.** One idea per sentence; modifiers next to what they modify; repeat the qualifier rather than letting it trail after a list; use "either X, or Y, or both" instead of "and/or".[^s1]
2. **Use a list** whenever a sentence carries several conditions or exceptions, numbering items that will be referred to later.[^s1]
3. **Test with readers.** In paraphrase testing, six to nine readers each stop at set points and explain the passage in their own words; wherever someone restates it differently from the writer's intent, the text has a problem to fix.[^s1] This is a practical detector of amphiboly: two readers who paraphrase the same sentence two ways have found a second parse.
4. **When analysing someone else's text,** rewrite the key premise in plain form both ways and check whether the argument survives on the reading the author actually meant.[^s2]

Pitfalls: shorter is not automatically unambiguous, and a sentence that is easy to read can still be read two ways, which is why the guidelines pair drafting rules with reader testing.[^s1]

## Evidence

Comparative studies mostly favour plain versions. Kimble's 2016 summary reports that in a 2005 test of California court forms, readers answered 81% of questions correctly on a plain proof-of-service form versus 61% on the original, and 95% versus 65% on a subpoena; in the early 1980s, rewritten FCC marine-radio rules raised correct answers from about 10.7 to 16.9 out of 20 while nearly halving response time.[^s4] These are advocacy summaries of mostly small studies.[^s4] A peer-reviewed experiment by Masson and Waldron (1994) is more sobering: redrafting standard contracts with simpler words and sentence structure reliably improved non-experts' comprehension, yet absolute comprehension stayed very low, because readers also misunderstood legal concepts that clashed with their prior beliefs.[^s3] Plain language therefore helps with structural and lexical ambiguity but cannot by itself remove conceptual difficulty, hence the `mixed` evidence status.

## Connections

- **Counteracts** amphiboly: rewriting a sentence so that only one grammatical structure is possible removes the slide between readings the fallacy needs.[^s2]
- **Detects** amphiboly: paraphrase testing surfaces sentences that readers restate in different ways.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Masson and Waldron's 1994 contract-redrafting study, with the limits noted above.[^s3]
- **Applied in** legal reasoning and drafting, where unclear modifiers otherwise go to courts and their canons.[^s5]
- **Contrasts with** the last-antecedent rule, an after-the-fact interpretive canon that guesses at a modifier's reach rather than preventing the ambiguity.[^s5]
- **Related** to precising definitions, which fix the meaning of single words (the remedy for equivocation) as plain drafting fixes sentence structure, and to the principle of charity, which governs which reading to choose once ambiguity is already present.[^s2]
