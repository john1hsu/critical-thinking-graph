# Counterargument

A counterargument (also called an objection) is an argument or consideration put forward against a claim, or against the argument offered for it.[^s4] It is the other half of the basic unit of critical thinking: an argument gives reasons for a conclusion, and a counterargument gives reasons to doubt the conclusion or to doubt that those reasons support it.[^s1][^s4] Because most everyday reasoning is defeasible, meaning its conclusions can be overturned by further information, the question "what could be said against this?" is never idle.[^s1]

## Origin

Answering the other side is as old as dialectic, but modern theory gives it two sharper forms. In epistemology and artificial intelligence, John Pollock's work from the 1970s and 1980s, especially his 1987 account of defeasible reasoning, classified the ways a reason can be defeated, and the Stanford Encyclopedia entry by Robert Koons notes that Stephen Toulmin had earlier separated an argument's warrant from its rebuttal conditions.[^s1] In argumentation theory, the pragma-dialectical school of Amsterdam treats argument as a critical discussion between a protagonist and an antagonist, whose possible moves are doubt, a counter-standpoint, or a counterargument.[^s4] In psychology, Deanna Kuhn's 1991 study *The Skills of Argument* made the ability to generate counterarguments one of the core skills by which she assessed ordinary people's reasoning.[^s3]

## How it works

A counterargument can aim at three targets.[^s1][^s4]

1. **The conclusion.** It offers independent reasons to think the conclusion is false. Pollock calls this a *rebutting* defeater: a reason to believe the denial of what was concluded.[^s1]
2. **The inference.** It grants the premises but gives a reason to think they do not support the conclusion in this case. Pollock calls this an *undercutting* defeater; his example is learning that the lighting is unusual, which leaves "it looks red" true but stops it indicating that the object is red.[^s1]
3. **A premise.** It disputes one of the reasons themselves, which, if successful, removes part of the support.[^s4]

A workplace example: a manager argues that the team should move its weekly meeting online because attendance in the office has dropped. One colleague rebuts the conclusion: in-person meetings resolve blockers faster, so the cost of moving outweighs the benefit. Another undercuts the inference: attendance dropped in a month when two people were on leave, so the drop says little about the format. A third attacks a premise: the badge data shows attendance has not dropped at all. Each objection demands a different reply, which is why naming its target is the first step in answering it.[^s1][^s4]

## How to apply it

- **Generate the other side on purpose.** Ask what someone who disagreed would say, and look for evidence that would count against your view, not merely a rival story. Kuhn's interviews found that some people produce real counterarguments when asked, while others fail, decline, or offer only an alternative theory with no evidence against their own.[^s3]
- **Locate the target.** Decide whether the objection attacks the conclusion, the step, or a premise, since only then do you know what a reply must show.[^s1]
- **Answer, don't just mention.** Amjarso argues that raising an anticipated objection commits you to dealing with it; mentioning it and moving on leaves the defence incomplete.[^s4] Concede what is right, then rebut or limit what is not.[^s2][^s4]
- **Watch for selective anticipation.** Because the arguer chooses which objections to raise, pre-empting only the weakest ones can create an appearance of fair play while steering the discussion.[^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

People often leave counterarguments out. In Wolfe and Britt's 2008 experiments with university students, about 40% of essays written after researching a set of pro and con sources contained no other-side information at all, and the rate did not depend on which side the writer was assigned, which suggests the gap lies in beliefs about what makes a good argument, not personal opinion.[^s2] Instructions to research both sides reduced one-sided essays from about half to under a third, while a text urging writers simply to "know the opposition" had no significant effect.[^s2] A text challenging the belief that good arguments are just facts helped high-ability writers include more other-side material but backfired for low-ability writers, partly because they stopped pairing concessions with rebuttals.[^s2] Persuasion research points the same way: Amjarso cites O'Keefe's 1999 meta-analysis of more than 40 experiments as finding that messages which raise and refute the other side outperform one-sided ones, which in turn outperform messages that raise opposing views without answering them.[^s4] These are findings about student writers and audiences in experiments; they show what helps, not that every objection deserves equal space.

## Connections

- **Broader: argument.** A counterargument is itself an argument, directed at another claim or argument.[^s1][^s4]
- **Studied in argumentation theory.** Pragma-dialectics models objections as the antagonist's moves in a critical discussion and analyses how arguers anticipate them.[^s4]
- **Studied in the psychology of reasoning.** Wolfe and Britt's experiments, published in *Thinking & Reasoning*, measure when writers include or omit the other side.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with myside bias.** Wolfe and Britt define myside bias in writing as the failure to include any other-side argument, the very omission that engaging counterarguments repairs.[^s2]
- **Applied in argumentative writing.** Concession followed by rebuttal is the move the same studies track in student essays.[^s2]
- **Related: defeasible reasoning, Toulmin model, method of counterexample.** Rebutting and undercutting defeaters come from the theory of defeasible reasoning, and Toulmin's rebuttal conditions are an earlier version of the same idea.[^s1]
