# Ambiguity

An expression is ambiguous when it has more than one legitimate meaning: "bat" can name a flying mammal or a piece of sports equipment, and "bank" a river's edge or a financial institution.[^s1] In the philosophical sense, ambiguity belongs to the words or sentences themselves, not to a confused speaker or a murky situation.[^s1] For critical thinking it matters because arguments are made of language. If a key word means one thing in a premise and another in the conclusion, an argument can look valid while connecting nothing, which is the fallacy of equivocation.[^s4]

## Origin

Aristotle put ambiguity at the start of the study of fallacies. In *On Sophistical Refutations* he observes that we argue with names as stand-ins for things, and since names are finite while things are unlimited, one word or phrase must cover several meanings; people who miss the shift are misled, whether reasoning alone or listening to others.[^s2] He then lists six ways a refutation can only appear to succeed through language, and says these depend on language and are six in number.[^s2] Plantin's dictionary of argumentation names the six as homonymy (equivocation), amphiboly, composition, division, accent and form of expression, and files homonymy, amphiboly and accent under ambiguity.[^s6]

## How it works

Sennet's Stanford Encyclopedia entry distinguishes three main kinds.[^s1]

- **Lexical ambiguity**: one word form carries unrelated meanings (homonymy, as with "bank"), or related senses blur into one another (polysemy).[^s1]
- **Structural (syntactic) ambiguity**: the words are clear but the grammar allows two parses, as in "superfluous hair remover", or scope readings such as "every woman squeezed a man" (one man, or a man each?).[^s1]
- **Pragmatic ambiguity**: the same sentence can perform different speech acts or be used to refer in different ways.[^s1]

Textbooks treat the structural kind in arguments as amphiboly: a used-car advert or a stray pronoun ("they" meaning the dogs or the pictures) can be read two ways because of how the sentence is built.[^s5] The classic equivocation runs: a feather is light; whatever is light cannot be dark; so a feather cannot be dark.[^s5]

A workplace case shows the cost. A project plan states that "all critical defects must be fixed before release", and a manager argues that a cosmetic login-page glitch is critical because the login page is critical to the product, so the release must wait. "Critical" has slid from "severity rating" to "important part of the system". Dowden compares such meaning shifts to a bait-and-switch and calls equivocation the illegitimate switching of a term's meaning during reasoning.[^s4]

## How to spot and handle it

Ambiguity differs from three look-alikes. A vague word such as "bald" has borderline cases but one meaning; a context-sensitive word such as "I" changes reference without being ambiguous; and a general word such as "aunt" (mother's or father's sister) is merely unspecific.[^s1] Philosophers use rough tests: if "that bank isn't a bank" can be true, the word has two meanings, whereas "she is an aunt but isn't an aunt" is simply contradictory; odd-sounding coordinations (zeugma) also signal ambiguity.[^s1] Sennet cautions that these tests are fragile, since subtle related senses, metaphor and intonation can mislead them.[^s1]

In practice: underline the load-bearing terms in an argument, paraphrase each premise with a precise substitute, and check that the substitute still fits the conclusion. If it does not, the argument equivocates.[^s4] When the meaning is unclear but the speaker's intent is not, prefer the reading that makes the argument strongest, as the principle of charity advises.

## Evidence and limitations

Ambiguity is not simply a defect of language. Piantadosi, Tily and Gibson (2012) argued that when context already supplies meaning, an efficient code should reuse short, easy forms; in English, German and Dutch corpora, shorter and more frequent words had more meanings and homophones, although the German syllable result ran against prediction and the languages studied are related.[^s3] The lesson for reasoners is that ambiguity is normal and usually harmless in conversation, where context resolves it, but becomes dangerous in arguments, contracts and specifications, where a reader can exploit the unresolved reading. Sennet also warns of the opposite error: claiming that a word is ambiguous just to escape a counterexample, against Grice's maxim that senses should not be multiplied beyond necessity.[^s1]
