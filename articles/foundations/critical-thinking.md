# Critical thinking

Critical thinking is careful, goal-directed thinking about what to believe or what to do. Philosophers have proposed many competing definitions, but David Hitchcock's survey treats them as rival conceptions of one concept: they differ in whether they cover building arguments or only appraising them, whether the goal is belief alone or also action, and which norms make the thinking "careful".[^s1] The most cited short formulation is Robert Ennis's: reasonable, reflective thinking focused on deciding what to believe or do.[^s2] On almost every account it has two ingredients: *abilities* (the skills of clarifying, inferring and judging) and *dispositions* (the habits that make a person actually use those skills).[^s1]

## Origin

John Dewey put the idea on the educational agenda in *How We Think* (1910), under the name "reflective thinking", and analysed it into phases running from a felt problem through hypothesis and reasoning to testing.[^s1] Progressive schools in the 1930s adopted it as a goal, and Edward Glaser (1941) showed experimentally that high-school students' critical thinking could be improved.[^s1] Ennis's 1962 analysis of its aspects launched research and testing, and in 1990 Peter Facione led a Delphi study for the American Philosophical Association in which 46 experts agreed a consensus statement.[^s1][^s3] That report described critical thinking as purposeful, self-regulating judgment and listed six core skills: interpretation, analysis, evaluation, inference, explanation and self-regulation.[^s3] Harvey Siegel defended it as an educational ideal, and Richard Paul added a "strong sense" version that requires engaging opposing views on their best terms.[^s1]

## How it works

Ennis organises the abilities into clusters: basic clarification (focusing on the question, analysing arguments, asking clarifying questions), judging the bases of a decision (the credibility of sources and observation reports), inference (deduction, generalisation, explanatory inference, value judgments), advanced clarification (defining terms, finding unstated assumptions) and supposition and integration (reasoning from premises one rejects).[^s2] His dispositions centre on caring that beliefs are true and decisions justified, and caring to present positions honestly and clearly.[^s2]

A workplace example: a manager is told that a new onboarding programme "cut staff turnover by a third". Thinking critically, she first clarifies the claim (turnover measured how, over what period?), then asks about the source (the vendor that sold the programme), looks for an unstated assumption (that nothing else changed, such as a pay rise in the same quarter), and considers an alternative explanation before deciding whether to renew. The abilities let her ask those questions; the disposition is what makes her ask them about a result she would like to be true.[^s2]

The two ingredients come apart. Hitchcock notes that someone who habitually jumps to conclusions, or who holds views dogmatically, will not think critically even if they have the skills.[^s1] Facione's panel likewise described the ideal critical thinker by traits such as inquisitiveness, fair-mindedness, honesty about one's own biases and willingness to reconsider.[^s3]

## How to apply it

A practical checklist drawn from these accounts: state the question precisely; identify the reasons and conclusion being offered; judge the credibility of each source; surface hidden assumptions; consider alternative explanations or options; draw a conclusion no stronger than the evidence; and review the reasoning before acting.[^s2][^s3] Hitchcock argues these activities need not follow Dewey's fixed sequence; in practice they repeat and occur in different orders.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

Whether critical thinking is one general skill is disputed. John McPeck (1981) argued that there are no general thinking skills, only subject-specific ones, so the subject should be taught within disciplines; critics reply that some reasoning abilities clearly do generalise.[^s1] Cognitive scientist Daniel Willingham (2020) argues that critical thinking depends heavily on domain knowledge and that transfer of trained skills to new contexts is weak, so instruction should be planned subject by subject over several years.[^s4]

The teaching evidence is real but modest. Abrami and colleagues' 2015 meta-analysis (341 high-quality studies) found a small average gain in generic critical thinking (g ≈ 0.30), with larger gains when explicit instruction was combined with dialogue, applied problems and mentoring.[^s6] Huber and Kuncel's 2015 meta-analysis found that skills and dispositions improve substantially over a normal college experience, but that curriculum-wide programmes do not necessarily add long-term gains.[^s5] Hitchcock also records criticisms that the standard conception favours detached, solitary reason over emotion and collaboration, while noting studies that found no sex differences in performance.[^s1]

## Connections

Critical thinking is a species of reasoning that presupposes the notion of an argument.[^s1] Dewey is credited with introducing it as an educational aim, under another name.[^s1] It is applied and studied in critical thinking education, whose effectiveness Abrami et al.'s meta-analysis supports.[^s1][^s6] Cognitive psychology studies its dependence on knowledge and its limited transfer.[^s4] McPeck's subject-specificity thesis challenges the idea of critical thinking as a general skill.[^s1] The Delphi report is the best-known consensus statement of it, and its dispositional side overlaps with actively open-minded thinking and metacognition.[^s3]
