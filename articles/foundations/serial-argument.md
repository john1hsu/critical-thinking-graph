# Serial argument

A serial argument is a chain: premises support an intermediate conclusion, and that conclusion then serves as a premise for the next step, until the reasoning reaches its final claim.[^s4] Douglas Walton's definition, as reported by G. C. Goddu, is that the conclusion of a first argument also functions as a premise in a second.[^s2] Each link in the chain can have its own internal structure, simple, linked or convergent, and can rest on any argument scheme.[^s4] Pragma-dialectics calls the same structure subordinate (or subordinative) argumentation, because a reason that is itself open to doubt is backed by a further reason beneath it.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The term comes from Monroe Beardsley's 1950 textbook *Practical Logic*, which also gave informal logic the labels "convergent" and "divergent" argument.[^s1] Christian Plantin's dictionary of argumentation traces "serial argumentation" to Beardsley as well and links it to older logical forms: the polysyllogism, a chain of syllogisms in which each conclusion feeds the next, and the related forms known as the sorites and the epicheirema.[^s4] Frans van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst's pragma-dialectical theory supplied the alternative name "subordinate argumentation" in 1992.[^s4] Walton later listed serial structure alongside linked, convergent and divergent structure as the four basic ways sub-arguments combine.[^s2]

## How it works

Take a workplace example. An operations lead argues: the vendor's last three deliveries arrived a week late, so the vendor cannot currently meet our schedule; and since the product launch depends on on-time parts, we should line up a second supplier. The late deliveries support an intermediate claim (the vendor cannot meet the schedule), and that claim, together with the launch dependency, supports the recommendation. The intermediate claim does double duty: it is the conclusion of one step and a premise of the next.[^s2]

Plantin shows that the same words can sometimes be read either way. In a textbook example he cites, the remark that a colleague is a Taurus and stubborn and so will negotiate badly can be reconstructed as a chain (Taurus, therefore stubborn, therefore a poor negotiator) or as two independent reasons converging on one conclusion. Which reading is right depends on how the speaker understands the terms.[^s4]

## How to spot it

Francisca Snoeck Henkemans studied the wording that signals complex argumentation. Ordinary indicators such as "because", "since" and "therefore" mark support in general; subordination is more clearly signalled when they are combined so that one reason is itself justified, as in "the trip is expensive, because there are no charter flights, and therefore we should not go".[^s3] She also warns that indicators give no certainty: some words are ambiguous, and the content of the standpoint can override them, so the analyst should ask how the arguer would answer criticism of each reason.[^s3]

A practical test: if a statement is offered as a reason for the main claim and is also given its own support, mark it as an intermediate conclusion and draw the chain. Goddu notes that, in a diagram, serial structure is simply where one statement appears as the conclusion of one sub-argument and as a premise of another.[^s2]

## How to evaluate it

Evaluate from the bottom up. Check whether the basic premises support the intermediate conclusion, then whether the intermediate conclusion, taken as established, supports the next step.[^s2] If an intermediate step fails, everything above it loses that line of support unless some other path reaches the conclusion.[^s2]

Plantin stresses a weakness that formal chains do not have. In a valid polysyllogism each step follows fixed rules, so truth passes along the whole chain; in everyday argument the inference principle can shift from one link to the next. A chain of analogies whose basis of comparison changes can drift far from its start, and a long causal chain (dry spring, poor harvest, dear bread, unrest, a fallen government) loses plausibility with each added step, even when every step looks reasonable.[^s4] That is why long chains, including slippery-slope arguments, deserve a link-by-link check rather than a judgment on the overall story.[^s4]

## Limitations

Classifying structure is not always clean. The same passage can be reconstructed as serial or convergent, and the choice depends on interpretation.[^s4] Goddu also argues that structural labels matter less than tracing which statements do double duty, since an argument's line of support can be identified without first settling every label.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Broader: argument.** A serial argument is a way of combining sub-arguments into one larger argument.[^s2]
- **Introduced by Monroe Beardsley in *Practical Logic*.** Hitchcock reports that the term "serial argument" comes from that 1950 book.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with convergent argument.** Plantin's Taurus example shows the same remark read as a chain or as independent converging reasons.[^s4]
- **Contrasts with linked argument.** Goddu separates linked/convergent structure, a question of how premises for one conclusion are grouped, from serial structure, a question of statements doing double duty across sub-arguments.[^s2]
- **Studied in informal logic and applied in argument mapping.** Walton's typology, discussed in *Informal Logic*, treats serial structure as one of four diagram patterns.[^s2]
- **Related to pragma-dialectics.** Its term for the structure is subordinate argumentation, and Snoeck Henkemans catalogued its verbal indicators.[^s3]
- **Related to slippery slope.** A slippery slope is a causal chain whose plausibility weakens with each step.[^s4]
