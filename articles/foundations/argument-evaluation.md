# Argument evaluation

Argument evaluation is the work of deciding how good an argument is: whether its premises deserve to be believed and whether, taken together, they give real support to the conclusion. Logic textbooks frame this as two separate questions, one about the premises and one about the link from premises to conclusion, and different standards apply to that link depending on whether the argument promises certainty or only probability.[^s2] Informal logic adds standards suited to everyday arguments in natural language, and argumentation theory adds dialectical and rhetorical perspectives that judge arguments as moves in a discussion aimed at an audience.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The deductive standards go back to the logical tradition: an argument is valid when its conclusion must be true if its premises are, and sound when it is valid and its premises are in fact true.[^s1] Non-deductive arguments are only ever ampliative, offering degrees of support, so they are judged by strength rather than validity.[^s1] In the 1970s the informal logic movement argued that these standards were too narrow for the arguments people actually make. Ralph Johnson and J. Anthony Blair's textbook *Logical Self-Defense* (1977) organised evaluation around three basic failings (an irrelevant reason, a hasty conclusion and a problematic premise), which came to be read as three positive criteria: premises must be relevant, sufficient and acceptable. Trudy Govier later packaged the same idea in her ARG mnemonic (acceptable, relevant, good grounds).[^s3] Freeman argues that these three conditions are necessary for a cogent argument but that each still lacks an agreed philosophical analysis, and proposes reading them in terms of epistemic justification.[^s3]

## How it works

A practical procedure, described in the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, avoids having to guess what the arguer intended. First ask whether the argument is valid; if it is, ask whether its premises are true, which settles soundness. If it is invalid, ask whether the premises would make the conclusion probable (strength) and whether those premises are probably true (cogency).[^s2] The informal-logic version asks the same questions in a different vocabulary: is each premise acceptable to a reasonable audience, does each premise bear on the conclusion, and do the premises together supply enough grounds to make the conclusion reasonable to believe?[^s3][^s4]

Consider a workplace case. A team lead argues: "We should move our ticketing system to Vendor B, because B's licence is cheaper, the team liked B's demo, and B is used by a well-known retailer." Acceptability: is the price comparison current and does it include migration costs? Relevance: the retailer's choice says little about whether B fits this team's workflows. Sufficiency: cost and one demo leave out data security, integration and support, so the conclusion is supported only in part: the premises are acceptable but partly irrelevant and jointly insufficient.[^s3][^s4]

Dialectical approaches judge an argument as a contribution to a dialogue: pragma-dialectics, for example, sets normative rules for resolving a difference of opinion, and Hamblin emphasised that fallacies must be judged in their dialectical context. Rhetorical approaches, such as Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca's New Rhetoric, assess arguments against the audience they address, with the universal audience as the normative ideal.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. Reconstruct the argument: state the conclusion and list each premise, including any unstated assumptions.[^s4]
2. Check each premise for clarity and acceptability; reject claims that conflict with what is known to be the case.[^s4]
3. Check relevance: does each premise raise or lower the likelihood of the conclusion?[^s4]
4. Check sufficiency: are all parts of a complex conclusion addressed, and is each part given enough support?[^s4]
5. Decide the standard: valid and sound if it claims certainty, or strong and cogent if it claims probability.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

Hoffmann and Catrambone (2023) turned the RSA criteria into an eight-step checklist and used it to code hundreds of student-built argument maps. In a 2019 lab study of 95 students, unacceptable premises were rare (2.6% of premises), irrelevant premises were more common (11.0%), and insufficiently justified parts of a conclusion were the most frequent failure (34.7%); a 2018 classroom sample showed a similar pattern.[^s4] The authors caution that such criteria can objectively flag specific defects but cannot by themselves certify that an argument is good overall.[^s4] More broadly, the standards are contested: relevance and sufficiency resist general definition, and some patterns traditionally labelled fallacies can still raise a conclusion's probability on Bayesian accounts.[^s3][^s1]

## Connections

Argument evaluation is part of critical thinking and presupposes the notion of an argument. It is studied in informal logic, where Freeman places the relevance, sufficiency and acceptability criteria at the centre of the field, and in argumentation theory, which adds dialectical and rhetorical standards.[^s3][^s1] Its RSA criteria were shaped by Johnson and Blair's *Logical Self-Defense*.[^s3] Validity, soundness and cogency are its core verdicts for deductive and non-deductive arguments.[^s2] It is taught and studied in critical thinking education, where checklists derived from it have been used to score student argument maps.[^s4]
