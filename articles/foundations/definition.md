# Definition

A definition explains what a word or phrase means by pairing the expression being defined (the *definiendum*) with an expression that does the defining (the *definiens*). Philosophers separate definitions that try to fit how a word is already used from those that assign it a meaning by decision, and they ask whether a definition lets the defined term be replaced by its definiens wherever it occurs.[^s1] For critical thinkers, definitions matter because most disputes about whether something "counts" as X are really disputes about what X means, and an argument can only be evaluated once its key terms hold still.[^s2]

## Origin

The idea that a definition states what a thing essentially is goes back to Aristotle, and John Locke later split *real* definitions (stating a thing's real essence) from *nominal* ones (stating the idea a word is attached to), a distinction still used though it is admittedly rough.[^s1] Twentieth-century work refined the picture: Rudolf Carnap's *explication* keeps a term's central uses while stipulating the rest, and Gottlob Frege's definition of number made the logical requirements on definitions a research topic in their own right.[^s1] The purpose-based taxonomy found in logic courses (lexical, stipulative, precising, theoretical, persuasive), together with rules for good definitions drawn from Copi and Cohen's logic textbook, is the version most critical-thinking teaching uses; it is presented alongside Locke's warning that words lacking fixed meanings obstruct knowledge.[^s2]

## How it works

**By purpose.** A *lexical* definition reports existing usage; a *stipulative* one assigns a meaning, often to a new term; a *precising* one narrows a lexical meaning to cut down vagueness; a *theoretical* one ties a term to a wider theory, as Newton did with "mass"; and a *persuasive* one loads a term with emotive force in order to sway attitudes.[^s2] Philosophers add *descriptive* definitions, graded by how closely they fit usage, and *ostensive* ones that introduce a word by pointing at an example.[^s1]

**By method.** A denotative definition works through the term's extension (the things it applies to) by listing or pointing at members; a connotative definition works through its intension (the shared features), most classically by naming a genus and then the difference that marks the term off within it.[^s2] A course handout puts the same split as nominal definitions (fixing usage) versus real ones that are either intensional, as in "humans are rational animals", or extensional, by examples.[^s5]

**Rules for a good definition.** Common teaching lists: capture essential rather than incidental features; avoid circularity; match the term's extension, being neither too broad ("bird" as warm-blooded animal) nor too narrow (feathered egg-layer); avoid figurative or obscure wording; and prefer affirmative to negative phrasing where possible.[^s2] Some handouts also require neutrality, ruling out emotionally loaded definientia.[^s5] Formal treatments are more cautious: they stress eliminability and conservativeness, and note that work on revision theory argues some circular definitions can be legitimate.[^s1]

**A workplace example.** A support team's dashboard reports that "resolved tickets" rose 30 percent after a process change. One manager counts a ticket as resolved when an agent closes it; another counts it only when the customer has not reopened it within a week. Both readings are lexically plausible, so the dispute over whether the change worked is partly verbal. Writing down one definition, checking it against borderline cases (closed, then reopened on day eight) and recounting under it turns a quarrel about words into a factual question that data can settle.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. When an argument turns on whether a case falls under a term, ask what definition is being assumed; arguments from verbal classification usually rest on an argument from definition.[^s4]
2. Say which kind of definition you are offering: reporting usage, stipulating for this discussion, or sharpening a vague term.[^s2]
3. Test it against the rules: try to find items it wrongly includes or excludes, and check that it does not quietly reuse the term or its opposite.[^s2][^s5]
4. Watch for definitions tailored to one disputed case. A persuasive definition shifts a term's descriptive content while keeping its approving or disapproving tone, so the redefined word carries its old prestige to a new target.[^s3]
5. If the parties agree once terms are fixed, the disagreement was verbal; if they still disagree, the definition has exposed the genuine issue.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Studied in** philosophy of language, where the logic of definitions, their kinds and their requirements are analysed.[^s1]
- **Applied in** argument from verbal classification: Walton and Macagno argue that classification arguments are typically backed by an argument from definition, and they draw on Aristotle's *Topics* for ways to assess definitions.[^s4]
- **Studied in** informal logic and argumentation theory, which treat definitions as moves in persuasion dialogues.[^s4]
- **Contrasts with** persuasive definition: an ordinary definition aims to clarify meaning independently of the case at hand, whereas a persuasive one is built ad hoc around the case to redirect attitudes.[^s3]
- **Related to** precising, stipulative and operational definitions (its kinds), to ambiguity and vagueness (the defects definitions repair), to equivocation and the no-true-Scotsman move (failures that exploit shifting definitions), to begging the question (a circular definition can hide its conclusion), and to verbal disputes, which clarified definitions dissolve.[^s1][^s2][^s3]
