# Explanation

An explanation is a set of statements that tells why or how something is the case, where that something is already accepted as true.[^s1][^s2] Philosophers of science call the thing to be explained the *explanandum* and the statements offered to account for it the *explanans*.[^s3] Explanations look like arguments: both link claims with words such as "because" and "so". The difference lies in what the passage is for. An argument tries to show *that* a doubtful claim is true; an explanation takes the claim as given and shows *why* it holds.[^s1][^s2] Telling the two apart is one of the first skills in critical thinking, because judging an explanation by the standards for an argument, or the reverse, produces confused criticism.[^s1]

## Origin

Interest in explanation is ancient, but the modern technical vocabulary comes from philosophy of science. In 1948 Carl Hempel and Paul Oppenheim proposed the deductive-nomological (DN) model, on which a scientific explanation is a deductive argument: the explanandum follows from true premises that include at least one law of nature.[^s3] Hempel added an inductive-statistical version for events covered by probabilistic laws.[^s3] These models made explanation a kind of argument. Later accounts pulled them apart. Wesley Salmon's statistical-relevance and causal-mechanical models, the unificationist accounts of Michael Friedman and Philip Kitcher, and Bas van Fraassen's pragmatic theory of explanations as answers to contrastive why-questions all treated explanation as something other than mere derivation.[^s3]

In informal logic the distinction became a teaching staple. Trudy Govier's essay on why arguments and explanations differ, collected in her *Problems in Argument Analysis and Evaluation* (updated edition 2018), sets out the case against critics who said the line cannot be drawn.[^s1]

## How it works

Govier identifies several differences.[^s1]

- **Direction.** An argument moves from better-known premises toward a conclusion that is in doubt. An explanation starts from a fact that is accepted and cites factors that account for it.[^s1]
- **Purpose.** Argument aims at justification or rational persuasion. Explanation aims at understanding how or why something came about, often by naming causes.[^s1]
- **Flow of certainty.** In an argument, confidence is meant to pass from premises to conclusion. In an explanation, confidence in the explained fact does not change.[^s1]

A workplace example shows the same sentence pattern doing both jobs. A support manager says: "Ticket volume spiked on Monday because the password-reset email went out with a broken link." If everyone already knows about the spike and wants to understand it, this is an explanation; the spike is the explanandum. Now suppose the manager says: "The reset email went out with a broken link, and broken links in reset emails always flood the queue, so we should expect a spike on Monday." The spike is now a prediction in doubt, and the statement is an argument for it.[^s1][^s2]

The quick test is to ask whether the main claim is already taken for granted by speaker and audience. If it is, and the other statements say why it is so, the passage is an explanation; if the other statements are meant to show that it is true, it is an argument.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Find the target claim** and ask whether the listener already accepts it.[^s2]
2. **Ask what the "because" is doing.** Is it giving evidence for the claim, or a cause or reason behind it?[^s1]
3. **Use the right standard.** Evaluate an argument for premise acceptability and support. Evaluate an explanation for whether the cited factors are true, relevant and sufficient to account for the fact; Salmon stressed that irrelevant factors, harmless in a valid argument, ruin an explanation.[^s3]
4. **Watch for hybrids.** Govier grants that one passage can do both jobs, as when convergent reasons for a claim also name its causes, but holds that the distinction stays useful.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

The distinction is normative and conceptual, not an empirical finding. Its boundary is disputed. Some critics argue that the same passage can be read either way, and that whether a claim counts as accepted depends on the audience; Govier replies that a good argument can be a poor explanation and vice versa, so the two need different criteria.[^s1] Within philosophy of science, the DN model's treatment of explanation as argument faces well-known counterexamples. Deriving a flagpole's height from its shadow fits the model yet explains nothing, which shows that a sound derivation is not automatically an explanation.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Confused with argument.** The shared "because" form makes students and practitioners mistake explanations for arguments and the reverse, a confusion Govier's essay is written to dispel.[^s1][^s2]
- **Studied in philosophy of science**, where the nature of scientific explanation has been a central topic since Hempel and Oppenheim.[^s3]
- **Formalized by the deductive-nomological model**, the first precise account of what makes something a scientific explanation.[^s3]
- **Used inside inference to the best explanation.** In abduction a hypothesis is accepted because it would best explain the evidence, judged by virtues such as simplicity and coherence.[^s4] Govier notes that this is an argument, not an explanation: the known fact is the premise and the hypothesis is the conclusion.[^s1]
