# Argument

In logic and critical thinking, an argument is not a quarrel. It is a set of statements in which some, the premises, are put forward as reasons for another, the conclusion.[^s2][^s3] An argument has exactly one main conclusion and at least one premise, and the claimed link between them is called the inference.[^s3] Everything else in the evaluation of reasoning, including validity, soundness, inductive strength and the fallacies, is a property of arguments or a way they go wrong, so the concept comes first.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Aristotle gave the first systematic account. In *Prior Analytics* I.1 he describes a deduction (*syllogismos*) as discourse in which, once certain things are stated, something different from them follows of necessity.[^s4] His logical works, together with his study of how arguments are used in debate and how they can be faked in *Sophistical Refutations*, set the agenda for the next two millennia.[^s1] In the twentieth century the focus widened. Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument* (1958) questioned whether formal deduction is the right model for everyday reasoning, and Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca's New Rhetoric, pragma-dialectics and Walton's argument schemes followed, building the modern field of argumentation theory.[^s1]

Philosophers still disagree about what makes a group of statements an argument. A structural definition says it is enough that someone offers some statements as reasons for another. A pragmatic definition adds a purpose: the arguer is trying to rationally persuade an audience of the conclusion. Critics note that arguments can aim at other attitudes as well, such as suspicion.[^s2]

## How it works

The Stanford Encyclopedia separates the *argument*, a premise-conclusion structure, from *argumentation*, the communicative activity of producing and exchanging reasons in which arguments occur.[^s1] Analysis starts with the structure.

**Finding the parts.** Indicator words help. "Because", "since" and "given that" usually introduce premises; "therefore", "so", "thus" and "hence" usually introduce conclusions.[^s2][^s3] These words are clues, not proof, because the same words also appear in sentences that argue nothing.[^s2] Questions, commands and greetings cannot be premises or conclusions, since they are not statements that can be true or false.[^s3]

**Standard form.** Writing the premises on numbered lines above a line, with the conclusion below it, makes the structure visible.[^s2] Take a workplace example. A team lead writes: "We should postpone the launch. QA found two unresolved data-loss bugs, and our release policy forbids shipping with open data-loss defects." In standard form:

1. QA found two unresolved data-loss bugs in the release.
2. Release policy forbids shipping with open data-loss defects.
3. Therefore, we should postpone the launch.[^s2]

Here the premises are **linked**: neither supports the conclusion without the other. Premises are **convergent** when each gives independent support, as when separate reasons all point to the same forecast. An argument is **extended** when one premise is itself the conclusion of a sub-argument.[^s2] An argument with an unstated premise is an **enthymeme**, and reconstruction means supplying the missing step charitably.[^s2]

**Kinds of support.** In a deductive argument the arguer claims the premises guarantee the conclusion. In an inductive argument the claim is only that they make it probable. Some logicians add conductive arguments, in which convergent reasons are weighed together.[^s2] Abductive and analogical arguments are further types, inferring the best explanation of the facts or reasoning from similarity between cases.[^s1]

**Arguments versus explanations.** The same "X because Y" shape can do two jobs. An argument tries to show *that* something is so. An explanation tells *why* or *how* something already accepted is so.[^s3] Saying the smoke is here because there is a fire explains the smoke; saying there must be a fire because of the smoke argues for the fire.[^s3] The IEP adds that whether a passage counts as one or the other can depend on context and on what the audience already accepts.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Find the conclusion first.** Ask what the speaker wants you to believe or do. Then ask what reasons are given for it.[^s3]
2. **Check that it is an argument.** Rule out explanations, reports, conditional statements nobody is asserting, and circular restatements that only repeat the conclusion.[^s2][^s3]
3. **Put it in standard form**, adding any suppressed premise the argument needs. Choose the most plausible version unless there is clear evidence against it.[^s2]
4. **Judge it by the right standard.** Don't fault an inductive argument for not being deductively valid. Ask instead how strongly its premises support the conclusion.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Studied in argumentation theory and logic.** Formal logic studies which argument forms are valid, while argumentation theory studies how arguments work in real dialogue and persuasion.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with explanation.** An explanation aims to increase understanding of something already accepted. An argument aims to make an unaccepted claim acceptable.[^s2][^s3]
- **Contrasts with inference.** The IEP separates an argument, the set of statements put forward, from inference, the mental act of drawing a conclusion.[^s2]
- **Prerequisite for validity, soundness and the fallacies.** Each of these assumes the premise-conclusion structure described here.[^s1][^s2]
- **Related to the principle of charity and the Toulmin model.** Charity governs how gaps are filled during reconstruction. Toulmin's model offers a richer alternative to the plain premise-conclusion layout.[^s1][^s2]
