# Reasoning

Reasoning is what a person does when they get from some things they already accept, such as beliefs, evidence, or goals, to a conclusion they take those things to support. Philosophers describe it as an inferential process that takes some of a subject's attitudes as input and yields a new or changed attitude as output.[^s1] Arguments, validity, inductive strength and the fallacies are the standards and products of this activity, so it sits underneath almost everything else in critical thinking.[^s2]

## Origin

The idea that there are two kinds of reasoning is old. A long tradition separates theoretical reason, which settles what to believe about how things are, from practical reason, which settles what to do. The two differ in subject matter (facts and their explanation versus value and action) and in what they change: theoretical reflection revises beliefs, while practical reflection forms intentions.[^s1] One modern reading treats both as normative, governed respectively by epistemic norms about belief and practical norms about action.[^s1]

In the 1980s the philosopher Gilbert Harman pressed a further distinction that critical thinkers often miss. In *Change in View: Principles of Reasoning* (MIT Press, 1986) he argued that reasoning is a change in one's view, while logic is the theory of what implies what. Logic tells you that premises imply a conclusion; it does not tell you whether to accept the conclusion or give up a premise.[^s3]

## How it works

Reasoning is usually sorted by how strongly its conclusions are supposed to follow. Deductive reasoning aims at conclusions guaranteed by the premises; inductive reasoning generalises from experience or applies a generalisation to a case, and yields only probable conclusions; abductive reasoning (inference to the best explanation) moves from evidence to the hypothesis that best explains it.[^s2] An introductory textbook illustrates induction with a neighbour who has walked her dog every morning, so will probably do so today, and abduction with a car that will not start, which points to a flat battery.[^s2]

Harman's point shows up in an everyday workplace case. A finance analyst believes "every purchase over 500 dollars needs a manager's sign-off" and sees a 620-dollar order with no sign-off. Logic says these premises imply a policy breach. But the analyst may reason differently: the order is so routine that perhaps the threshold changed, so the right move is to check the rule rather than file a complaint. Accepting the conclusion and doubting a premise are both open, and which is reasonable depends on how confident one is in each belief.[^s3] Harman also observed that inconsistent premises logically imply anything, yet nobody thinks a person with inconsistent beliefs may conclude whatever they like.[^s3]

Practical reasoning follows the same pattern with goals in place of facts. A team deciding whether to ship on Friday weighs risk, deadlines and staff availability; on some views the reasoning ends in a judgment about what they ought to do, on others directly in an intention or in the action itself.[^s1]

## How to apply it

- **Name the type.** Ask whether a conclusion is meant to be certain (deduction), probable (induction), or the best available explanation (abduction), and judge it by the matching standard: validity, strength, or explanatory virtues such as simplicity and depth.[^s2]
- **Treat "it follows" as a fork.** When an argument leads somewhere surprising, ask which is weaker, your confidence in the conclusion's falsity or your confidence in a premise, before accepting either.[^s3]
- **Separate belief from action.** Keep "what is true here?" apart from "what should we do?", since the second also needs values and goals.[^s1]
- **Reason with others.** Put your answer up for challenge; arguments from a peer who disagrees are a cheap check on solitary error.[^s5]

## Evidence

Psychologists have long documented that individuals reason poorly on many laboratory tasks and favour their own views. Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber (2011) proposed that this is because reasoning evolved mainly to produce and evaluate arguments in communication, not to improve solitary belief. On their account confirmation bias is a feature of argument production, and people reason better when a problem is set in an argumentative exchange.[^s4] The theory is influential but contested; the target article appeared with an extended open peer commentary and discussion section (pages 74–111).[^s4]

Some of its predictions have support. In a 2014 study by Trouche, Sander and Mercier, 22% of adults solved a disjunctive logic problem alone, but 63% did after group discussion. The member with the right answer usually won the group over even when not the most confident, and good arguments, rather than confidence, explained most mind changes.[^s5] The authors also found a problem on which confident but wrong members often prevailed, so group reasoning is no guarantee.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Studied in logic.** Logic supplies the standards of validity and strength used to assess deductive and inductive reasoning.[^s2]
- **Studied in epistemology.** Theoretical reasoning is governed by epistemic norms about what one ought to believe.[^s1]
- **Studied in the psychology of reasoning.** Experimental work on how people actually reason, including its failures, is the starting point for Mercier and Sperber's theory.[^s4]
- **Contrasts with logical consequence.** Harman separates the activity of changing one's view from the relation of implication that logic describes.[^s3]
- **Explained by the argumentative theory of reasoning.** Mercier and Sperber hold that reasoning's function is to devise and assess arguments; this is one proposed explanation, not a consensus.[^s4]
- **Related to confirmation bias.** On the argumentative view, the tendency to find support for one's own position is a predictable product of reasoning done alone.[^s4]
