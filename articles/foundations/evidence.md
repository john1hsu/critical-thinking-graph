# Evidence

Evidence is whatever bears on whether a claim is true: it makes a difference to what a person is justified in believing.[^s1] In everyday use the word covers physical objects, such as a fingerprint or a server log, while philosophers have variously identified evidence with experiences, known propositions or mental states; Thomas Kelly's Stanford Encyclopedia entry traces this clash to the several different jobs the concept is asked to do.[^s1] For critical thinking the core idea is simpler. Premises in an argument are only as good as the evidence behind them, and a good reasoner believes a claim to the degree that the total available evidence supports it.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The ideal of proportioning belief to evidence has a classic statement in David Hume's *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (1748). In the section on miracles, Hume describes the wise person as weighing opposing observations, siding with whichever side has more experiments behind it, and holding the conclusion with matching confidence; probability, he says, presupposes such an opposition of observations.[^s3] In the nineteenth century W. K. Clifford hardened the norm into an ethical duty: it is always wrong to believe on insufficient evidence.[^s2] In the twentieth century Carl Hempel and Rudolf Carnap tried to make evidential support precise, separating a yes-or-no notion of confirmation from a quantitative one built on the probability calculus, the ancestor of present-day Bayesian accounts.[^s1] Contemporary *evidentialism*, defended by Earl Conee and Richard Feldman since 1985, holds that whether a belief is justified depends on whether the believer's evidence supports it.[^s2]

## How it works

Kelly distinguishes four roles evidence plays.[^s1]

- **Justifier.** Evidence is what makes believing reasonable. Rationality means responding properly to one's evidence, which is compatible with being misled by it.[^s1]
- **Guide to truth.** Evidence is a sign or indicator of what it is evidence for, as a characteristic rash indicates a particular infection.[^s1]
- **Neutral arbiter.** Public evidence is supposed to settle disputes between rival theories and secure agreement across observers, though theory-laden observation and differing prior beliefs complicate this.[^s1]
- **Something to be weighed.** Support comes in degrees and from many items at once.[^s1][^s3]

The standard probabilistic test is that E is evidence for hypothesis H when learning E makes H more probable than it was, and evidence against H when it makes H less probable; proof and refutation are the limiting cases.[^s1] Kelly notes objections: raising a claim's probability slightly does not always seem enough to count as evidence for it.[^s1]

Two further principles matter in practice. The **total evidence requirement** says justification depends on the whole body of evidence, not a convenient subset, because a reason that supports a belief on its own can be **defeated** by further information.[^s1] Defeaters come in two kinds: *rebutting* defeaters support the opposite conclusion, while *undercutting* defeaters cut the link between the evidence and the hypothesis without supporting the opposite.[^s1]

Take a workplace example. A product manager sees that sign-ups rose 20 percent the week a new onboarding flow launched and concludes the flow worked. The rise is evidence: it is more probable if the flow helped than if it did not. But the total evidence also includes a marketing campaign that ran the same week. That fact is an undercutting defeater, since the rise is expected whether or not the flow helped, so it no longer discriminates between the two explanations. Learning that sign-ups through the old flow in a holdout group rose just as much would be a rebutting defeater.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Richards Heuer's CIA text on intelligence analysis turns these ideas into working habits.[^s4]

1. **Ask about diagnosticity, not just consistency.** A high fever tells a doctor the patient is ill but says little about which illness, because it fits many diagnoses. Evidence that is consistent with every live hypothesis has little or no diagnostic value; the items that discriminate between hypotheses should drive the judgment and get the hardest accuracy checks.[^s4]
2. **Weigh abstract evidence fairly.** Vivid, concrete and personal information has more impact than it deserves, while statistical data, which often carries more evidential weight, tends to be discounted.[^s4]
3. **Notice what is missing.** People rarely factor gaps in the evidence into their confidence; Heuer recommends listing relevant information that is absent and adjusting confidence accordingly.[^s4]
4. **Check reliability, not only coherence.** The mind is oversensitive to how consistent a set of evidence looks and undersensitive to how reliable it is, and impressions often survive after the evidence behind them is discredited.[^s4]
5. **Use all of it.** Before concluding, ask whether any known information would change the verdict if it were included, in line with the total evidence requirement.[^s1]

## Limitations and debates

Philosophers disagree about what evidence is. Timothy Williamson holds that one's evidence is exactly what one knows; Conee and Feldman count current mental states; others prefer facts or experiences.[^s1] Evidentialism faces objections from forgotten evidence, from cases where evidence entails a claim the believer has not grasped, and from William James's view that some beliefs may be held on passional grounds when evidence is not decisive.[^s2] Whether evidence can be fully neutral between rival theories is also disputed, since its bearing can depend on background theory and prior beliefs.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Studied in epistemology.** Evidence is central to theories of justified belief and to the philosophy of science.[^s1]
- **Formalized by Bayesian reasoning.** The probability-raising account of support, and Bayesian accounts that combine evidence with prior probabilities, give evidential support a quantitative form.[^s1]
- **Applied in analysis of competing hypotheses.** ACH builds its matrix around the diagnosticity of each item of evidence against several hypotheses at once.[^s4]
- **Influenced by David Hume.** Hume's maxim that a wise person proportions belief to the evidence is the classic statement of the norm and serves as the epigraph of Kelly's entry.[^s3][^s1]
- **Related: evidentialism, testimony and defeasible reasoning.** Evidentialism makes evidence the sole basis of justification; testimony is one major source of evidence; and the defeat of evidence by new information is what makes most everyday reasoning defeasible.[^s2][^s1]
- **Related: argument from ignorance and belief perseverance.** Heuer warns that an absence of evidence does not by itself disprove a hypothesis, and that impressions often outlive the evidence that produced them; both are characteristic ways of mishandling evidence.[^s4]
