# Assumption

An assumption is a claim that a piece of reasoning takes for granted instead of supporting. Most assumptions are never said out loud, and that is exactly why they matter: a conclusion can follow neatly from its stated reasons and still be no better than the unstated claim holding it up.[^s2][^s6] Lists and tests of critical-thinking abilities routinely include identifying assumptions, alongside working with definitions, and understanding what an assumption is sits next to argument, premise and conclusion among the basic concepts a careful reasoner needs.[^s1]

## Origin

Talk of "assumptions" is old and everyday, but its careful analysis is a product of the critical-thinking and informal-logic movement. Robert Ennis's paper "Identifying Implicit Assumptions" (*Synthese*, 1982) and his widely used outline of critical-thinking abilities list "attribute unstated assumptions" as an advanced clarification skill and separate several senses of the word.[^s2] In 1985 David Hitchcock, writing in *Informal Logic*, distinguished the assumptions of an argument from the assumptions of an arguer, a distinction Ennis adopted.[^s2][^s4] The Paul-Elder framework of the Foundation for Critical Thinking made assumptions one of its eight elements of thought, glossed as presuppositions, axioms and what is taken for granted.[^s6]

## How it works

Ennis separates four senses that ordinary speech blurs together.[^s2]

- **Pejorative sense.** "You're just assuming that" suggests the claim is doubtful or false. This flavour often rides along with the other senses, but it is not part of what makes something an assumption.[^s2]
- **Presupposition.** A claim that must be true for a statement to make sense at all.[^s2] Gilbert Plumer defines it as a necessary condition for a premise or conclusion to be true, or for one of its terms to refer to anything.[^s3]
- **Needed assumption** (Hitchcock's "assumption of the argument"). A claim the reasoning requires to be at its strongest, even if not strictly logically required.[^s2][^s4]
- **Used assumption** (Hitchcock's "assumption of the arguer"). A claim the person actually relied on, which can only be inferred like a hypothesis, from what they said and did.[^s2]

A workplace example shows the difference. A manager says: "Since our tracking tool's licence went up again, we should switch to the cheaper one; it costs a fifth less." The phrase "went up again" presupposes earlier price rises.[^s2][^s3] The argument needs the assumption that the cheaper tool meets the team's needs about as well, since otherwise the saving does not justify the switch.[^s2][^s4] The manager may also have used an assumption the argument does not need, such as that migration will take only a day; that is a fact about the arguer's thinking, testable by asking.[^s2]

Plumer treats assumption and presupposition as basic logical notions and calls a logical assumption a proposition that must hold for the rest of an argument to be fully cogent.[^s3] Hitchcock adds that the general claim linking reasons to conclusion in an enthymematic argument is often better seen as a non-formal rule of inference the arguer relies on than as a missing premise.[^s4]

## How to apply it

1. **State the conclusion and reasons, then ask what else must be true** for the reasons to support the conclusion.[^s2][^s5]
2. **Separate needed from used.** Supply what the argument needs; treat claims about what the arguer believed as hypotheses to check.[^s2]
3. **Ask the standard questions.** The Paul-Elder guide asks what you are taking for granted, whether those assumptions are justified, and which could reasonably be questioned.[^s6]
4. **Test the key ones.** The CIA's *Tradecraft Primer* recommends writing down the current line of analysis, listing every stated and unstated premise it depends on, challenging each, and asking how far the conclusion would shift if one failed.[^s5] Its example from a 2002 sniper investigation shows how an unexamined assumption about the suspect's vehicle could narrow a search too early.[^s5]

## Limitations

Assumption-finding is interpretation, so different readers can attribute different assumptions to the same text.[^s4] Interpreters also add premises for different purposes, such as explaining why someone concluded what they did, strengthening a weak argument, or discrediting it, and these aims should not be confused.[^s4] Plumer cautions that in some contexts assumption, presumption and presupposition seem to merge into the single idea of an implicit premise, yet they differ in kind, and his paper sets aside the practical question of how to find candidate assumptions.[^s3]

## Connections

Assumption contrasts with presumption: Plumer argues every presumption is an assumption or presupposition, but presumptions are distinctively conventional, introduced by rules such as those governing promises, while assumptions are not.[^s3] It also contrasts with presupposition, which concerns whether a statement makes sense rather than whether an argument is at its strongest.[^s2][^s3] Assumptions are one of the elements of thought in the Paul-Elder critical-thinking framework.[^s6] The concept is studied in informal logic, where Hitchcock's analysis of enthymematic arguments shows how a needed assumption fills the gap in an enthymeme.[^s4] The key assumptions check is the structured technique for surfacing and testing them, and the concept was sharpened by Robert Ennis's account of critical-thinking abilities.[^s2][^s5]
