# Vagueness

A term is vague to the extent that it has borderline cases: instances where it is unclear whether the word applies, however much we know about the instance.[^s1] "Bald", "tall", "poor", "child" and "red" are standard examples; a man with a thinning crown may be neither clearly bald nor clearly not bald.[^s2] Dowden describes vagueness as fuzziness of meaning, which comes either from borderline cases or from degrees, whereas a word like "seven" is not vague at all.[^s2] For critical thinking, vagueness matters because many everyday arguments, policies and forecasts turn on words whose edges are blurred, and because those blurred edges invite two opposite mistakes: pretending there is a sharp line, and concluding there is no difference at all.[^s3]

## Origin

Sorensen's Stanford Encyclopedia entry traces the modern philosophical notion to Charles Sanders Peirce, whose 1902 entry in the *Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology* called a proposition vague when it is intrinsically uncertain whether the speaker would have counted some possible state of affairs as included, an uncertainty rooted in the speaker's indeterminate linguistic habits rather than the listener's ignorance.[^s1] Bertrand Russell's 1923 paper "Vagueness" developed the idea and located vagueness in the relation between words and things rather than in things themselves, and Gottlob Frege treated vague predicates as a defect of definition.[^s1] Research on vagueness then expanded rapidly after 1975, the year of Kit Fine's supervaluationist account.[^s1]

## How it works

Philosophers distinguish vagueness from two neighbours.[^s1] An **ambiguous** word has several distinct senses, and the speaker can resolve it by saying which one is meant; a vague word has one sense with blurry edges, and no literal choice of sense removes the borderline cases.[^s1] A **general** word covers many cases without having borderline ones: "prime number" is general but not vague.[^s1] Ordinary speech blurs these, using "vague" to mean "unspecific", which Sorensen notes has bred confused explanations.[^s1]

Vagueness produces the **sorites paradox**. A one-day-old is a child; if someone is a child at *n* days, one more day cannot change that; so, step by step, a 100-year-old is a child.[^s1] Each premise looks true and the conclusion is false. The main theories respond differently: epistemicists (notably Timothy Williamson) keep classical logic and say there is a sharp cutoff we cannot know; supervaluationists say borderline sentences lack a truth value while sentences true on every admissible sharpening are "supertrue"; degree theorists assign truth values between 0 and 1; and contextualists let the boundary shift with the conversation.[^s1] Some theorists also argue that "borderline" is itself vague, a problem called higher-order vagueness.[^s1]

A workplace example: a team policy says remote staff must be available for "urgent" requests outside hours. A request at 9 p.m. to fix a broken checkout page is clearly urgent; a request to reword a slide is clearly not; a request to resend a report the client wants "tonight if possible" is borderline. Arguing about where "urgent" ends will not settle it, because the word itself does not decide the case. The team needs a precising definition, such as "anything blocking revenue or a contractual deadline within 24 hours".[^s2]

## How to apply it

- **Spot it.** Ask whether a key word admits degrees or borderline cases, and ask "how much?" or "compared with what?"; "near", "poor" and "soon" all depend on a reference point.[^s2]
- **Match precision to purpose.** Casual talk rarely needs exact boundaries, but law, science, measurement and specifications do, which is why science prefers sharply defined terms and why a judge must rule on a borderline "theft" anyway.[^s2]
- **Watch for weasel words.** Vague claims are hard to refute, which makes them attractive to persuaders ("up to 40 dollars an hour"); yet, on limited evidence, a vaguer conclusion can also be the more defensible one.[^s2]
- **Avoid the line-drawing fallacy.** The inference "there is no sharp line between X and Y, so X and Y do not really differ" fails because borderline cases do not erase clear cases; 2 p.m. is day and 2 a.m. is night.[^s3] Nor does an imprecise boundary make every boundary acceptable.[^s3]
- **Translate vague probability words into numbers.** Sherman Kent recounted a 1951 CIA estimate calling an invasion of Yugoslavia that year a "serious possibility": Kent meant about 65 to 35 in favour, policy readers took it as much lower, and his own board members had odds from 20-80 to 80-20 in mind.[^s4] His proposed fix paired each phrase with a numeric range, though colleagues he called "poets" resisted the stiffer prose.[^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

Vagueness itself is a conceptual property, not an empirical effect, but its costs in communication have been measured. In a 2019 online experiment with 924 members of the public, Wintle and colleagues found wide spread in the numbers people attached to intelligence-style phrases such as "unlikely" and "very likely"; putting the official numeric range in brackets in the text raised overlap with the guidelines to 66%, against 32% with no guidance, while tables and tooltips helped little because many readers never opened them.[^s5] The lesson is that a written convention only reduces vagueness if readers see it.[^s5]

Vagueness is not simply a flaw. Sorensen's entry reports arguments that it aids learning and communication and that vague legal standards can be strategic, deterring conduct near a boundary; others reply that the useful feature is really generality.[^s1] Textbooks agree that forcing precision everywhere would be arbitrary and impractical; nobody could learn "bald" by hair count.[^s3] Which logic of vagueness is correct remains an open dispute among philosophers.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Studied in** philosophy of language and philosophical logic, the home of Sorensen's survey of its theories.[^s1]
- **Explains** the sorites paradox, which exists only because predicates like "child" tolerate small changes.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Charles Sanders Peirce in the sense that his 1902 definition launched the modern analysis.[^s1]
- **Applied in** legal reasoning, where judges must still decide borderline cases and those rulings set precedent.[^s2]
- **Explains** the line-drawing fallacy, which wrongly treats the absence of a sharp boundary as the absence of a difference.[^s3]
- **Related to** ambiguity (several meanings, not fuzzy edges), precising definitions and bright-line rules (ways to sharpen a vague term), slippery-slope arguments, and words of estimative probability, the analysts' remedy for vague likelihood language.[^s1][^s3][^s4]
