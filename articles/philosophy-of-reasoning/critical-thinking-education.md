# Critical thinking education

Critical thinking education is the effort to teach people, from primary school to professional training, to interpret, analyse and evaluate claims and arguments well, and the research field that asks which ways of doing so work. Its central questions are practical: should reasoning be taught in its own course or inside subjects like history and science, which classroom activities produce measurable gains, and do those gains carry over to problems students meet later?[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The modern tradition is usually traced to John Dewey, whose Laboratory School in Chicago built its curriculum around children's own inquiry; teachers' and pupils' accounts describe gains in problem-solving habits, though without control groups or standard tests.[^s1] In 1941 Edward Glaser wrote critical-thinking teaching materials for several grade levels and, with Goodwin Watson, the tests later known as the Watson-Glaser tests; secondary students given ten weeks of his instruction improved more than a control group.[^s1] Later programmes include Matthew Lipman's Philosophy for Children, cross-curricular university requirements, and an OECD project on creativity and critical thinking run across 11 countries.[^s1] The 1990 Delphi consensus gave the field a widely used working definition of what such teaching should aim at.[^s1]

## How it works

Robert Ennis's 1989 typology sorts instruction into four approaches, and Abrami and colleagues' 2015 meta-analysis used it to classify interventions.[^s1]

- **General:** critical thinking taught as its own subject, apart from content courses.[^s1]
- **Infusion:** deep subject teaching with explicit attention to reasoning principles.[^s1]
- **Immersion:** deep subject teaching that invites critical thinking but never names its principles.[^s1]
- **Mixed:** a general course combined with infusion or immersion.[^s1]

Cognitive scientists add advice about what makes any of these work. Tim van Gelder argued in 2005 that reasoning is a hard, higher-order skill that improves only through active, guided practice with feedback, that transfer to new contexts has to be taught rather than assumed, and that a small amount of theory (a shared vocabulary for premises, objections and fallacies) helps students notice and correct their own errors.[^s3] Daniel Willingham's 2019 paper for the NSW Department of Education stresses that skilled thinking leans on domain knowledge, so it recommends naming the specific reasoning skills for each subject, teaching them explicitly, and revisiting them across several years.[^s2] He also recommends problem comparison: students contrast two worked problems that share a deep structure but differ on the surface.[^s2]

A workplace example: a company wants its analysts to judge vendor claims better. A two-hour lecture on fallacies would be a pure general approach with little practice. A design closer to the evidence would teach a few named moves, such as asking what a figure is compared with or mapping an argument. Analysts would then practise them on the team's real past decisions, discuss their maps in pairs, get feedback from a senior reviewer, and meet the same moves again in later quarters on different kinds of claims.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

Abrami and colleagues' 2015 meta-analysis pooled 341 effect sizes from experimental and quasi-experimental studies before 2010. Instruction had a small-to-moderate average effect (0.30).[^s1] Teaching that involved dialogue, authentic or anchored problems, and mentoring did better, especially in combination. The mixed approach scored highest (0.38 against 0.23 to 0.29 for the others), but those differences were not statistically significant.[^s1] Van Gelder reported that students in argument-mapping courses improved faster than those in conventional courses, though his article gives no effect sizes.[^s3]

Two cautions temper these results. Huber and Kuncel's 2016 meta-analysis found that skills and dispositions grow substantially over a normal college education. Curriculum-wide efforts to boost critical thinking, however, did not clearly add long-term gains.[^s4] An AERA summary of the study, covering 71 reports over 48 years, likens the overall skill gain to moving from the 50th to the 72nd percentile. It adds that general critical-thinking instruction did not beat an ordinary college experience in the long run.[^s5] Willingham notes that transfer from generic programmes is modest and fades quickly, and that experts often reason no better than anyone else outside their own field.[^s2] Few studies are randomised, because classrooms are hard to randomise. University implementations have often lacked a shared theory or enough time to take hold.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Influenced by John Dewey.** His inquiry-centred Laboratory School is the usual starting point of the tradition.[^s1]
- **Supported by Abrami et al. (2015).** Its meta-analysis is the main evidence that explicit instruction improves measured critical thinking.[^s1]
- **Challenged by Huber and Kuncel (2016).** Their meta-analysis found that adding critical-thinking curricula did not clearly beat ordinary college in long-run gains.[^s4][^s5]
- **Studied in cognitive psychology.** Research on practice, transfer and belief preservation shapes how the field designs instruction.[^s3]
- **Related to argument mapping, the Socratic method and metacognition.** Mapping makes argument structure visible. Dialogue and questioning were among the most effective features in the meta-analysis. Self-correction depends on students monitoring their own reasoning.[^s1][^s3]
- **Related to confirmation bias.** Van Gelder lists belief preservation among the habits that teaching must deliberately counter.[^s3]
