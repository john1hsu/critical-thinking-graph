# Testimony

In epistemology, testimony is not limited to sworn statements in court. It covers any case where one person tells another something and the listener comes to believe it on that basis.[^s2] Most of what anyone believes about history, geography, science, medicine or even their own birthday arrives this way, so the question of when a belief formed from someone's say-so is justified sits under almost every act of critical thinking about sources.[^s1][^s3] Philosophers disagree about how to define testimony precisely. Some identify it with assertion, while C. A. J. Coady (1992) added conditions such as the statement being offered as evidence on an open question by someone with relevant standing.[^s1]

## Origin

The modern debate is usually traced to two eighteenth-century Scots. David Hume (1740, 1748) held that we are entitled to rely on testimony only because experience has shown us that reports tend to match the facts, so testimonial justification reduces to perception, memory and induction.[^s1][^s2] Thomas Reid (1764) replied that trust in what others say is a natural endowment rather than a lesson learned from experience, which is why young children rightly believe their caregivers long before they could have checked anyone's track record.[^s2] Coady, whose work in 1973 and 1992 revived the topic, coined the labels "reductionism" and "anti-reductionism" for the two camps.[^s2]

## How it works

The central question is what a listener needs in order to be justified.

- **Reductionism** says the hearer needs positive reasons, not themselves taken from testimony, for thinking the speaker is reliable.[^s1] *Global* versions want reasons to trust testimony in general; Coady objected that no one has checked enough reports to supply them, and that any such check would itself lean on other people's reports.[^s1][^s2] *Local* versions, defended by Elizabeth Fricker, ask only for reasons to trust this speaker on this occasion and stress the hearer's duty to watch for signs of untrustworthiness.[^s1][^s2]
- **Anti-reductionism**, associated with Reid, Coady and Tyler Burge (1993), says hearers have a defeasible entitlement to accept what they are told, provided they have no reason to doubt it.[^s1][^s2]
- **Hybrid views** split the difference. Jennifer Lackey (2008) requires some positive reasons on the hearer's side plus actual reliability on the speaker's side; Fricker (1995) lets children believe without reasons while asking more of adults.[^s1]

Consider a workplace case. A new analyst asks a colleague where the quarterly sales file lives and is told "the finance shared drive, in the Q3 folder." A strict local reductionist must say the analyst is justified only if they already have evidence that this colleague is reliable about file locations. Critics point out that we accept directions from strangers this way all the time, and that local reductionism seems to make such ordinary beliefs unjustified.[^s1] An anti-reductionist says the analyst may simply believe it unless something is off, such as the colleague having just joined the company yesterday. The worry pressed against that view is gullibility: an anonymous blog post or a strange notebook should not earn default trust.[^s1]

A second debate asks whether testimony can only *pass along* knowledge the speaker already has. Lackey's "creationist teacher" case challenges this. A teacher who personally rejects evolution still teaches the evidence for it accurately, and her students seem to come to know the theory from a speaker who does not believe it.[^s1] Other live questions include whether a speaker's personal assurance ("I'm telling you") adds justification beyond evidence, and how a committee or organisation can testify when no single member believes the report it issues.[^s1]

## Evidence

Developmental psychology supplies empirical support for the idea that trust is selective rather than blind. A 2018 *Annual Review of Psychology* synthesis by Harris, Koenig, Corriveau and Jaswal reports that infants already seek out knowledgeable informants. As children grow, they get better at spotting inaccurate claims and at weighing an informant's access to the facts, group membership and agreement with others.[^s3] The same review notes that children sometimes defer to an informant for social reasons rather than epistemic ones, and may set aside their own beliefs when testimony is counterintuitive.[^s3] Sperber and colleagues (2010) argue that, because communication leaves listeners open to accidental or deliberate misinformation, humans have evolved a suite of epistemic-vigilance mechanisms that check both who is speaking and what is said.[^s4] Findings like these do not settle the philosophical dispute, but they bear on it: Leonard's SEP entry notes that work in social psychology has been used to question whether ordinary hearers can do the monitoring that local reductionism demands.[^s1]

## How to apply it

- **Ask what kind of reliance you are placing.** Accepting a colleague's directions and accepting an anonymous viral claim are both testimony, but the stakes and the available defeaters differ.[^s1]
- **Look for defeaters, not just credentials.** Both camps agree that evidence of a speaker's incompetence, insincerity or conflict of interest should undercut trust.[^s1][^s2]
- **Check the speaker's access.** Ask whether the speaker was in a position to know; children learn to make this check early.[^s3]
- **Check the content as well as the source.** Vigilance works on plausibility and coherence too, not only on who is speaking.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Studied in epistemology** as a basic source of justification alongside perception, memory and inference.[^s1]
- **Studied in developmental psychology**, which tracks how children learn from and selectively trust informants.[^s3]
- **Explained in part by epistemic vigilance**, the proposed cognitive mechanisms that let people benefit from communication while filtering out unreliable reports.[^s4]
- **Requires justified true belief**, the baseline analysis of knowledge that testimonial knowledge is measured against.[^s2]
- **Linked to David Hume and Thomas Reid**, the historical sources of reductionism and anti-reductionism.[^s1][^s2]
- **Practical neighbours:** evaluating expert testimony, the argument from expert opinion, source credibility and source evaluation apply these questions to particular speakers.[^s1]
- **Related to philosophical skepticism**, since global reductionism invites a regress about how any reliance on others could be grounded.[^s1]
