# Justified true belief

The justified true belief (JTB) analysis says that a person S knows a proposition p exactly when three conditions hold: p is true, S believes p, and S is justified in believing p.[^s1] It is an attempt to give conditions that are each necessary and together sufficient for *propositional* knowledge (knowing *that* something is so), as opposed to knowing a person or knowing how to do something.[^s1] Because each condition is easy to state and test, the analysis is a useful working checklist for anyone asking "do we actually know this, or do we just believe it?"

## Origin

Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper framed JTB as the shape that attempts to define knowledge "have often" taken, citing close variants by Roderick Chisholm (1957: accepting p, having adequate evidence, p true) and A. J. Ayer (1956: p true, being sure of p, having the right to be sure).[^s4] In a footnote he added that Plato seems to consider such a definition in the *Theaetetus* and perhaps accepts one in the *Meno*.[^s4] In the *Theaetetus*, the third proposal is that knowledge is true belief together with an account (*logos*); Socrates tests three readings of "account", rejects each, and the dialogue ends without a definition.[^s2]

The label "traditional analysis" is itself disputed. Julien Dutant (2015) argues that the view most philosophers held before Gettier was not JTB but what he calls *classical infallibilism*: knowing requires a belief bearing a discernible mark that only true beliefs can have.[^s3] On that reading, JTB is partly a twentieth-century reconstruction that Gettier's cases made famous.

## How it works

Each condition does distinct work.[^s1] **Truth**: nobody can know something false; at most they can believe it. **Belief**: knowledge is a state of the knower, so a claim that is true but that you do not accept is not something you know. **Justification**: it screens out lucky guesses. Steup's example is a person who irrationally becomes convinced he has a serious illness and turns out to be right; his belief is true but not knowledge because nothing supported it.[^s1]

A workplace example shows both the checklist and its weak point. An on-call engineer believes the payments service is up because the monitoring dashboard shows green. The belief is true (the service is up) and justified (the dashboard is normally reliable). On JTB, she knows the service is up. But suppose the dashboard froze an hour ago and the service merely happens to still be running. Her belief now satisfies all three conditions yet seems true by luck, which is the pattern Gettier exploited.[^s4] Gettier's own cases relied on two premises: one can be justified in believing something false, and justification carries over to what one validly deduces from a justified belief.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Used as a review question, JTB separates three failures that are often blurred in meetings and reports.[^s1] Ask: is the claim actually true (or what would show it false)? Does the team actually hold it, or is it an assumption no one has examined? What is the justification, and is it connected to the truth of the claim in the right way, or could the claim be true for reasons unrelated to our evidence? The last question is the one Gettier cases teach: a belief can rest on a false intermediate step and still land on the truth.[^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

As a philosophical analysis, JTB is not an empirical hypothesis, but it is widely regarded as refuted as a definition. Gettier's counterexamples show that truth, belief and justification are not jointly sufficient for knowledge.[^s4] Responses either strengthen the justification condition or add a fourth condition; Steup notes that a "no false lemmas" clause fails against cases where the subject infers nothing false, and that reliabilist accounts face fake-barn style cases.[^s1] Even the belief condition has critics, such as Colin Radford's example of a quiz contestant who answers correctly while disclaiming belief, though JTB defenders reply that the contestant lacks justification.[^s1] The historical claim that JTB was ever the received view is also challenged.[^s3]

## Connections

JTB is a central topic studied in epistemology, where it is the usual starting point for the analysis of knowledge.[^s1] It is challenged by the Gettier problem, introduced in Gettier's 1963 paper, whose counterexamples are an application of the method of counterexample to a proposed definition.[^s4] It contrasts with reliabilism, which replaces the justification condition with a belief's being produced by a reliable process, and with internalist accounts that require justification to be recognisable by the subject.[^s1] It also contrasts with classical infallibilism, which Dutant argues was the actual pre-Gettier tradition.[^s3] Its ancestry runs back to Plato's *Theaetetus* and its failed attempt to define knowledge as true belief with an account.[^s2]
