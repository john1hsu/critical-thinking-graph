# Conversational implicature

A conversational implicature is something a speaker means, suggests or implies without saying it, which the hearer infers from the way the remark fits the conversation.[^s2] If a colleague asks "Are you coming to the offsite?" and you answer "I have a client call that day," you have not said you are skipping the offsite, but you have communicated it.[^s2] For critical thinkers the idea marks a boundary that matters in every argument: what a statement logically commits its speaker to is one thing, and what listeners reasonably take it to suggest is another.[^s2]

## Origin

The term and the theory come from the Oxford philosopher H. P. Grice. The SEP entry on Grice traces the idea to a 1961 paper on perception and says it was a central theme of his William James Lectures at Harvard in 1967.[^s1] The key lecture was published in 1975 as "Logic and Conversation".[^s3] Grice used implicature to answer ordinary-language philosophers such as Austin, who inferred from the fact that a remark would be odd to say that it must be false or meaningless. Grice argued the oddness could come from conversational rules, not from the sentence's truth conditions.[^s1] The same move let him defend the logician's reading of connectives: a conditional can be true in the material sense yet misleading to assert.[^s1]

## How it works

Grice's starting point is the cooperative principle: make your contribution such as the accepted purpose of the exchange requires.[^s3] He spelled this out in four groups of maxims. Quantity asks for as much information as needed and no more. Quality asks speakers not to say what they believe false or lack evidence for. Relation asks for relevance. Manner asks for clarity, brevity and order.[^s3] A speaker implicates q by saying p when the hearer can only square p with the presumption of cooperation by supposing the speaker believes q, and the speaker expects the hearer to work this out.[^s3] Implicatures arise both when speakers seem to observe the maxims and when they flout one openly, as in irony.[^s2]

Grice separated these from conventional implicatures, which ride on word meaning: "poor but honest" signals a contrast because of "but", whatever the context.[^s3] Among conversational implicatures, generalized ones attach to a form by default, as "some" suggests "not all", while particularized ones depend on the specific setting.[^s2]

Take a workplace case. A project lead tells the director, "Some of the test cases passed." The director concludes that some failed and delays the release. The lead's report does not entail any failures; the director inferred "not all" because a cooperative speaker who knew all had passed would presumably have said so (quantity).[^s2] The lead can consistently add "in fact, they all passed", which shows the "some failed" content was an implicature.[^s2] The director's reasoning is not foolish, but it treats a defeasible suggestion as a premise.

## How to apply it

1. **Separate said from suggested.** When an argument turns on a remark, write down what the words commit the speaker to and, separately, what you took them to suggest.[^s2]
2. **Test by cancelling.** Add "though not necessarily Q" or "indeed, not-Q". If the result is consistent, Q is an implicature, not an entailment.[^s1][^s2]
3. **Name the maxim.** Ask which maxim produced the inference (quantity for "some", relation for an off-topic answer, manner for the order of events in "and"). This shows what background assumption it depends on.[^s2][^s3]
4. **Ask instead of assuming.** If a decision rides on the implicature, request the explicit claim. Conversely, a speaker who relies on hints cannot be held to them as assertions, which matters when judging whether someone misled you.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

The theory has been refined and challenged. Davis's SEP entry reports that Gricean reasoning seems to overgenerate, predicting implicatures that hearers do not draw, and that cooperation often fails to fix a single implicated content.[^s2] Neo-Griceans such as Horn and Levinson reorganized the maxims around scales like all/most/some, while Sperber and Wilson's relevance theory replaced them with a single principle of relevance balanced against processing effort.[^s2] The SEP Grice entry treats relevance theory as pursuing different but compatible aims.[^s1]

Experimental work on scalar implicatures supports the core phenomenon but shows it is variable, so the status here is mixed. A 2014 study by Janssens, Fabry and Schaeken reviews findings, starting with Noveck in 2001, that children often accept "some giraffes have long necks", reading "some" logically. Their own experiments found pragmatic "not all" readings rose with age, from under half of 3-year-olds' answers to about 80% at age 5 and over 90% at age 7, and shifted with task format and content.[^s4] Working memory had no reliable effect in their sample.[^s4] Whether hearers compute these inferences by default or only at a processing cost remains debated.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Paul Grice** in the 1967 William James Lectures, building on earlier work on perception.[^s1]
- **Introduced in "Logic and Conversation"**, which states the cooperative principle, the maxims and the working definition.[^s3]
- **Explained by the cooperative principle**: an implicature is what the hearer must suppose to keep the speaker cooperative.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with logical consequence**: implicatures can be cancelled and reinforced; entailments cannot be cancelled without contradiction.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with relevance theory**, which explains the same inferences with one principle of relevance instead of four maxims.[^s2]
- **Studied in pragmatics**, the study of meaning in use, where the semantics–pragmatics boundary is still argued over.[^s1]
- **Related** to the cancellability test and conditional perfection, both treated in this graph as implicature phenomena, and to unwarranted contrast, the fallacy of treating the "not all" reading of "some" as entailed.[^s2]
