# Educational assessment

Educational assessment is the practice and study of gathering evidence about what learners know and can do, and of using that evidence well. Its two classic purposes differ: summative assessment certifies or ranks attainment at the end of a unit, while formative assessment, in Black and Wiliam's broad sense, is any activity by teachers or students whose results are used as feedback to change the teaching and learning under way.[^s2] For critical thinking the field asks a harder version of its usual question: can a test score really show how well someone reasons, and whether they are inclined to reason that way when nobody prompts them?[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Assessment of reasoning has a long track record in standardised instruments. David Hitchcock's survey in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy lists general tests such as the Watson-Glaser Critical Thinking Appraisal, the Cornell Critical Thinking Tests, the essay-based Ennis-Weir test, the California Critical Thinking Skills Test, the Halpern assessment, the Collegiate Learning Assessment and ETS's HEIghten, plus aspect-specific tests like Norris and King's Test on Appraising Observations.[^s1] Robert Ennis set out much of the critique of these tools, including a 1984 checklist of problems (unsound multiple-choice keys, test takers' background beliefs, untrained essay graders) and a 1996 paper on whether dispositions can be assessed at all.[^s1][^s4] On the formative side, Paul Black and Dylan Wiliam's 1998 review screened 681 publications, kept about 250, and became the standard reference for the claim that classroom feedback raises achievement.[^s2] A 2014 ETS report by Ou Lydia Liu and colleagues reviewed how higher-education and workforce frameworks define critical thinking and how well existing tests measure it, then proposed an operational definition for next-generation assessments.[^s3]

## How it works

Two technical ideas run through the field. **Validity** belongs to an interpretation of scores for a given use, not to a test in itself, and is built from several kinds of evidence. **Reliability** is consistency across forms, items, occasions or raters; it is needed for validity but does not guarantee it, just as a bathroom scale can be consistently wrong.[^s1] A designer therefore starts from the decision the scores will inform, defines the construct, writes tasks that elicit it, and then checks that scores behave as the interpretation predicts.[^s1][^s3]

A workplace example: a firm wants to know whether a new analyst-training course improves judgement. Asking trainees to rate their own open-mindedness on a survey is cheap but easy to game, because the preferred answer is obvious.[^s1][^s4] A sounder design gives each trainee a realistic memo before and after the course and asks for a written evaluation of its main claim, without saying which habit is being scored. Two trained raters then code whether the response looks for alternative explanations, and the firm checks how often the raters agree. Results go back to trainees as specific comments rather than a bare grade, which is the kind of task-focused feedback Black and Wiliam found most useful.[^s2][^s4]

## Assessing skills versus dispositions

Skills can be sampled with well-built items: Norris and King validated their observation test by interviewing students about why they chose each answer.[^s1] Dispositions are harder. Ennis argued they are hidden until a situation calls them out, that face-valid self-report items are easy to fake, and that factor analysis shows only which items cluster, not what they measure. He favoured guided open-ended tasks scored for whether a disposition was shown, lacking or not revealed; in his project raters agreed closely, yet two items correlated only weakly, so he called the approach exploratory.[^s4] Hitchcock reports Facione's 2000 finding that scores on the California dispositions inventory and the matching skills test correlate only weakly, which suggests skills and dispositions must each be taught and assessed directly.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

Black and Wiliam reported that strengthening formative feedback produced sizeable gains across the studies they reviewed, with effect sizes in the studies they cite mostly between about 0.5 and 0.8, and they found no reports that enhanced feedback did harm. They also listed caveats: many studies ran in special conditions, outcome measures varied, definitions of formative assessment were loose, and gains on tests that closely mirror instruction may reflect teaching to the test.[^s2] For critical-thinking tests, Hitchcock summarises Rear's 2019 objections that standardised instruments sidestep disputes about the concept, fall short on validity and reliability, and do not sample real academic tasks.[^s1] Liu and colleagues likewise flag unresolved challenges in designing, implementing and using such tests, which is why evidence for the field is best described as mixed.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Applied in critical thinking education.** Tests such as the Critical Thinking Assessment Test were built so faculty could use results to improve their teaching.[^s1]
- **Supported by Black and Wiliam (1998).** Their review is the main evidence that formative use of assessment raises learning.[^s2]
- **Studied in psychometrics.** Validity, reliability and the psychometric quality of existing critical-thinking tests are the core of the ETS review.[^s3]
- **Influenced by Robert Ennis.** His checklists and his work on disposition assessment shaped how the critical-thinking community judges its tests.[^s1][^s4]
- **Related to thinking dispositions, self-report measurement and metacognition.** Disposition scales depend on self-report, whose weaknesses Ennis analysed, and student self-assessment is one of the formative practices Black and Wiliam highlight.[^s2][^s4]
