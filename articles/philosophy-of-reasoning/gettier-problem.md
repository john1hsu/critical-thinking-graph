# Gettier problem

The Gettier problem is the discovery that a belief can be true, and held for good reasons, and still not count as knowledge. In a Gettier case the person's evidence points one way, the facts that make the belief true lie somewhere else, and the two meet only by coincidence.[^s1][^s2] The problem matters for critical thinking because it separates being right from being right for the right reasons, a gap that shows up wherever people judge a conclusion by whether it turned out true.

## Origin

For much of the history of philosophy, a common working definition held that knowledge is justified true belief (JTB): you know a claim if it is true, you believe it, and you are justified in believing it.[^s1] Edmund Gettier noted that Plato appears to consider such a definition in the *Theaetetus*, and that Roderick Chisholm (1957) and A. J. Ayer (1956) had defended close variants.[^s2] In a paper of under three pages, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (*Analysis*, 1963), Gettier offered two counterexamples to all three versions.[^s1][^s2]

His argument used two premises. First, a person can be justified in believing something false. Second, justification carries over through deduction: if you are justified in believing P and you correctly deduce Q from it, you are justified in believing Q.[^s2] In Case I, Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get a job and has ten coins in his pocket, so he infers that whoever gets the job has ten coins. In fact Smith gets the job, and by chance he also has ten coins. In Case II, Smith justifiably believes Jones owns a Ford and infers "Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona." Jones owns no Ford, but Brown happens to be in Barcelona.[^s2] In both cases the belief is justified and true, yet Smith does not know it.

## How it works

Every Gettier case has the same structure: fallible but good evidence, a false step or misleading circumstance, and a lucky match between the belief and the truth.[^s1] A workplace version: a manager sees a dashboard showing that a nightly backup job succeeded and concludes "last night's data is backed up." The dashboard has been frozen since the previous week because of a display bug, so it proves nothing. By coincidence, the backup did run last night. The manager's belief is true and was reasonable, but it rests on a broken signal, so it was not knowledge. If the backup had failed, the manager would have believed exactly the same thing.[^s1]

Alvin Goldman's 1976 "fake barns" case extends the pattern. A driver looks at a real barn in a district full of barn facades and correctly thinks "that's a barn." Nothing in their reasoning is false, but the environment makes the true belief lucky. Most epistemologists judge that this too is not knowledge, though some deny it is a genuine Gettier case.[^s1]

## Responses

The IEP survey groups the main attempts to repair the definition:[^s1]

- **No false lemmas or core evidence.** Require that the belief not depend on any false belief. This handles Gettier's own cases, since both rely on a false step, but not the fake-barn case.[^s1]
- **Defeasibility.** Require that no unknown truth would undercut the justification if the person learned it (Lehrer and Paxson, 1969).[^s1]
- **Causal connection.** Require that the fact that makes the belief true appropriately cause the belief (Goldman, 1967).[^s1]
- **Infallibilism.** Require justification that cannot be wrong. This removes luck but tends toward skepticism, since little everyday belief meets that bar.[^s1]

No proposal has won general agreement. Some philosophers go further and argue that knowledge can sometimes coexist with luck.[^s1]

## Evidence

Gettier's argument rests on the intuition that the subjects in these cases do not know. Weinberg, Nichols and Stich (2001) reported preliminary data suggesting that people from some cultural groups were more willing to call Gettier cases knowledge.[^s1] Later, larger studies did not support that variation. A 2017 study by Machery, Stich, Rose and colleagues surveyed 2,230 participants at 24 sites in 23 countries and 17 languages. On the more informative measure, a clear majority (roughly 70–90%) at 23 of 24 sites gave the Gettier answer. The exception was a very small sample, and the authors cite an earlier replication by other researchers that also found no cultural difference.[^s3] The intuition looks broadly shared. The authors still call for further replication, and the study used a single vignette.[^s3]

## How to apply it

- **Ask whether the reasons would still hold if the conclusion were false.** If you would believe the same thing either way, being right says little about your reasoning.[^s1]
- **Check the false step.** Trace the chain of inference. A true conclusion reached through a false intermediate belief is fragile.[^s2]
- **Don't score reasoning only by outcomes.** Brock (2018) argues that a student's correct answer can come from a misconception that happens to fit one case. One example is "every moving object has a resultant force acting on it," which gives the right answer for an accelerating rocket and the wrong one for a satellite at constant speed. Teachers can catch this by asking where the belief applies and testing it on contrasting cases.[^s4] The same check works for forecasts, audits and post-project reviews.

## Connections

- **Introduced by Edmund Gettier in "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?"** (1963).[^s2]
- **Studied in epistemology**, where it sparked decades of work on the analysis of knowledge and epistemic luck.[^s1]
- **Targets justified true belief**, which must be understood first. Gettier traces that definition back to Plato.[^s2]
- **Uses the method of counterexample.** A single case where the definition is satisfied but the term does not apply refutes a proposed definition.[^s2]
- **Relies on deductive closure.** Gettier's cases depend on justification carrying over through valid deductive inference.[^s2]
- **Supported by cross-cultural data.** The 2017 multi-country study found the core intuition widely shared.[^s3]
- **Applied in science education** to detect students whose correct answers rest on unreliable reasoning.[^s4]
