# Bloom's taxonomy

Bloom's taxonomy is a classification of what students are expected to be able to do as a result of instruction.[^s1] It gives teachers, trainers and assessors a shared vocabulary for learning goals and a way to check that goals, activities and tests line up.[^s1][^s4] In critical-thinking discussions it is the usual source of the phrase "higher-order thinking": analyzing, evaluating and creating are treated as more demanding than recalling or restating information.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The original *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, Handbook I: Cognitive Domain* appeared in 1956. Benjamin S. Bloom, an American educational psychologist, led a group of university examiners and measurement specialists that included Max Engelhart, Edward Furst, Walker Hill and David Krathwohl.[^s1][^s4] Their practical aim was to let faculty at different institutions exchange test items that measured the same objective, so that item banks could be shared instead of rebuilt.[^s1] The handbook defined six categories: Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis and Evaluation, ordered from simple and concrete to complex and abstract.[^s1] Companion taxonomies were later developed for the affective domain (values and attitudes) and the psychomotor domain (physical skills).[^s3]

In 2001 Lorin Anderson, Krathwohl and colleagues published a revision, *A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing*.[^s1] It renamed the categories as verbs (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create), swapped the top two so that Create now sits above Evaluate, and split "knowledge" out into a separate dimension.[^s1][^s3]

## How it works

The revised framework has two dimensions.[^s1] The cognitive-process dimension holds the six verbs above, broken into 19 more specific processes such as "differentiating" or "critiquing".[^s1] The knowledge dimension distinguishes factual, conceptual, procedural and metacognitive knowledge; the last, knowledge about one's own thinking, was new in the revision.[^s1] Crossing the two gives a taxonomy table: each objective, activity and test item is placed in a cell, which makes gaps and mismatches visible.[^s1][^s3]

Consider a workplace example. A compliance team writes a training goal that says staff will "understand the expense policy", but the end-of-module quiz only asks them to recall spending limits. Placed on the table, the goal sits at Understand while the quiz sits at Remember with factual knowledge, so the assessment cannot show whether the goal was met.[^s1] If the real aim is for staff to judge whether an unusual claim is legitimate, the goal belongs under Evaluate, and the quiz should present borderline cases and ask for a justified decision.[^s1][^s3]

## How to apply it

1. Write each objective as a verb plus the kind of knowledge it acts on, then place it in the taxonomy table.[^s1]
2. Check alignment: the activities and assessments for an objective should sit in the same cell, not a lower one.[^s1][^s3]
3. Scan the table for coverage. A course that clusters entirely in Remember and Understand gives learners little practice in analysis or evaluation.[^s3]
4. Judge verbs in context rather than from a lookup list. The same verb, such as "explain" or "select", can demand very different thinking depending on the task, so check what the assessment actually requires.[^s2]

## Evidence

The original authors treated the six categories as a cumulative hierarchy, in which mastery of each simpler level was a prerequisite for the next.[^s1] The 2001 revision relaxed this: the categories may overlap and are no longer assumed to be strictly cumulative, although their central points still run from simple to complex.[^s1] Critics question whether the ordering holds at all, arguing for example that understanding can result from analyzing and applying rather than preceding them.[^s2]

How the taxonomy is used in practice is also weakly grounded. A 2020 study by Newton, Da Silva and Peters collected 47 Bloom's verb lists from 35 UK higher-education sources and found little agreement: of 401 unique verbs, 251 were assigned to a single tier, but only 10 of those appeared in more than half the lists, and four verbs appeared at all six levels.[^s2] Most lists cited no evidence, and 25 cited no source at all.[^s2] The authors concluded that the taxonomy's popularity rests more on convention than on evidence, and that the revision's nuance about a non-rigid hierarchy is usually lost.[^s2]

## Connections

Bloom's taxonomy was introduced by Benjamin Bloom and his committee in the 1956 *Handbook I*.[^s1] It is applied above all in educational assessment, where its original purpose was building comparable test items and its revised purpose is aligning objectives, instruction and assessment.[^s1] It contrasts with the SOLO taxonomy of Biggs and Collis, which classifies how well a learner connects pieces of information rather than which cognitive operation they perform.[^s3] The claim that its levels form a reliable hierarchy, and the verb lists built on that claim, are challenged by the 2020 analysis of Newton and colleagues.[^s2] Its metacognitive-knowledge category, added in the revision, links it to metacognition, and its upper tiers are the usual reference point when educators describe analysis and evaluation as higher-order skills.[^s1][^s2]
