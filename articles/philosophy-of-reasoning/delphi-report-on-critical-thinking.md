# Delphi Report on critical thinking

The Delphi Report is the common name for *Critical Thinking: A Statement of Expert Consensus for Purposes of Educational Assessment and Instruction*, published in 1990 by the philosopher Peter A. Facione for the American Philosophical Association (APA). It set out an expert consensus on what critical thinking is: purposeful, self-regulating judgment that produces interpretation, analysis, evaluation and inference, together with an explanation of the evidence, concepts, methods, criteria or context on which that judgment rests.[^s1] Its definition, its list of six core skills and its portrait of the "ideal critical thinker" became a widely used reference point for teaching and testing critical thinking.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

In December 1987 the APA's Committee on Pre-College Philosophy asked Facione, then at Santa Clara University, to investigate critical thinking and how it could be assessed; California State University, Fullerton helped fund the work.[^s1] Rather than write a definition himself, Facione ran a Delphi process: a panel of 46 experts answered successive questionnaires, saw anonymised summaries of each other's replies, and revised their positions over six rounds between February 1988 and November 1989.[^s1] Just over half of the panel were philosophers (52%), with the rest from education (22%), the social sciences (20%) and the physical sciences (6%).[^s1]

## How it works

The report organises critical thinking into two dimensions. The cognitive dimension has six core skills, each with sub-skills:[^s1]

- **Interpretation**: categorising, decoding significance, clarifying meaning.[^s1]
- **Analysis**: examining ideas, detecting arguments, analysing arguments.[^s1]
- **Evaluation**: assessing claims and assessing arguments.[^s1]
- **Inference**: querying evidence, conjecturing alternatives, drawing conclusions.[^s1]
- **Explanation**: stating results, justifying procedures, presenting arguments.[^s1]
- **Self-regulation**: self-examination and self-correction.[^s1]

The affective dimension lists dispositions such as inquisitiveness, open-mindedness, fair-mindedness, honesty about one's own biases, prudence in judgment, willingness to reconsider, and diligence and persistence in seeking relevant information.[^s1] The panel treated the ideal critical thinker who combines all of these as an aspiration that few people fully reach.[^s1]

A workplace example shows how the skills fit together. A team lead reviewing a vendor's claim that a new tool "cuts ticket resolution time by 40%" first interprets what is being measured, analyses the claim into its premises (which teams, which period, compared with what), evaluates the quality of the pilot data, infers whether a similar gain is plausible for her own team, explains her recommendation and the evidence behind it to her manager, and finally self-regulates by checking whether her enthusiasm for the vendor coloured her reading.[^s1] The report's dispositions describe the habits that make her likely to run through these steps without being prompted.[^s1]

The report closes with 15 recommendations. Among them: aim teaching at producing people who actually use their skills outside class, teach and assess dispositions as well as skills, start critical-thinking education in early childhood and extend it to all students, and judge assessment instruments by content validity, construct validity, reliability and fairness.[^s1]

## Influence

The consensus became the stated basis of commercial tests. The publisher of the California Critical Thinking Skills Test describes the Delphi conceptualisation as the test's foundation and reports separate scores for analysis, inference and evaluation.[^s3] Researchers adapting the test for other languages repeat that lineage, though a 2003 study of a Persian version found modest internal consistency (KR-20 of 0.62 for the total score) and subscales that did not line up with the report's six skills.[^s4] The definition also anchors research synthesis. Abrami and colleagues' 2015 meta-analysis of strategies for teaching critical thinking, published in *Review of Educational Research*, adopted it as its working definition. Their overall finding was a small positive effect of instruction (g = 0.30 across 341 high-quality studies). The largest gains came from combining dialogue, authentic problems and mentoring.[^s2] Outside schools, university courses in fields such as geospatial intelligence open with the report's definition and its description of the ideal critical thinker.[^s5]

## Limitations

The consensus was not unanimous. Agreement was above 95% for analysis, evaluation and inference but lower, though still above 87%, for interpretation, explanation and self-regulation.[^s1] About 61% of panellists held that dispositions are part of what "critical thinking" means, while roughly 30% wanted the term limited to cognitive skills, a split the report records rather than resolves.[^s1] The report itself warns that its sub-skill classification is not the only defensible one. It notes that the relations between the sub-skills, and between skills and dispositions, had not been tested empirically. It also cautions that paper-and-pencil tests can be skewed by reading ability, background knowledge, culture and socioeconomic status.[^s1] Because philosophers made up a majority of the panel, the result reflects that discipline's view of reasoning more than, say, cognitive psychology's.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Authored by Peter Facione.** He designed and ran the Delphi process for the APA and wrote the report.[^s1]
- **Applied in critical-thinking education.** Its definition is the working definition of the largest meta-analysis of critical-thinking instruction.[^s2]
- **Applied in educational assessment.** The California Critical Thinking Skills Test claims it as its conceptual basis, and translated versions of the test inherit that claim.[^s3][^s4]
- **Applied in intelligence analysis training.** A Penn State geospatial-intelligence course uses its consensus statement as the course's framing definition of critical thinking.[^s5]
- **Related to thinking dispositions, actively open-minded thinking and metacognition.** Its affective dispositions and its self-regulation skill describe the same habits of reflective, self-correcting judgment.[^s1]
