# Context of discovery and context of justification

The distinction between the **context of discovery** and the **context of justification** separates two questions that are easy to run together: *how did someone come up with this idea?* and *is the idea any good — what evidence and reasoning support it?* The first is a question about history, psychology or sociology; the second is a question about logic and evidence. Treated as a principle of critical thinking, it says that an answer to the first question does not, by itself, settle the second.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The labels are usually traced to Hans Reichenbach's *Experience and Prediction* (1938), where he contrasted the way a scientist actually finds a result with the way the result is presented and rationally reconstructed for assessment.[^s2] The underlying idea is older: the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that nineteenth-century writers such as William Whewell already separated the "happy thought" that generates a hypothesis from its later verification, and Ward links the distinction to Frege's rejection of psychologism in logic.[^s1][^s2] Karl Popper's *Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934/1959) put the point sharply: how a new idea occurs to someone is a question of fact for psychology, whereas the logic of knowledge concerns only justification or validity, that is, the methods of testing.[^s1] For much of the twentieth century this view assigned discovery to the empirical sciences and justification to philosophy.[^s1]

Reichenbach's own version was more intricate than the slogan. Aufrecht argues that he distinguished a psychological task from three epistemological ones (descriptive, critical and advisory), and that his "rational reconstruction" was still a descriptive exercise, so the familiar two-box picture is a later simplification.[^s4]

## How it works

The principle asks you to file each consideration about a claim in the right box before using it:

- **Discovery questions:** Who proposed this? What inspired it — a dream, an analogy, a hunch, a rival's mistake, a podcast? What motives or social pressures were in play?[^s1]
- **Justification questions:** What predictions or reasons support it? How has it been tested? What would count against it?[^s1]

A workplace example: a team lead dismisses a proposed change to the on-call rota because the idea "came from the intern who read it on a forum". That is a discovery fact. The justification question is whether the new rota reduces missed alerts and burnout, which can be checked by trialling it for a month and comparing incident data. Keeping the boxes apart stops the origin story from standing in for the test.[^s2]

Ward reports that logic textbooks traditionally define the genetic fallacy as exactly this conflation: assuming that an account in one context automatically fixes the account in the other.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Name the claim** being assessed, separately from the person or process behind it.
2. **Sort the remarks** made about it into origin remarks and support remarks.
3. **Set origin remarks aside provisionally** and ask what the support remarks establish on their own.
4. **Bring an origin back in only with a stated link.** An origin is relevant to justification when there is independent reason to think the process that produced the belief is reliable or unreliable — for instance, a measurement taken with a faulty instrument, or a testimony whose source has a track record. Ward stresses that such relevance has to be shown within the context of justification, not assumed from the story of discovery.[^s2]

The common pitfall is overcorrection: treating every fact about origins as irrelevant. On externalist and reliabilist views of knowledge, some causes of a belief do matter to its justification, so the principle forbids *automatic* inference from origin to verdict, not every use of origins.[^s2]

## Limitations and critiques

From the late 1950s onward, critics argued that the sharp line misdescribes science. Norwood Hanson defended a logic of discovery built on abductive ("retroductive") reasoning from surprising data, and later philosophers argued that conceiving and testing a hypothesis are both reasoning processes, so discovery is not a mysterious leap beyond philosophical analysis.[^s1] Thomas Kuhn's historical account of theory choice was read as a direct challenge to the distinction; Hoyningen-Huene revisits this debate and argues that many philosophers wrongly concluded Kuhn had simply failed to understand it.[^s3] Hoyningen-Huene also observes that the "DJ distinction" became a cluster of related distinctions rather than one, a point Ward and Aufrecht take up.[^s2][^s3][^s4] The Stanford entry concludes that there is now broad agreement that philosophical and empirical study of discovery are not mutually exclusive.[^s1]

None of these critiques licenses the genetic fallacy. Even writers who reject a rigid separation accept that the path to an idea does not by itself make it true or false.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Counteracts the genetic fallacy** (`ct:genetic-fallacy`): the fallacy is standardly characterised as conflating the two contexts, so explicitly separating them is the corrective.[^s2]
- **Introduced by Hans Reichenbach** (`ct:hans-reichenbach`), whose 1938 book popularised the terms, though Aufrecht shows his conception differed from the later standard reading.[^s2][^s4]
- **Influenced by Karl Popper** (`ct:karl-popper`), whose *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (`ct:the-logic-of-scientific-discovery`) confined the logic of knowledge to testing; Aufrecht notes that Popper's contrast between questions of fact and questions of validity may have been a source for Reichenbach's terms.[^s1][^s4]
- **Challenged by Thomas Kuhn** (`ct:thomas-kuhn`), whose views on theory choice were taken as an attack on the distinction.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with inference to the best explanation** (`ct:inference-to-the-best-explanation`) in the sense that abductive "logics of discovery" were proposed precisely to bring hypothesis generation back within reasoning.[^s1]
- Related to **debunking** (`ct:debunking`) and the **psychology of reasoning** (`ct:psychology-of-reasoning`), which study belief origins empirically.[^s1][^s2]
