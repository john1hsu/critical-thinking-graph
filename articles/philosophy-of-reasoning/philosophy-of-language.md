# Philosophy of language

Philosophy of language asks how language manages to mean anything at all. Its core topics are meaning, reference (how a word picks out a thing), truth, and use, including speech acts and the implied content of what people say.[^s1] In the sense most often meant today, the label covers work in the Anglo-American analytic tradition and its early twentieth-century German and Austrian roots, though other traditions also theorise about language.[^s1] For critical thinking it is the field that explains why arguments can go wrong before any inference is drawn: a premise can be ambiguous, vague, referentially empty or quietly suggestive.

## Origin

The modern field grew out of the logic revolution of the late nineteenth century.[^s1] Gottlob Frege's 1892 work on sense and reference, following John Stuart Mill's earlier account of names as denoting things (1843), set the agenda, and Bertrand Russell's "On Denoting" (1905) supplied a rival analysis.[^s1] Frege and Russell both hoped to expose the logical structure hidden beneath ordinary grammar.[^s1] Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* (1922) tied sense to what can be expressed in a logical system, the Vienna Circle's logical positivists made verifiability the test of meaning, and Tarski (1933, 1944) gave a formal definition of truth.[^s1] Mid-century the emphasis shifted to use: Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), ordinary-language philosophy and J. L. Austin's speech-act theory (1962).[^s1] Later landmarks include Davidson's truth-conditional semantics (1967), Kripke's and Putnam's work on reference in the 1970s, and Grice's theory of implicature.[^s1][^s3]

## How it works

The field's questions cluster into a few families.[^s1]

- **Reference.** How does "the CFO" or "Mercury" latch onto one object? Frege used identity statements to argue that a name carries descriptive content beyond its bearer, since "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative in a way "Hesperus is Hesperus" is not.[^s2] Russell treated definite descriptions as disguised quantifiers, so "the present King of France is bald" is meaningful but false; Kripke later proposed that names are passed along causal chains from an original naming.[^s2]
- **Meaning and truth.** Truth-conditional theories say that to know a sentence's meaning is to know when it would be true; use-based theories tie meaning to the practices and inferences a sentence licenses.[^s1]
- **Speech acts.** Saying is also doing: promising, warning and ordering are acts performed with words, and the act intended can differ from its effect on the hearer.[^s1]
- **Implicature.** Grice's cooperative principle and maxims of quantity, quality, relation and manner explain how hearers infer what a speaker means from how something is said.[^s3]

A workplace example shows the last point. A colleague asks whether there is somewhere to refuel before a long drive, and you reply that a station is around the corner. You have not said it is open, but you have implied it, because a cooperative speaker would not mention a closed station.[^s3] Grice's test separates this from logical entailment: you can cancel the suggestion by adding "though I don't know if it's open", whereas you cannot cancel what a statement logically entails.[^s3]

## Why it matters for reasoning

Several fallacies and paradoxes in this graph are, at bottom, problems of meaning, so their diagnosis borrows the field's tools.[^s1] Ambiguity and equivocation turn on one expression carrying two meanings; vagueness and the sorites turn on predicates without sharp boundaries; the liar paradox turns on truth and self-reference.[^s1] Scott Jacobs, writing in *Informal Logic* in 2016, applies Gricean pragmatics to argumentation: audiences rely on reasonable presumptions about communication even to work out what an argument is, and much that goes wrong in argument comes from speakers exploiting those presumptions and listeners failing to notice.[^s4] A practical habit follows: when evaluating a claim, separate what was literally asserted from what was merely implied, and hold the speaker accountable for each.[^s3][^s4]

## Limitations

The field is not unified. Descriptivist and causal theories of reference still compete, and Kripke's reply to Donnellan on referential uses of descriptions left that debate open.[^s2] Minimalists and contextualists continue to disagree about how much context fixes what a sentence says.[^s1] Grice's analysis of conditionals as material conditionals also struggles with conditionals embedded inside belief reports, according to Warner and Grandy.[^s3]

## Connections

Philosophy of language was shaped by Frege's sense-reference distinction and by Wittgenstein's early and late work (*influencedBy*).[^s1] Russell's theory of descriptions is a founding influence on theories of reference (*influencedBy*).[^s2] Tarski's formal treatment of truth fed truth-conditional semantics (*influencedBy*).[^s1] Gricean pragmatics is applied in argumentation theory to explain how arguments are interpreted and exploited (*appliedIn*).[^s4] Ambiguity, vagueness, the sorites and the liar paradox are studied within the field, and its roots lie in logic, which is why logic is listed as a prerequisite.[^s1]
