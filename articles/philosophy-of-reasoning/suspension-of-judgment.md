# Suspension of judgment

Suspension of judgment is the third doxastic option alongside belief and disbelief: on a question you have considered, you neither accept nor reject the answer, and you hold the question open.[^s2] As a principle of critical thinking it says that when the available evidence supports neither side, the fitting response is to withhold, not to pick the side that happens to be undefeated.[^s2] It matters in practice because many bad arguments work by hiding this option and presenting "proven true" and "proven false" as the only outcomes.[^s3]

## Origin

The idea is ancient. Pyrrhonian sceptics called it *epoché*: they set opposing arguments and appearances against each other, found them of equal force (*isostheneia*), and so suspended judgment, which they claimed brought tranquillity as a by-product.[^s4] Later Pyrrhonists systematised the routes to suspension in Aenesidemus' ten modes and Agrippa's five, which include disagreement, regress and circularity.[^s4] The sceptics did not stop acting; they followed appearances, nature, custom and their crafts without committing to claims about how things really are.[^s4]

Modern epistemology turned the idea into a norm. William Clifford's dictum that it is always wrong to believe on insufficient evidence is the classic statement of the evidence requirement, and Feldman and Conee's evidentialism extends it to all three attitudes: belief, disbelief or suspension is justified for a person at a time when their evidence supports that attitude.[^s2] Jane Friedman's 2013 paper in *Philosophical Studies* argued that suspension is a genuine attitude toward a question, not merely the absence of belief.[^s1]

## How it works

Friedman's analysis separates suspension from simply having no opinion. You lack beliefs about countless questions you have never considered, such as the closest living relative of the hippo, yet you are not agnostic about them; and someone who avoids thinking about a topic resists belief without suspending.[^s1] Suspending is typically how inquiry ends when it fails to settle a question: the inquirer registers "undecided" and keeps the question live, rather than dropping it.[^s1]

A workplace example: a product team sees a dip in sign-ups the week after a page redesign. One manager says nobody has proven the redesign caused it, so it didn't; another says nobody has proven it didn't, so it did. Both are arguments from ignorance. Pragma-dialectical analysis locates the second error in each: assuming the discussion must end with a positive or negative standpoint, ignoring the neutral position with no standpoint.[^s3] The disciplined reply is to suspend judgment on the cause, say what test would settle it (an A/B comparison, say), and meanwhile choose a reversible action.

## How to apply it

Before concluding, ask whether the evidence actually favours one answer or merely fails to refute it; only the first licenses belief.[^s2] When someone argues from a failed defence, return the burden of proof to the claimant and name "not yet known" explicitly as a legitimate outcome.[^s3] Keep suspension tied to a question and a plan of inquiry, so it does not decay into indifference.[^s1] Where a decision cannot wait, separate acting on the best available option from believing it is true, as the ancient sceptics separated living by appearances from assent.[^s4]

Pitfalls: suspension is not always right. When the evidence does favour one side, withholding is as unjustified as believing without evidence, and evidentialism treats it that way.[^s2] Blanket Pyrrhonian suspension about everything is a philosophical position, not a workplace habit, and James' reply to Clifford argues that some forced, momentous choices permit belief beyond the evidence.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

Suspension of judgment **counteracts the argument from ignorance**, because that fallacy works by ignoring the neutral, undecided position and absolutizing the failure of a defence.[^s3] Its status as the fitting response to balanced evidence is **explained by evidentialism**, which assigns belief, disbelief or suspension according to what the evidence supports.[^s2] The practice was **influenced by philosophical skepticism**, whose Pyrrhonian wing made *epoché* its central method.[^s4] It is **studied in epistemology**, where Friedman's work reopened the question of what kind of attitude it is.[^s1] It is closely tied to the **burden of proof** and to **actively open-minded thinking**, and it guards against the **false dilemma** of "proven or disproven".[^s3]
