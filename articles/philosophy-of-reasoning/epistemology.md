# Epistemology

Epistemology is the philosophical study of knowledge. The name joins the Greek *episteme* (knowledge) and *logos* (study or account), and its central questions are what knowledge is, how it differs from mere opinion, how beliefs come to be justified, and what we can know at all.[^s1] Ram Neta's Stanford Encyclopedia entry frames the field more broadly as the study of kinds of cognitive success, such as knowledge and justified belief, and of their opposite, cognitive failure.[^s2] For critical thinking, epistemology supplies the standards behind everyday words such as *evidence*, *reason*, *justified* and *I know*.

## Origin

The questions are ancient. Plato, in the *Meno* and the *Theaetetus*, presents and defends a version of the "justified true belief" analysis of knowledge.[^s1] Early modern philosophy made skepticism the driving problem: Descartes, in the *Meditations on First Philosophy*, raised the possibility that all experience might be a dream or a deceiving demon's work and then tried to rebuild knowledge on certain foundations, while Hume pressed skeptical arguments about the external world and about induction.[^s1] In 1963 Edmund Gettier's paper "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" showed that a justified true belief can still be true by luck, which set much of the twentieth-century research agenda on the analysis of knowledge and justification.[^s1]

## How it works

Epistemologists usually separate three kinds of knowing: knowing *how* (riding a bicycle), knowing by *acquaintance* (knowing a person or a city) and knowing *that* something is the case, which is the main focus.[^s1][^s2] For knowledge-that, the work splits into a few linked questions.[^s2]

- **Nature.** What must be added to true belief to make knowledge? Justification is the classic answer; Gettier cases show it is not enough, and proposed fixes add a no-defeater or causal condition.[^s1]
- **Justification.** Internalists hold that what justifies a belief must be accessible to the believer's own mind; externalists such as reliabilists hold that a belief is justified if a reliable process produced it, whether or not the believer can tell.[^s1][^s2]
- **Structure.** Foundationalists say chains of reasons end in basic beliefs that need no further support; coherentists say beliefs are justified by how well they hang together as a system.[^s1][^s2]
- **Sources.** Perception, introspection, memory, reason (a priori knowledge) and testimony are the standard routes to knowledge.[^s2]
- **Limits.** Skepticism, whether local to one domain or global, asks whether any of these routes delivers knowledge at all.[^s1][^s2]

A workplace example shows the questions at work. A product manager says, "I know the new onboarding flow cut churn." Epistemology prompts follow-ups: is the belief based on a dashboard (perception of data), a colleague's report (testimony) or an inference from a before-and-after comparison (reason)? Would the same evidence have appeared if churn fell for another reason, so the belief is true only by luck? Is the claim supported by one striking number or by a set of findings that fit together? These are the internalism, Gettier and foundationalism-coherentism debates turned into review questions.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters for critical thinking

Sharon Bailin argues that weak reasoners often lack epistemological understanding rather than particular skills.[^s3] Her example is a graduate student whose essays string together summaries, leave out sources and attach a conclusion unconnected to any reasons; he does not grasp that a conclusion must be supported by evidence, nor what epistemic status a cited source has.[^s3] On her account, critical thinking needs an understanding of how reasons, evidence, justification and conclusions connect, a view of knowledge as the product of ongoing comparison of competing claims, and the recognition that any belief could be mistaken; views of knowledge as certain and handed down by authority, or as mere opinion, block it.[^s3] She adds that this understanding can grow through practice, without a formal epistemology course, provided its basis is made explicit.[^s3]

## Limitations

Epistemology is a normative and conceptual discipline, so its claims are tested by argument and counterexample rather than by experiment, and its core questions remain open: there is no agreed analysis of knowledge after Gettier, and internalism versus externalism and foundationalism versus coherentism are live disputes.[^s1][^s2] Neta notes that the field has widened beyond individual knowers to include epistemic harms and wrongs, with feminist epistemology and work on epistemic injustice asking how social position affects who is believed.[^s2] Bailin's claim that epistemological understanding is the core of critical thinking is one philosopher's position, offered as a corrective to skills-and-dispositions accounts rather than a settled consensus.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Influenced by Plato.** His *Meno* and *Theaetetus* set out the account of knowledge as justified true belief that later epistemology inherits and argues over.[^s1]
- **Applied in critical thinking education.** Bailin argues that teaching critical thinking means building students' understanding of reasons, evidence and justification, the subject matter of epistemology.[^s3]
- **Related.** The Gettier problem and the justified true belief analysis are its best-known topics; the problem of induction and the regress of reasons are classic challenges for theories of justification; philosophy of science applies its questions to scientific knowledge.[^s1][^s2]
