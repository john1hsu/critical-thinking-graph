# Pragmatics

Pragmatics is the study of language in use. Where semantics and logic have traditionally studied the meanings of expression types (words and sentences as such), pragmatics studies utterances: particular acts by particular speakers at a particular time and place, and how the circumstances and intentions surrounding them shape what gets communicated.[^s1] It is often summed up as the study of context-dependent meaning.[^s1] Gunter Senft's survey stresses that language use depends on cultural, situational and interpersonal context and convention, not only on grammar and vocabulary.[^s2] For a critical thinker, pragmatics is the discipline that explains the gap between what someone literally said and what they got across, which is exactly the gap that suggestion, innuendo and many misreadings of arguments live in.[^s4]

## Origin

The term comes from the philosopher Charles Morris, who in "Foundations of the Theory of Signs" (1938), written for the *International Encyclopaedia of Unified Science*, divided the study of signs into syntax, semantics and pragmatics, and assigned to pragmatics the relation of signs to their interpreters.[^s1][^s2] Rudolf Carnap (1942) sharpened the division: syntax deals only with relations among expressions, semantics with expressions and what they designate, and pragmatics with any inquiry that explicitly mentions the language user.[^s1] The field's classic period, roughly the mid-1960s to the mid-1980s, brought J. L. Austin's and John Searle's speech-act theory and Paul Grice's theory of implicature.[^s1][^s2] Most pragmatic theorists today are neo-Griceans of one stripe or another, while Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson's relevance theory offers a rival account built on principles of relevance.[^s1] Alongside the philosophical tradition, linguists and anthropologists developed work on deixis, conversation analysis and ritual talk.[^s2]

## How it works

Korta and Perry separate two jobs that context does.[^s1]

- **Near-side pragmatics** concerns what is needed to fix *what is said*: resolving ambiguity, settling vague terms, and working out who "I", "you", "here", "the manager" or "it" refer to on this occasion.[^s1]
- **Far-side pragmatics** concerns what speakers *do* and *imply* beyond what they say: the speech act performed (promising, warning, requesting) and the implicatures a hearer is entitled to draw.[^s1]

Core topics include deixis and indexicals, presupposition (what a sentence takes for granted, and how that passes into larger sentences), implicature governed by Grice's cooperative principle and maxims of quantity, quality, relation and manner, and speech acts, including indirect ones where a question works as a request.[^s1][^s2] Conversation analysts add turn-taking and repair, the ways people fix misunderstandings as they talk.[^s2]

A workplace example shows both sides. A director asks in a stand-up whether the vendor contracts have been reviewed, and the analyst answers, "Some of them have." Near-side work settles which contracts "them" picks out. Far-side work explains why the director now believes that not all of them have been reviewed: a cooperative speaker who knew all were done would have said so.[^s1] That belief is an implicature, not something the sentence logically entails, so if it later turns out every contract was reviewed, the analyst said nothing false, though the answer was misleading.[^s1]

## Why it matters for reasoning

Scott Jacobs, writing in *Informal Logic* in 2016, argues that the pragmatic principles of expression and interpretation are what let audiences work out what an argument even is, and that much of what goes wrong in argumentation comes from speakers exploiting those presumptions while audiences struggle to notice.[^s4] His case studies show how vague wording such as "only one example of many" invites the hearer to supply the most damaging reading while the speaker keeps deniability.[^s4] The practical lesson is to reconstruct an argument from what was said, then list separately what was merely implied or presupposed, and ask whether the implied part is supported.[^s4][^s1] The same tools explain why charitable reconstruction of unstated premises is legitimate: filling in what a cooperative speaker must have meant is a pragmatic inference, not a guess.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

Experimental pragmatics tests these ideas on real interpreters. A 2014 study in *Psychologica Belgica* reviews Noveck's 2001 finding that most seven- to eight-year-olds accept "Some giraffes have long necks", reading "some" logically, where adults typically take it to mean "some but not all".[^s3] Across three experiments with three- to seven-year-olds, the authors found a clear developmental rise in pragmatic readings, but also that task format and content changed response rates substantially, so how such inferences are measured strongly shapes the results.[^s3]

The field has no settled boundary with semantics. Minimalists keep semantic content narrow, contextualists (including relevance theorists and Korta and Perry themselves) hold that pragmatic processes help determine what is said, and Kent Bach proposes a middle category of "implicitures".[^s1] Senft notes that the line between pragmatics and sociolinguistics or anthropological linguistics is also hard to draw.[^s2]

## Connections

Pragmatics was named and introduced by Charles Morris (*introducedBy*) in "Foundations of the Theory of Signs" (*introducedIn*).[^s1] Grice's theory of implicature is the dominant influence on current theory (*influencedBy*).[^s1] It contrasts with semantics, which studies the conventional meaning of expression types rather than utterances (*contrastsWith*).[^s1] It is applied in argumentation theory as normative pragmatics, which explains how arguments are interpreted and exploited (*appliedIn*).[^s4] Conversational implicature, scalar implicature, conditional perfection, the cancellability test and unwarranted contrast are studied within it; presupposition and relevance theory are central topics; and it sits under the philosophy of language and linguistics.[^s1][^s2]
