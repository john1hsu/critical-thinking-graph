# Operational definition

An operational definition specifies a concept by the procedure used to detect or measure it, so that "does this case count?" becomes a question anyone can answer by carrying out the procedure.[^s3] Research-methods teaching pairs it with a *conceptual* definition, which describes what a construct is and how it relates to other variables; the operational definition then states precisely how the variable will be measured.[^s3] As a critical-thinking tool it turns a vague word into a checkable criterion, and it makes plain when a disagreement is really about which criterion to use.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea comes from the American physicist Percy Williams Bridgman, who set it out in *The Logic of Modern Physics* (1927).[^s1] Hasok Chang's Stanford Encyclopedia entry traces it to two experiences: Bridgman's high-pressure experiments, where no trusted instrument existed and so the meaning of measured quantities was unclear, and Einstein's analysis of simultaneity, which showed that a familiar concept had no settled meaning until a way of checking it was specified.[^s1] Bridgman's slogan was that a concept means no more than a set of operations, a claim he later conceded went too far when read out of context.[^s1]

Psychology took the idea up quickly. Edwin Boring coined the label "operationism" and S. S. Stevens promoted it; behaviourists used it against introspective methods.[^s1] Verhaegh's archival study of Harvard in the 1930s argues that Skinner and Boring were already applying Bridgman's ideas to mind and behaviour before Stevens published his well-known papers, so there were several "operationisms" rather than one.[^s4]

## How it works

A conceptual definition answers "what is it?"; an operational definition answers "how will we tell?"[^s3] The Dalhousie textbook's example is stress: conceptually an adaptive response to perceived threat, operationally a score on a life-events checklist, a perceived-stress questionnaire, or a cortisol level.[^s3] Each yields a different, explicit test for whether someone counts as "highly stressed".

A workplace example: a team argues about whether a product has a "churn problem". Until churn is operationalised, each side can count or discount cases as it likes. Once the team agrees that a customer has churned when there has been no login and no payment for 90 days, the question becomes a query anyone can run, and later disagreements have to be about the threshold rather than about individual customers. This is the same move Bridgman made for length and simultaneity, scaled down to everyday decisions.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. **Start from the conceptual definition.** Write down what the term is supposed to capture before choosing a measure, and say which theory or purpose it serves.[^s2]
2. **State the procedure.** Name the observation, instrument, threshold and scoring rule, precisely enough that someone else would classify the same cases the same way.[^s3]
3. **Fix it before looking at the cases.** Flake and Fried (2020) recommend choosing and justifying measures, and setting scoring rules, ideally before data analysis; they note that a 10-item questionnaire can be summed in 1,023 different ways, each a potential forking path.[^s2]
4. **Use more than one operation where you can.** When several operational definitions of one construct agree ("converging operations"), that is evidence the construct is being captured, not just one instrument's quirks.[^s3]
5. **Report changes openly.** Flake and Fried list undisclosed modifications of measures and switched outcomes as questionable measurement practices; they cite a finding that nearly a third of 89,204 registered studies changed their primary outcome after registration.[^s2]

## Limitations

Critics, summarised by Chang, objected that an operational definition rarely exhausts a concept's meaning, that useful theoretical concepts need not have one, and that it is unclear what counts as an "operation".[^s1] Carl Hempel warned that defining each concept by its own procedure would split science into many unconnected concepts, since every new instrument would define a new quantity.[^s1] Bridgman himself admitted that operational analysis eventually reaches a point where sharpness disappears, and later psychologists treated operational definitions as provisional and multiple rather than as the full meaning of a term.[^s1] A measure can also be perfectly reliable yet invalid: the textbook's example is using finger length to measure self-esteem.[^s3] Operationalising therefore makes a claim checkable; it does not by itself make the measure the right one.

Chang's entry still sees lasting value: tying concepts to operations keeps theories empirically testable and warns against extending a concept to cases where nothing could check it.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Percy Williams Bridgman in *The Logic of Modern Physics*.** Chang's entry dates the doctrine to Bridgman's 1927 book.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with a conceptual definition.** The textbook separates describing a construct from specifying how it is measured, and treats both as needed.[^s3]
- **Applied in psychology.** Boring, Stevens and Skinner carried operational definition into psychology, in rival versions.[^s4][^s1]
- **Challenged by Hempel.** His fragmentation objection is one of the main critiques recorded by Chang.[^s1]
- **Studied in the philosophy of science.** Operationalism is a classic topic there, discussed alongside measurement and meaning.[^s1]
- **Related to precising definitions, vagueness and falsifiability.** An operational definition is a precise, procedure-based way of fixing a term's application, and Chang links its value to keeping concepts testable.[^s1] Fixing the criterion in advance is also what Flake and Fried recommend against flexible, after-the-fact measurement choices, which ties it to [researcher degrees of freedom](../scientific-reasoning/researcher-degrees-of-freedom.md) and preregistration.[^s2]
