# Philosophical skepticism

Philosophical skepticism is the view, or the set of arguments, that we do not know things we ordinarily take ourselves to know. Klein frames skepticism about a field as the claim that suspension of judgment is the only justified attitude toward its propositions.[^s1] This differs from ordinary doubt about things nobody claims to know, such as the exact number of stars in the galaxy; the philosophical skeptic targets our confident, everyday knowledge.[^s1] Skeptical arguments matter to critical thinkers less as conclusions to adopt than as stress tests: they show exactly which assumptions a body of knowledge rests on.

## Origin

The word comes from a Greek verb for examining carefully, and Sextus Empiricus described the skeptic as an inquirer who keeps searching rather than claiming to have found the truth or declaring it unfindable.[^s4] Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE), who wrote nothing, is reported to have taught that things are indeterminate, so we should withhold opinion and thereby reach tranquillity.[^s2] Around 273 BCE Arcesilaus turned Plato's Academy toward skepticism, arguing both sides of questions and attacking the Stoic claim that some impressions guarantee their own truth; Carneades (c. 213–129 BCE) continued the Academic line.[^s2] The Academy's skeptical turn drew on Socrates' profession of knowing only his own ignorance and on the open-ended arguments of Plato's dialogues.[^s4] Sextus Empiricus (2nd or 3rd century CE) left the fullest surviving account of Pyrrhonism in his *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*.[^s2]

In early modern philosophy, René Descartes' *Meditations* (1641) used dreaming and an all-powerful deceiver to raise doubt about the senses and even arithmetic. Newman stresses that Descartes used doubt as a method for finding secure foundations, not to endorse skepticism; in his basket-of-apples analogy, one empties the basket and returns only the sound apples.[^s3]

## How it works

Klein distinguishes forms by scope. Humean skepticism denies inductive knowledge of the unobserved; Cartesian skepticism denies justified belief about the external world while allowing knowledge of one's own mind; Pyrrhonian skepticism is global, denying that any proposition is known.[^s1]

Two argument patterns do most of the work. The **closure argument** runs: if I am justified in believing an ordinary claim, I am justified in believing that a skeptical hypothesis incompatible with it is false; I am not justified in denying the hypothesis (I cannot tell from the inside that I am not a handless brain in a vat); so I am not justified in the ordinary claim.[^s1] The **regress argument**, from the ancient modes attributed to Agrippa, says any reason offered for a belief must either rest on an unsupported assumption, circle back on itself, or launch an endless chain of further reasons, and none of these seems to justify.[^s1][^s2]

A workplace example shows both. A finance lead trusts the month-end revenue dashboard. The closure form asks: does she have grounds to rule out that the upstream feed silently stalled, producing figures that look exactly like fresh ones? If she cannot rule it out, how does she know this month's number? The regress form asks why she trusts the feed: because it reconciles with the ledger. Why trust the ledger? Because it reconciles with the feed. The chain either stops at an assumption, loops, or keeps going.[^s1][^s2]

## Responses

Klein surveys several replies.[^s1] **Foundationalism** holds that some beliefs are justified without inferential support, stopping the regress; **coherentism** rejects the one-way picture of justification; **infinitism** accepts endless chains of reasons. Against the closure argument, Fred Dretske and Robert Nozick denied that knowledge is closed under known entailment, Ernest Sosa proposed safety in place of sensitivity, and contextualists such as Stewart Cohen, Keith DeRose and David Lewis argued that the standard for "knows" rises when skeptical possibilities are raised.[^s1]

Ancient critics pressed a practical objection: if the skeptic assents to nothing, how can they act? Arcesilaus answered with the reasonable, Carneades with the convincing impression, and Sextus with following appearances, nature, custom and practical skills without theoretical commitment.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Used as a method rather than a creed, skepticism yields practical habits. Separate "we are confident" from "we have ruled out the alternative that would look the same," and name the scenario your evidence cannot distinguish from the good case.[^s1][^s3] Trace any key belief's chain of reasons and notice where it ends in an assumption or loops back; that is where an independent check adds the most.[^s2] Suspend judgment when rival accounts are equally well supported, while still acting on the most convincing option where action is needed, as Carneades recommended.[^s2] Follow Descartes' example by doubting in order to rebuild on firmer ground, not to stop inquiring.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader: epistemology,** and **studied in epistemology,** where skeptical arguments shape theories of knowledge and justification.[^s1]
- **Influenced by Plato** and the Socratic dialogues, whose avowals of ignorance helped turn the Academy toward skepticism; this links it to the **Socratic method**.[^s4]
- **Contrasts with dogmatism,** Sextus' name for those who claim to have found the truth, and **with foundationalism,** a leading reply to the regress argument.[^s2][^s1]
- **Related to the problem of induction,** which Klein treats as the Humean form of skepticism, and to **David Hume** and **René Descartes**, whose arguments define the Humean and Cartesian forms.[^s1][^s3]
- **Related to begging the question,** the fallacy that the circularity mode of the regress argument rules out as a source of justification, and to **justified true belief** and the **Gettier problem,** which ask what knowledge requires and so set the target skeptical arguments aim at.[^s1][^s2]
- **Related to actively open-minded thinking:** the Pyrrhonist ideal of continuing to search instead of declaring the question closed parallels the modern disposition to keep weighing alternatives before settling.[^s4]
