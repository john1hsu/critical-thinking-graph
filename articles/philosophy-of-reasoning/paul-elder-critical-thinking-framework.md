# Paul–Elder critical thinking framework

The Paul–Elder framework is a model of critical thinking built by the philosopher Richard Paul and Linda Elder at the Foundation for Critical Thinking. It treats critical thinking as the art of analysing and evaluating one's own thinking in order to improve it: thinking that is self-directed, self-disciplined, self-monitored and self-correcting.[^s1] The model has three linked parts. Thinkers identify the **elements of thought** in any piece of reasoning, assess them against **universal intellectual standards**, and, by doing this habitually, develop **intellectual traits**.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Paul's interest in reasoning goes back to his 1968 dissertation, which criticised formal logic as a model of everyday validation; the elements and standards came more than two decades later.[^s2] In the early 1980s he argued that typical critical-thinking courses taught a "weak sense" skill set of argument parsing and fallacy spotting, which students could turn to defending their own prejudices. He proposed instead a "strong sense" that examines whole world views, weighs arguments dialectically against their rivals, and confronts self-deception.[^s6] He founded the Center for Critical Thinking and Moral Critique in 1980 and the Foundation for Critical Thinking in 1991.[^s2] He first called the standards "perfections of thought" before renaming them, and from the 1990s he developed the full framework with Elder, publishing it in compact "thinker's guides" such as *The Miniature Guide to Critical Thinking Concepts and Tools*.[^s2][^s1]

## How it works

The eight **elements of thought** are the parts present in all reasoning: purpose; the question at issue; information; interpretations and inferences; concepts; assumptions; implications and consequences; and point of view.[^s1] The **intellectual standards** are the criteria applied to those parts: clarity, accuracy, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, logic, significance and fairness, each paired with probing questions such as how a claim could be checked.[^s1] Elder notes that credibility and practicality could be added to the list.[^s2] The **intellectual traits** that are meant to grow from consistent practice are intellectual humility, autonomy, integrity, courage, perseverance, empathy, confidence in reason and fair-mindedness.[^s1][^s3]

Consider a manager drafting a proposal to move a team to a four-day week. Working through the elements, she states the purpose (retain staff without losing output), the question (would output hold?), the information (a pilot at another site), her inferences, the concepts in play ("output" measured how?), her assumptions (that the pilot site is comparable), the implications (customer coverage on Fridays) and the points of view of clients and finance.[^s1] Applying the standards, she asks whether the pilot data are accurate, whether "output" is precise enough to measure, and whether she has considered the strongest objection fairly.[^s1] The traits describe the attitude that keeps such checking honest: being willing to find that her favoured plan is weaker than she hoped.[^s1][^s6]

## How to apply it

Use the elements as a checklist when reading or writing any argument, report or plan, and use the standards as questions to put to each element.[^s1] Paul and Elder present this as a tool for assessing one's own thinking first, since the obstacles they stress are egocentric and sociocentric tendencies: the pull to favour one's own or one's group's views.[^s1][^s2] A frequent pitfall, noted by Elder, is that teachers adopt the elements but skip the standards, which defeats the framework's purpose.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

The framework is normative: it sets out what good thinking looks like rather than describing a tested psychological mechanism. Evidence on its use in teaching is encouraging but limited. At the University of Louisville, engineering faculty used it to design assignments and a four-point holistic rubric. Among 49 students who completed four years, mean rubric scores rose from about 2.6 as freshmen to 3.15 as seniors.[^s4] A 2015 follow-up reported significant gains in all three cohorts tracked.[^s5] These were descriptive studies without control groups, with heavy attrition (26% of consenting students finished) and uneven agreement between raters, so they cannot isolate the framework's effect.[^s4][^s5] Elder herself suggests Paul may have overestimated how readily people can be changed into critical thinkers.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Broader: critical thinking.** It is one of the best-known operational models of the general concept.[^s1]
- **Introduced by Richard Paul and Linda Elder.** Paul's earlier work supplied the standards and the strong-sense idea; Elder co-developed and co-published the framework.[^s2][^s1]
- **Applied in critical-thinking education and engineering education.** The University of Louisville presents it to students as its critical-thinking model, and its engineering school built a four-year curriculum and rubric on it.[^s3][^s4][^s5]
- **Counteracts myside bias.** Its strong-sense commitment to fair-mindedness and dialectical weighing of rival views targets the tendency to reason in favour of one's own side.[^s6]
- **Related to the Socratic method, the Delphi Report, thinking dispositions and metacognition.** Its standards supply Socratic questions, its traits overlap the Delphi dispositions, and its core activity is thinking about one's own thinking.[^s1][^s2]
