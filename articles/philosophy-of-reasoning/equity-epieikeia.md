# Equity (epieikeia)

Equity (Greek *epieikeia*) is the principle that a general rule, because it is written for the ordinary run of cases, must sometimes be corrected when it meets a case its author did not foresee, and that the correction should follow what the rule-maker would have said had they been present.[^s1] It is the positive counterpart of a familiar reasoning error: where the fallacy of accident presses a sound general rule onto an exceptional case, equity asks whether the case falls within the rule's purpose before applying it.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Aristotle gives the classic account in Book V, chapter 10 of the *Nicomachean Ethics*. Every law is stated in general terms, and some matters cannot be stated correctly in general terms, so the lawgiver legislates for what holds in the majority of cases.[^s1] When a case arises that the general wording handles badly, the fault lies neither in the law nor in the lawgiver but in the nature of practical affairs, and equity is the amendment that makes good the gap.[^s1] He compares it to the flexible leaden ruler used by masons on Lesbos, which bends to the shape of each stone: a variable subject needs a variable rule.[^s1] He also describes the equitable person as one who does not press their legal rights to the harm of others and will accept less than their due.[^s1]

Thomas Aquinas carried the idea into medieval moral theology as *epikeia*, treating it as a virtue and a part of justice.[^s2] His examples became standard: deposits should normally be returned, but not a sword deposited by someone who now demands it in a fit of madness, or money reclaimed in order to make war on one's own country.[^s2]

## How it works

The reasoning has three moves. First, identify the rule and the goal it serves. Second, ask whether the present case is one the rule's authors had in view, or whether something unusual about it means that literal application would defeat that goal. Third, if it would, decide as a reasonable author of the rule would have decided for this case.[^s1][^s3]

A workplace example: a lab policy says no visitors may enter after 18:00, to protect experiments and staff. At 19:00 a colleague collapses and the guard hesitates to admit paramedics. Read literally, the rule bars them; read equitably, a safety rule was never meant to keep out emergency help, so the guard should let them in. Aquinas would add that this case is manifest, so it needs execution rather than interpretation.[^s2]

## How to apply it

- State the rule's purpose before checking its wording; a rule can only be corrected against the goal it serves.[^s3]
- Look for explicit or implicit qualifiers. Archie's logic course notes that practical rules are usually meant as "as a rule" or "other things being equal" claims, so treating them as exceptionless is the error.[^s4]
- Reserve equity for clear cases. Aquinas holds that departing from the letter is justified when harm is evident, while doubtful cases should go to whoever has authority to interpret the rule.[^s2]
- Be ready to give up a legal or procedural entitlement when insisting on it would harm others; Aristotle treats this as part of equity, not an extra.[^s1][^s3]

**Pitfalls.** Equity can be abused as a licence to ignore any inconvenient rule. Beever argues that Aristotle did not mean equity as justice rebelling against law; it is a way of reading a rule by its spirit and its author's intent, so it gives no warrant to substitute one's own preferences.[^s3] Where predictability matters more than case-by-case fit, a deliberately rigid rule may be the better design.

## Evidence and limitations

Equity is a normative principle, not an empirical finding. Its main scholarly debate concerns scope. Beever reads Aristotelian equity as a principle of interpretation aimed at the legislator's intent, and argues it addresses ordinary citizens as well as judges; he contrasts this with readings that make it a corrective that overrides law in the name of justice.[^s3] He also draws on Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, where a man wearing only a ring who strikes someone is guilty under the words of a law against wounding with iron but innocent in reality.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Aristotle in the *Nicomachean Ethics*:** Book V, chapter 10 is the founding text.[^s1]
- **Counteracts the fallacy of accident:** Aquinas holds that following the letter of the law where it should not be followed is wrong, because applying a rule framed for common cases to an exceptional one defeats justice; that misapplication is what logicians call accident, defined by Jevons as arguing from a general rule to a special case.[^s2][^s4]
- **Studied in jurisprudence and applied in legal reasoning:** Beever's analysis appears in *Legal Theory* and treats equity as a principle of statutory interpretation.[^s3]
- **Broader and related:** equity sits within defeasible reasoning, because it treats practical rules as holding other things being equal rather than without exception.[^s4] It reads a rule by its author's intent rather than its bare wording, which makes it a cousin of the principle of charity, and it stands in tension with bright-line rules, whose value lies in not bending to the case.[^s3]
