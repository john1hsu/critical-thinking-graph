# Socratic method

The Socratic method is inquiry by question and answer. Instead of telling someone they are wrong, the questioner asks them to state what they believe, then asks further questions that draw out what else they accept, until the original claim is seen to clash with those other commitments or to rest on reasons that do not hold.[^s5][^s2] In its modern teaching form it means giving learners questions rather than answers, so that they practise probing their own thinking.[^s4]

## Origin

The method is named after Socrates of Athens, who wrote nothing; what we know of his questioning comes mostly from Plato's dialogues, with Xenophon and Aristotle as further witnesses.[^s5] In the early dialogues such as the *Euthyphro*, *Laches* and *Charmides*, Socrates professes ignorance, asks a confident speaker what some virtue is, and shows through agreed premises that the answers do not fit together, so each definition is withdrawn and the question is left open.[^s5] Britannica notes that scholars disagree over how faithfully Plato reports the historical Socrates, and that these inconclusive early dialogues are usually taken as the closest picture of him.[^s5]

Twentieth-century scholarship, led by Gregory Vlastos, named this pattern "the elenchus" (Greek for refutation or testing), following Richard Robinson, and treated it as Socrates' standard method: a "What is X?" question, an initial answer, a demonstration that the answer conflicts with the speaker's other premises, and the abandonment of the answer.[^s2] A 2002 collection edited by Gary Alan Scott challenged that picture; its contributors argue that Socrates used several kinds of refutation fitted to the character of each interlocutor, and that the dialogues also aim to exhort people towards philosophy rather than only to test definitions.[^s2][^s3]

## How it works

Take a workplace case. A team lead says, "Our best people are the ones who stay late." A Socratic colleague asks what "best" means here; the lead says, "the ones who deliver the most value." The colleague asks whether someone could deliver great value in fewer hours, and the lead agrees that this is possible. A further question follows: does the lead also think long hours can signal poor planning? Again the lead agrees. Now the original claim conflicts with two things the lead accepts, so it has to be revised, perhaps to "visible effort is easy to notice, but it is not the same as value." Nobody has been told the answer; the inconsistency came from the lead's own commitments, as in Socrates' exchange with Euthyphro.[^s5]

That is the core move Vlastos described.[^s2] Note what it does and does not show. The exchange proves that the lead's beliefs are inconsistent, not which belief is false; the lead could in principle give up one of the other premises instead. Scholars call this the "problem of the elenchus": the first claim is shown to clash with other premises rather than strictly refuted.[^s2] Hugh Benson accordingly holds that the method tests consistency rather than reveals truth, while Mark McPherran argues it can still support moral conclusions when a belief survives repeated testing.[^s3] Benson also stresses a sincerity condition: the respondent must say what they actually believe, or the refutation proves nothing about them.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Paul and Elder present Socratic questioning as the outward form of the inner critical voice a good thinker develops.[^s4] Their checklist of what to probe gives a usable sequence:[^s4]

1. **Purpose and question.** What are we trying to decide, and what exactly is being asked?
2. **Information.** What data bear on this, and is it actually available?
3. **Concepts.** What do the key terms mean as used here?
4. **Assumptions.** What is being taken for granted?
5. **Interpretations and viewpoints.** How else could the data be read, and who would see it differently?
6. **Implications.** If this is true, what follows, and do we accept that?[^s4]

Pitfalls: questioning can become a trap rather than a joint inquiry, which is why the respondent's sincere agreement at each step matters.[^s3] Because a clash only shows that something must give, finish by asking which commitment is weakest instead of assuming the opening claim is the one to drop.[^s2]

## Evidence

Direct tests of the method are scarce. In a 2015 study of 55 adults receiving cognitive therapy for depression, sessions in which a therapist used more Socratic questioning than usual for that patient were followed by larger drops in depressive symptoms at the next session, an effect that held after controlling for therapeutic alliance.[^s1] The authors caution that the design was observational, covered only early sessions of one therapy and could not rule out unmeasured confounders, so it does not establish that the questioning caused the improvement.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Broader: dialectic.** The method is a form of reasoned dialogue in which claims are tested by an interlocutor's questions.[^s2]
- **Introduced by Socrates**, whose questioning in Plato's dialogues gives the method its name and model.[^s5]
- **Related: Plato**, the main source for Socrates' practice, and **epistemology**, since the early dialogues ask what knowing a virtue would require.[^s5]
- **Related: method of counterexample.** Refuting a proposed definition by a case it gets wrong is one of the standard Socratic moves.[^s5][^s2]
- **Applied in critical-thinking education**, where Paul and Elder treat Socratic teaching as the oldest and still most powerful way to cultivate critical thinking.[^s4]
- **Applied in cognitive-behavioural therapy**, where therapists use graded questions to help patients re-examine key beliefs.[^s1]
