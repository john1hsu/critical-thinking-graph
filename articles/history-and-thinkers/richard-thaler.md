# Richard H. Thaler

Richard H. Thaler is an American economist who spent his career showing that real people depart from the fully rational, selfish agent of standard theory in ways that are systematic enough to model. He is the Charles R. Walgreen Distinguished Service Professor of Behavioral Science and Economics at Chicago Booth, where he has taught since 1995, and he served as president of the American Economic Association in 2015.[^s2] The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded him the 2017 economics prize for giving the field "conceptual and empirical foundations" by bringing psychology into economic analysis.[^s1]

## Origin

Thaler earned his PhD at the University of Rochester in 1974; his thesis included an early wage-risk estimate of the value of a statistical life, later published with his adviser Sherwin Rosen.[^s1][^s2] He then taught at Cornell, which its economics department now describes as the birthplace of behavioral economics in the 1980s. There he taught managerial decision making, helped create a behavioral economics and decision research center in 1989, and helped launch a recurring conference on behavioral decision research in management.[^s3] His 1980 article "Toward a positive theory of consumer choice", published in the first issue of the *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*, set out his programme of describing how consumers actually choose rather than how an ideal agent should.[^s4][^s1] Cornell's account notes the paper was hard to place because the approach was then seen as fringe, and treats it as the field's starting point.[^s3] The Nobel committee notes he was the first economist to apply Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory to economic questions.[^s1]

## How it works

Thaler's method was to collect "anomalies": cases where a factor that standard theory treats as irrelevant reliably changes behavior. His *Journal of Economic Perspectives* column of that name grouped them under bounded rationality, self-control problems and social preferences.[^s1] In his 2017 Nobel lecture he called these "supposedly irrelevant factors" and opened with an anecdote: dinner guests, all economists, were relieved when he removed a bowl of cashews, even though theory says more options cannot hurt.[^s5]

Several of his ideas are now standard tools. He coined the **endowment effect** and explained the gap between what people will pay for a good and what they demand to give it up as loss aversion; in the 1990 mug and pen markets with Kahneman and Knetsch, median selling prices were roughly twice median buying prices and trading did not close the gap.[^s1] **Mental accounting** describes how people sort money into separate budgets with their own reference points, so a windfall and a salary are not treated as interchangeable.[^s1] The **planner-doer** model (with Shefrin, 1981) pictures a farsighted planner and a myopic doer inside one person.[^s1] With Shlomo Benartzi he designed Save More Tomorrow, in which employees commit in advance to raise retirement contributions at each future pay rise.[^s1] With Cass Sunstein he proposed **libertarian paternalism** in 2003 and popularized it in *Nudge* (2008): design the default and the presentation of options so that people do better by their own lights, while leaving them free to choose otherwise.[^s1][^s2]

A workplace example: a manager setting up a training sign-up can make attendance the default and let people opt out, instead of asking them to opt in. This mirrors the automatic-enrollment and opt-out features the committee highlights in Save More Tomorrow and retirement-plan defaults.[^s1] Thaler's lesson for a critical thinker is to ask, before trusting a prediction, whether the model assumes people ignore defaults, framing or timing.

## Evidence and limitations

Much of the evidence the Nobel committee cites has held up: a 2014 meta-analysis of 76 studies found willingness to accept exceeded willingness to pay by a geometric-mean factor of about 3.3, and automatic enrollment raised one firm's 401(k) participation from 49% to 86%.[^s1] The committee also records critiques. List (2004) found no endowment effect among experienced dealers, and Morewedge and colleagues (2009) attributed it to ownership rather than loss aversion.[^s1] Sugden and coauthors argue that libertarian paternalism wrongly assumes policymakers can identify people's true preferences, and Arad and Rubinstein report that some people resent being steered.[^s1] Thaler's results on stock-market overreaction, which made him a founder of behavioral finance alongside Robert Shiller, remain open to a risk-premium interpretation.[^s1]

## Connections

Thaler **introduced** behavioral economics in the sense that Cornell and later accounts credit him with founding it, though the committee frames his role as supplying its foundations.[^s3][^s1] He **introduced** the endowment effect, which he named, and libertarian paternalism, which he co-proposed with Sunstein.[^s1] He **authored** "Toward a positive theory of consumer choice" (1980).[^s4] He was **influenced by** prospect theory and by Daniel Kahneman, his frequent collaborator on fairness and endowment-effect experiments.[^s1] His work builds on loss aversion, underlies choice architecture and default-based policies such as automatic enrollment, and is defined against expected utility theory, whose predictions his anomalies contradicted.[^s1]
