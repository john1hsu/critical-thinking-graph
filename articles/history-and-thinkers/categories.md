# Categories

The *Categories* is a brief treatise attributed to Aristotle that stands first among his logical works.[^s3] It does two things a critical thinker still uses. Its opening lines give the first technical vocabulary for a name that applies to different things under different definitions, the idea later called equivocation or ambiguity.[^s3][^s5] Its body then sorts the simplest things we can say into ten kinds, starting with substance.[^s1][^s2] Studtmann calls it one of the most important works in philosophy.[^s2]

## Origin

The work dates from the fourth century BCE, and many scholars treat it as early, though both its date and its authorship have been questioned.[^s2] Grote, writing in 1872, reports that Prantl and Bonitz doubted it was genuine but finds their case unconvincing, adding that the ten-category doctrine is Aristotelian either way.[^s3] When Andronicus edited Aristotle's works in the first century BCE, he put the logical treatises first and the *Categories* at their head, the order the *Organon* has kept since.[^s3]

The opening distinction has roots in the Academy. Ward notes that Speusippus also classified names, and that Plato's views on naming shaped what Aristotle rejected.[^s4]

## How it works

**Ways of sharing a name (chapter 1).** Aristotle separates three cases.[^s1] Things are homonymous, or equivocally named, when they share a name but the definition that goes with the name differs. His example is a real man and a figure in a picture, both called "animal".[^s1] Things are synonymous, or univocally named, when name and definition both match, as a man and an ox are both animals.[^s1] Things are paronymous when one name derives from another by a change of ending, as "grammarian" from "grammar".[^s1] Grote credits Aristotle with giving these words their technical sense.[^s3] Ancient commentators argued the distinction had to come first, because readers could not follow the rest without it.[^s3]

Strictly, Aristotle classifies things by how a name applies to them, not words by how many meanings they carry. Ward and other scholars debate how linguistic this makes the doctrine.[^s4] Evans, reviewing Ward, simply treats homonymy as Aristotle's preferred name for ambiguity.[^s5] In the *Topics*, Aristotle turns it into a working test, using it to refine terms and detect ambiguity in dialectical debate.[^s4] Shields traces how homonymy grew from this first sketch into a critical tool used against Plato.[^s6]

**Saying and being in (chapter 2).** Expressions are either combined, such as "the man runs", or simple, such as "man" or "runs".[^s1] Some things are said of a subject, as "man" is said of an individual man. Others are present in a subject, as a piece of knowledge is in a mind.[^s1] Crossing the two yields four kinds of entity, from individual substances to general qualities.[^s2]

**The ten categories (chapter 4).** Each simple expression signifies substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action or affection: "horse", "two cubits long", "white", "double", "in the Lyceum", "yesterday", "sitting", "armed", "to lance", "to be lanced".[^s1] Primary substance, such as an individual man or horse, is neither said of nor present in anything, and everything else depends on it.[^s1][^s2]

**A workplace use.** A planning meeting hears that "the project is healthy" from both the finance lead and the engineering lead. Chapter 1 prompts the check: same name, but is the definition the same? If finance means under budget and engineering means few open defects, the two reports are homonymous, and agreeing that the project is healthy settles nothing.[^s1][^s5] The category list prompts a second check: one claim concerns a quantity (spend), the other a quality (code condition), so they cannot simply be averaged.[^s1]

## Influence and limits

Commentary began early and ran through Porphyry, Dexippus and Simplicius.[^s3] Porphyry's *Isagoge*, with its five predicables, came to be paired with the ten categories, and sixteenth-century Italian schools still lectured on the two together.[^s3] Later engagement, Studtmann notes, includes Plotinus, Aquinas and Ockham; Kant criticised the list as unsystematic.[^s2] The final chapters, on opposites, priority and motion, are largely superseded by the *Metaphysics*.[^s2] Scholars also dispute how Aristotle arrived at the ten categories and whether the work is chiefly logic or metaphysics.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

- **Authored by Aristotle; part of the Organon.** Andronicus's edition placed it first among the six logical works.[^s3]
- **Introduces ambiguity (as a technical notion).** Its homonymous/synonymous distinction is the source of the terms equivocal and univocal; Evans reads homonymy as Aristotle's word for ambiguity.[^s3][^s5]
- **Influenced Porphyry.** He commented on the work, and his *Isagoge* was taught alongside it.[^s3]
- **Influenced William of Ockham.** Ockham is among the medieval philosophers who worked through its scheme.[^s2]
- **Related to the Topics.** The *Topics* applies homonymy as a working test for ambiguity in debate.[^s4]
