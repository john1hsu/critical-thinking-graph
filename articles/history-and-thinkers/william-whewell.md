# William Whewell

William Whewell (1794-1866) was an English polymath, historian and philosopher of science who spent nearly his whole career at Trinity College, Cambridge, and served as its Master from 1841 until his death.[^s1][^s4] He is the reason the English language has the word "scientist", but his lasting contribution to critical thinking is a theory of how good scientific inferences are made and tested, drawn from a detailed study of the history of the sciences.[^s1] Two of his tests, successful prediction of new kinds of phenomena and the "consilience of inductions", remain standard ideas about what makes a theory worth believing.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Whewell was born in Lancaster on 24 May 1794, the eldest son of a carpenter, and reached Trinity College through grammar-school scholarships; he graduated second wrangler in 1816 and became a fellow the next year.[^s1][^s2] He held the Cambridge chairs of mineralogy (1828-1832) and moral philosophy (from 1838), was twice Vice-Chancellor, and helped found the British Association for the Advancement of Science.[^s1][^s4] His scientific work was mainly on tides: between 1833 and 1850 he wrote fourteen major papers on "tidology" and in 1835 coordinated simultaneous tide readings at more than 650 stations in nine countries, earning the Royal Society's Royal Medal in 1838.[^s2]

His reasoning ideas appeared in the *History of the Inductive Sciences* (1837) and *The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences, Founded upon Their History* (1840), later expanded into three works including the *Novum Organon Renovatum* (1858), a deliberate update of Francis Bacon's 1620 treatise on induction.[^s1][^s4] In 1833, answering a challenge from the poet Coleridge, he proposed "scientist" as a name for practitioners then called natural philosophers or men of science; he also supplied "anode", "cathode" and "ion" at Faraday's request.[^s1][^s2] He died in Cambridge on 6 March 1866 after a riding accident.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

Whewell held that all knowledge has two sides: facts supplied by the senses and ideas supplied by the mind, such as space, time and cause.[^s1] Induction, on his account, is not simply piling up observations. It is the act of "colligating" known facts by finding the right conception that binds them into a general law. His favourite case was Kepler: Tycho Brahe had the same observations of Mars, but only Kepler, by applying the conception of an ellipse, saw the orbit in them.[^s1] Discovery therefore often comes from a better idea rather than from new data.[^s1]

He then proposed tests for whether a colligation is true. A good theory should predict facts of a kind not used to build it; he valued such forecasts above explaining what was already known.[^s1] Stronger still is consilience, when a theory built for one class of facts turns out to explain a quite different class, as Newton's gravitation explained both falling bodies and planetary orbits.[^s1] Finally, a true theory grows more coherent and simpler as it extends, whereas a false one needs ever more patches; phlogiston theory failed this test when chemists weighed their products.[^s1]

A workplace example: an analyst explains a sales dip in one region by a competitor's new pricing. Whewell's tests ask more than whether the story fits the dip. Does it predict something not yet checked, such as the dip being largest in the product lines the competitor discounted? Does it also explain an unrelated pattern, such as a spike in price-match requests to customer service? If the analyst must keep adding special exceptions to save the story, it is failing the coherence test.[^s1]

## Reception and limitations

Whewell lost the nineteenth-century argument to John Stuart Mill, whose *System of Logic* attacked his account of induction; the two also disputed morality and political economy, and Whewell's influence was eclipsed for decades.[^s1][^s4] Twentieth-century critics of logical positivism revived him, and several read him as an early anti-inductivist who anticipated Popper's method of conjecture and refutation.[^s1] Laura Snyder's encyclopedia entry argues that reading is mistaken, since Whewell explicitly rejected treating discovery as mere guesswork; this remains a scholarly dispute about interpretation.[^s1]

Consilience itself has been examined critically. Menachem Fisch argued in 1985 that later writers use the term more loosely than Whewell did, distinguished two kinds of consilient process, and concluded that the stronger kind gives a theory unusually high support, while also exposing gaps in standard Bayesian theories of confirmation.[^s3] A further limitation is that Whewell grounded the necessity of the laws of nature in divine ideas, a theological commitment that few modern readers share.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Introduced the consilience of inductions**, the test that a theory earns special credit when it unexpectedly explains a different class of facts.[^s1][^s3]
- **Authored** *The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences* (1840) and the *History of the Inductive Sciences* (1837), the works in which his theory of induction was set out and grounded in history.[^s1][^s2]
- **Contrasts with John Stuart Mill**, his opponent in the century's main debate over what induction is and how it should be justified.[^s1]
- **Studied in philosophy of science**, where his account of consilience is still evaluated against Bayesian confirmation theory.[^s3]
- **Related:** his emphasis on novel prediction foreshadows **hypothesis testing** as stated earlier by **Christiaan Huygens**; he is often compared, contestedly, with **Karl Popper** and **falsifiability**; his coherence test is a warning against the **ad hoc hypothesis**; and his work is a theory of **inductive reasoning**.[^s1]
