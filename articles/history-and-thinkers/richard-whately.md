# Richard Whately

Richard Whately (1787-1863) was an English clergyman, logician and economist who ended his career as the Anglican archbishop of Dublin.[^s1] Two of his textbooks shaped how English speakers were taught to reason for most of the nineteenth century. *Elements of Logic* (1826) became the standard logic text for several generations of students, and *Elements of Rhetoric* (1828) supplied the account of presumption and burden of proof that debaters and argumentation theorists still start from.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Whately was born in London on 1 February 1787, studied at Oriel College, Oxford, became a fellow there and took holy orders.[^s1][^s2] He was made principal of St Alban Hall in 1825, professor of political economy at Oxford in 1829, and archbishop of Dublin in 1831, where he died on 8 October 1863.[^s1] His first widely read work, *Historic Doubts Relative to Napoleon Buonaparte* (1819), was a satire: it applied David Hume's sceptical test for miracle reports to the well-attested career of Napoleon to show that the same standard would make doubt of Napoleon's existence reasonable too.[^s1][^s2]

*Elements of Logic*, which the young John Henry Newman helped edit, was written against what Whately saw as Locke's deadening influence on Oxford logic.[^s3] Its content was deliberately conservative. It followed Henry Aldrich's 1691 Latin textbook, *Artis Logicae Compendium*, reduced arguments to syllogisms and confined logic to deduction.[^s3][^s2] Its achievement lay in its clear style and its defence of logic as a study of language that applies to every subject, and Augustus De Morgan later credited Whately with restoring logical study in England.[^s2][^s3]

## How it works

**Fallacies.** Whately split fallacies into two families. In a *logical* fallacy the conclusion does not follow from the premises. In a *material* fallacy it does follow, but something else has gone wrong: either a premise is unduly assumed, as in begging the question, or the argument proves something other than the point at issue (*ignoratio elenchi*), a group in which he placed the ad hominem, the appeal to authority and shifting ground.[^s5] He admitted that sorting real errors into these bins was partly a matter of choice, because the listener must supply a suppressed premise that is either false or fails to prove the conclusion. Mill quoted this explanation with approval, while later logicians objected to several of its placements.[^s5]

**Presumption and burden of proof.** In the *Rhetoric*, Whately described a presumption as a pre-occupation of the ground that stands until enough is said against it, not as a claim that one view is more probable.[^s4] Whoever disputes the presumed position carries the burden of proof, and for Whately the burden shifts only when the challenger raises a counter-presumption.[^s4] His examples include presumptions of innocence, in favour of existing institutions and against paradoxical or novel claims.[^s4] Recognised experts also receive deference, though he set against this a counter-presumption that the learned overrate their own authority.[^s4][^s6]

A workplace example shows the idea. When a team member proposes replacing a long-standing review process, the current process occupies the ground, so the proposer must make the case. If a colleague then shows that the current process has missed three serious defects this year, that evidence raises a counter-presumption, and defenders of the status quo now have to answer it.[^s4]

**Authority.** Hansen notes that the two books treat authority differently. The *Logic* gives the appeal to authority a brief, dismissive treatment, while the *Rhetoric* develops a positive account in which authority creates a defeasible presumption and in which epistemic authority is distinguished from administrative authority, features that anticipate modern accounts of the argument from expert opinion.[^s6]

## Limitations

Whately's formal logic added little that was new, and the developments of Boole, De Morgan and Mill owe it few direct debts.[^s2] His theory of presumption relies on common sense to decide which side holds the ground; critics read it as psychological rather than legal or epistemic, and Hohmann disputes the common claim that it simply mirrors legal practice.[^s4] Hansen also argues that Whately sometimes blurred a burden of proof with a weaker burden of rejoinder.[^s6]

## Connections

Whately is often cast as the source of the rhetorical **burden of proof** and **presumption**: later theorists such as Sidgwick, Rescher, Walton and Hansen all define their positions in relation to his account, even though the legal doctrine is older.[^s4] He **authored** *Elements of Logic*, *Elements of Rhetoric* and *Historic Doubts Relative to Napoleon Buonaparte*.[^s1] His logic was **influenced by** Aldrich's *Artis Logicae Compendium*, which it largely follows.[^s3] His division of fallacies is an ancestor of the modern study of the **informal fallacy**, and his treatment of authority foreshadows the **argument from expert opinion** studied in informal logic.[^s5][^s6]
