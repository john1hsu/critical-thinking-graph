# James Frederick Ferrier

James Frederick Ferrier (1808-1864) was a Scottish metaphysician who held the chair of moral philosophy and political economy at the University of St Andrews from 1845 until his death.[^s3] He is remembered today mainly for a word: he introduced "epistemology" into English as the name for the theory of knowing, in his *Institutes of Metaphysic* (1854).[^s1][^s3] The Keefe entry in the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls him arguably the best Scottish philosopher of his generation, though his idealism found few followers in his lifetime.[^s1]

## Origin

Ferrier was born in Edinburgh, studied at Edinburgh University and Magdalen College, Oxford, and qualified for the Scottish bar in 1832 before turning to philosophy.[^s1][^s3] Sir William Hamilton, the leading Scottish philosopher of the day, became his friend and mentor and may have prompted his 1834 visit to Heidelberg to study German idealism.[^s3] Ferrier first set out his views in a series of long *Blackwood's Magazine* essays from 1838, beginning with "An Introduction to the Philosophy of Consciousness", then in essays defending Berkeley and attacking Thomas Reid's common-sense philosophy.[^s1][^s4] He taught civil history at Edinburgh (1842) and covered Hamilton's logic and metaphysics class (1844-1845) before moving to St Andrews; later bids for Edinburgh chairs, in 1852 and 1856, failed, partly because his metaphysics was seen as un-Scottish.[^s1]

## How it works

The *Institutes of Metaphysic: The Theory of Knowing and Being* was published by Blackwood in Edinburgh in 1854; the library record of the Toronto copy notes that the term "epistemology" first appears in it on page 46.[^s2] The book is built like Euclid's geometry: it starts from one axiom and proves numbered propositions, each followed by a stated counter-proposition that is then refuted.[^s1] It has three parts: an Epistemology, or theory of knowing (22 propositions); an Agnoiology, or theory of ignorance (8 propositions); and an Ontology, or theory of being (11 propositions).[^s1]

Its central claims, in brief:

- **Knowing includes self-knowing.** Whatever we know, we also know ourselves as knowing it.[^s1]
- **Subject and object come together.** The smallest unit of knowledge is object-plus-subject; splitting them, as Ferrier thought Reid did, leads to error.[^s1][^s3]
- **Ignorance is only of the knowable.** To be ignorant is to lack knowledge one could have. What is contradictory, such as a Kantian thing-in-itself known by no mind, cannot be known, so it is not something we are ignorant of either.[^s1][^s3]

The ignorance thesis translates into a useful habit when a team lists its "unknowns". Suppose a project review asks, "What don't we know about why customers cancel?" Ferrier's distinction separates gaps that evidence could close (we have not asked churned customers) from questions posed so that no answer could count, which should be reframed rather than researched; on his account only the first kind is real ignorance.[^s1] This is an illustration of his idea, not his own example.

## Influence and limitations

Ferrier's term outlived his system. "Epistemology" became the standard English name for the study of knowledge and justification, while his idealism was linked by critics to Hegel and Spinoza and suspected of pantheism, charges he rejected.[^s1] George Davie's assessment in the Macmillan *Encyclopedia of Philosophy* is that the *Institutes* shaped later British thinkers such as Shadworth Hodgson, and that his early essays on self-consciousness, which anticipate twentieth-century phenomenology, were overlooked until a modern reappraisal.[^s4] Keefe notes that contemporary epistemologists still pay him little attention, and that his attacks on Reid, though sometimes sharp, slid at times into ad hominem remarks.[^s1] Sources also differ on small details: the IEP text gives 1863 for his death, while Britannica gives 11 June 1864.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced epistemology (the name).** Both the IEP and Britannica credit Ferrier with first using "epistemology" in English for the theory of knowledge, in the *Institutes* of 1854.[^s1][^s3]
- **Introduced agnoiology.** Britannica describes him as known for his theory of agnoiology, the second part of the *Institutes*, which treats ignorance as a topic in its own right.[^s3]
- **Influenced by George Berkeley.** Keefe identifies Berkeley as the most important influence on Ferrier, who defended Berkeley's idealism against the charge of simply denying matter.[^s1]
- **Influenced by William Hamilton.** Hamilton's circle drew Ferrier into philosophy and toward German idealism, even though Ferrier later rejected Hamilton's blend of Reid and Kant.[^s3][^s1]
