# John Buridan

John Buridan (Jean Buridan, c. 1300-c. 1360) was a philosopher who spent his whole career teaching in the arts faculty of the University of Paris. Henrik Lagerlund's Stanford Encyclopedia entry calls him the foremost logician of the later Middle Ages.[^s1] For critical thinkers, his lasting contribution is a mechanical test for syllogisms. It says which terms a statement "distributes", meaning which terms it makes a claim about every member of. It then rejects any pair of premises whose middle term is never distributed. A premise pair that fails it commits what is now called the fallacy of the undistributed middle.[^s1]

## Origin

Buridan was born before 1300 near Béthune in Picardy. He took his master's degree and teaching licence at Paris by the mid-1320s and served twice as rector of the university, in 1328 and 1340.[^s2][^s4] Unusually for a major medieval thinker, he never moved on to the theology faculty. He was a secular cleric, not a friar, and wrote mainly commentaries on Aristotle and textbooks of logic.[^s2] His *Summulae de dialectica* began as a commentary on Peter of Spain's standard logic manual. Buridan replaced passages he disagreed with until it became an independent and much longer textbook built around his own nominalist semantics.[^s2][^s3] His separate *Treatise on Consequences* sets out his account of what makes an inference formally valid.[^s1][^s3] Both works were taught widely in the new universities of central Europe, including Vienna, Prague and Kraków.[^s2][^s3]

## How it works

Buridan's key move was to treat the syllogism as one kind of *consequence*, an inference from an antecedent to a consequent. A syllogism is a consequence whose antecedent joins two premises. It is formally valid when every uniform substitution of its content terms keeps it truth-preserving.[^s1] He grounded validity in supposition, the medieval theory of reference. Whether a syllogism works depends on whether its terms stand for the same things across the premises.[^s1]

From that semantics he drew compact rules of distribution. Universal statements distribute their subjects, negative statements distribute their predicates, and no other terms are distributed. So "All A are B" distributes only A, "No A are B" distributes both terms, "Some A are B" distributes neither, and "Some A are not B" distributes only B.[^s1] A pair of premises can yield a conclusion only if the middle term, the one shared by both premises, is distributed at least once. Buridan ran through all sixteen combinations of premise types figure by figure. He rejected pairs with two negative premises, rejected two particular affirmatives, and struck out further pairs in each figure specifically because the middle was undistributed.[^s1]

A workplace case shows the test at work. "Some contractors hold security clearance. Some people with clearance are auditors. So some contractors are auditors." Both premises are particular affirmatives, so neither distributes the middle term "people with clearance". The contractors and the auditors could be entirely different subgroups of the cleared staff. Buridan's rules reject this pair before anyone looks at the content. That is what makes the rules useful: they catch the error even when the conclusion happens to sound plausible.[^s1]

He also extended the syllogism in ways later textbooks dropped. By counting indirect conclusions and an unusual negative idiom, he recognized 33 valid moods against Aristotle's 19. He worked out temporal and modal syllogisms as well, including an "octagon of opposition" for modal statements.[^s1]

## Legacy and limitations

Lagerlund's account holds that medieval syllogistic peaked with Buridan, and that his Paris successors, such as Albert of Saxony and Marsilius of Inghen, added little of substance to it.[^s1] His systematic approach was taken up by later teachers: Jodocus Trutfetter based his logic wholly on Buridan, and Renaissance Aristotelians such as George of Brussels drew on his methods.[^s1] The theory has critics. Geach attacked Buridan's theory of distribution in 1962, and King defended it in 1985. Lagerlund's account notes that some of Buridan's apparent gains, such as the extra moods, reduce to standard moods by swapping the premises.[^s1]

Buridan is better known outside logic for two things. One is his impetus theory of motion, which explained why projectiles keep moving after they leave the hand.[^s3][^s4] The other is "Buridan's ass", the animal that starves between two equally attractive piles of food. Britannica traces the dilemma to his commentary on Aristotle's *On the Heavens*, where the animal is a dog.[^s4] Zupko notes that the donkey version does not appear in his writings and was probably a later parody of his view that the will can postpone choosing.[^s2] Many colourful stories about his life, such as being thrown into the Seine in a sack, cannot be verified.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced the undistributed-middle test.** Lagerlund credits Buridan with requiring the middle term to be distributed and rejecting premise pairs on exactly that ground. The *name* of the fallacy is a later textbook label, which is why this edge is marked medium confidence.[^s1]
- **Authored the *Summulae de dialectica*.** It began as a commentary on Peter of Spain and grew into Buridan's own logic textbook.[^s2]
- **Authored the *Treatise on Consequences*.** It is the main source for his syllogistic and his account of formal validity.[^s1]
- **Influenced by Aristotle.** Most of his output consists of commentaries on Aristotle's works, and he reworked Aristotle's syllogistic.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Peter of Spain.** His *Summulae* took Peter's *Summulae logicales* as its starting text.[^s3]
- **Related to distribution of terms, the rules of the categorical syllogism and the square of opposition.** His five distribution rules are mapped onto the four statement types of the square, and they underlie the later rule lists.[^s1]
