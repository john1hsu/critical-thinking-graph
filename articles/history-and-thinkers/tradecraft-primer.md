# A Tradecraft Primer

*A Tradecraft Primer: Structured Analytic Techniques for Improving Intelligence Analysis* is a short unclassified handbook released by the US Government in March 2009 through the CIA's Center for the Study of Intelligence.[^s1] It sets out a dozen structured analytic techniques meant to help analysts challenge their own judgments, expose mind-sets, stimulate creativity and manage uncertainty, and it says plainly that it is not a complete guide to intelligence analysis.[^s1] It is one of the standard references on such techniques: a 2016 RAND review of their use across the US intelligence community took the *Primer*'s taxonomy as its starting point.[^s2]

## Origin

The *Primer* belongs to the wave of reform that followed the September 11 attacks and the flawed 2002 estimate on Iraqi weapons of mass destruction. Congress's 2004 Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act required analytic tradecraft standards, and the Director of National Intelligence later issued Intelligence Community Directive 203 to define them.[^s5] Mandel and Tetlock trace the spread of structured techniques as the community's main defence against judgment error to the same act and directive.[^s3] The RAND review notes that use of such techniques grew after the Iraq failure.[^s2]

The 2009 text was not the first version. Academic reference lists cite a 2005 edition under the same title, attributed to the CIA's Sherman Kent School for Intelligence Analysis.[^s4] The *Primer* does not name its drafters. Its account of how analysts think draws heavily on Richards Heuer's *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, and it closes its discussion of bias by quoting Heuer's call for analysts to be self-conscious about their reasoning.[^s1]

## How it works

The *Primer* opens with the problem the techniques are meant to solve. Analysts work through mental models, or mind-sets, which decide what information they notice and remember. Mind-sets form quickly and resist change. New data tends to be absorbed into the existing model, and conflicting data tends to be discounted. Experienced analysts can be the most exposed, because their expertise rests on models that have worked before.[^s1] It then lists common biases in four groups: in perception, in evaluating evidence, in estimating probabilities (availability, anchoring, overconfidence), and in perceiving causes.[^s1]

The techniques fall into three families.[^s1][^s2]

- **Diagnostic** techniques make arguments, assumptions and gaps visible: the key assumptions check, the quality-of-information check, indicators or signposts of change, and analysis of competing hypotheses.[^s1]
- **Contrarian** techniques challenge the current line: devil's advocacy, Team A/Team B, high-impact/low-probability analysis and "what if?" analysis.[^s1]
- **Imaginative** techniques generate new hypotheses and futures: brainstorming, outside-in thinking, red team analysis and alternative futures analysis.[^s1]

A timeline chart says when to use each one. At the start of a project, brainstorming and a key assumptions check help ensure nothing important is missed. While hypotheses are being tested, contrarian methods challenge the emerging view. As a final check, the team reviews its key assumptions again and uses devil's advocacy if a consensus has gone unquestioned for a long time.[^s1]

*Workplace example.* A product team is sure a new pricing tier will lift revenue. Following the *Primer*'s sequence, it begins by writing out the assumptions behind that view, such as that current customers will not downgrade. Midway it asks a colleague to argue the opposite case. Before launch it lists the early sales signs that would show the plan is failing. The point is not the specific tools but the order: expose the premises, attack the favoured view, then decide what to watch.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

The *Primer* asserts that the techniques improve analysis, but it reports no tests of them.[^s1] The RAND team reviewed a sample of finished CIA products. Most showed no use of the techniques, and those that did use them generally did better on tradecraft standards such as separating assumptions from judgments. The authors called these findings preliminary.[^s2] Mandel and Tetlock go further. They argue that structured techniques spread without evidence that they work, and that steps left to the analyst's discretion can add noise to judgments rather than remove it.[^s3]

## Connections

The *Primer* introduces the key assumptions check: its diagnostic section presents the technique under that name, with the steps and timing later accounts cite.[^s1] It is influenced by Richards Heuer, whose work on mind-sets and bias underpins its opening chapter.[^s1] It is applied in intelligence analysis, where RAND used its categories to classify the techniques found in finished assessments.[^s2] It is challenged by Mandel and Tetlock's 2018 critique of the evidence for structured analytic techniques in general.[^s3] Related entries include structured analytic techniques, analysis of competing hypotheses and devil's advocacy, all of which appear among its twelve methods, and debiasing, the broader project the techniques belong to.[^s1]
