# Daniel Kahneman

Daniel Kahneman (1934-2024) was a psychologist whose work persuaded economists that real people do not reason like the idealized rational agents of their models. With Amos Tversky he helped found behavioral economics, and in 2002 he shared the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences with Vernon Smith.[^s1] For critical thinkers he matters for two reasons: he gave a vocabulary (fast intuition versus slow deliberation) for why smart people make predictable errors, and late in his career he modelled intellectual honesty by publicly questioning research he had once endorsed.[^s2][^s5]

## Origin

Kahneman was born in Tel Aviv in 1934 while his mother was visiting relatives; the family lived in France, survived the Nazi occupation, and moved to British-ruled Palestine in early 1948.[^s1] After service in the Israel Defense Forces, where he trained as a psychologist, he took a degree in psychology and mathematics at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem (1954) and a PhD at the University of California, Berkeley (1961).[^s1] He taught at the Hebrew University, the University of British Columbia and Berkeley before joining Princeton in 1993, where he held a joint appointment in psychology and public affairs, co-designed a psychology-for-policy course, and became emeritus in 2007.[^s1] Later honors included the 2013 Presidential Medal of Freedom. He died on 27 March 2024, aged 90.[^s1]

## How it works

Kahneman's ideas form a toolkit rather than a single theory.

**Two modes of thinking.** In his Nobel lecture, revised for the *American Economic Review* in 2003, he contrasted an automatic, effortless mode of thought (System 1) with a slow, controlled, effortful one (System 2). Most judgments start as intuitions that the deliberate system checks only loosely. His illustration was the bat-and-ball puzzle, where roughly half of the Princeton and Michigan students he cites gave the quick, wrong answer.[^s2] The 2011 bestseller *Thinking, Fast and Slow* brought this framing to a general audience.[^s1]

**Attribute substitution.** In the same paper he recast the heuristics he had studied with Tversky as one mechanism: faced with a hard question, the mind quietly answers an easier, more accessible one. His examples included judging how probable a student's field of study is by how well a personality sketch fits it, while ignoring base rates.[^s2] He also argued that framing and default options steer choices because people tend to accept a problem as it is described to them, citing organ-donor enrolment that is far higher under opt-out than opt-in rules.[^s2]

**Remembered versus lived experience.** In a 1993 experiment, 32 students held a hand in 14 °C water for 60 seconds, and on another trial for 60 seconds followed by 30 seconds of slightly warmer water. Asked which trial to repeat, 22 chose the longer one, which contained strictly more discomfort. Memory tracked the worst moment and the ending, not the total.[^s4] This "peak-end" pattern and the neglect of duration became the basis of his later work on well-being.[^s1][^s4]

**When to trust intuition.** With naturalistic-decision researcher Gary Klein, a long-time critic, he wrote a joint paper concluding that expert intuition deserves trust only when the environment is regular enough to be predictable and the expert has had adequate practice with feedback. The authors added that feeling confident is no evidence of being right.[^s3] A workplace example: a nurse's sense that a patient is deteriorating has been trained by frequent, quick feedback, whereas an executive's gut feeling about a five-year market forecast has not, so the second deserves an explicit check.[^s3]

**Noise.** In *Noise* (2021), written with Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein, he turned from systematic bias to unwanted variability between judges who should agree.[^s1]

## Reception and critique

*Thinking, Fast and Slow* praised research on behavioral priming, including a well-known experiment on priming with words about old age. When a Belgian team failed to replicate that experiment in 2012, Kahneman wrote an open letter to priming researchers warning of a coming "train wreck" and proposing a chain of cross-lab replications with full methodological detail, an advance commitment to publish the results, and shared data.[^s5][^s6] A decade later he judged that the field had not repaired the damage.[^s6] His willingness to retreat on claims he had popularized made him a reference point in the replication crisis, and readers of his book should treat the priming chapter with caution even though his core findings on heuristics, framing and prospect theory remain well established.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

- **Studied in** judgment and decision making: his Nobel lecture maps intuitive judgment and choice as the core of his research program.[^s2]
- **Applied in** behavioral economics, which Princeton credits him and Tversky with helping to found by bringing cognitive psychology into economics.[^s1]
- **Influenced by** Amos Tversky, his principal collaborator on the work recognized by the Nobel committee.[^s1]
- **Related to** dual-process theory and attribute substitution (his lecture's central ideas), the peak-end rule from the 1993 cold-water study, the framing and anchoring effects, prospect theory, the heuristics-and-biases program, and the replication crisis that his 2012 letter anticipated.[^s2][^s4][^s5]
