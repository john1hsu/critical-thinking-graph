# Peter Geach

Peter Thomas Geach (1916-2013) was a British philosopher and logician who spent his career showing how the logic of Gottlob Frege could expose errors in older ways of reasoning about language, generality and meaning.[^s1] He taught at Birmingham from 1951, became Professor of Logic at Leeds in 1967, and was elected a Fellow of the British Academy in 1965.[^s1] Several tools that critical thinkers use today carry his mark: the name of the quantifier shift fallacy, a sharp argument about what makes an inference valid across contexts, and a sustained attack on the textbook doctrine of terms.[^s1][^s4][^s6]

## Origin

Geach was born in London, read Greats at Balliol College, Oxford, and married the philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe in 1941.[^s1] With Max Black he published English translations of Frege's philosophical writings in 1952, which made Frege's ideas widely available to English-speaking philosophers.[^s1] His own books include *Mental Acts* (1957), which drew on Wittgenstein and Aquinas, *Reference and Generality* (1962; third edition 1980), *Logic Matters* (1972) and *Reason and Argument* (1976), a short logic text that grew out of the elementary course he taught at Leeds.[^s1] His Leeds inaugural lecture was titled "A History of the Corruptions of Logic".[^s1]

## How it works

Geach's main contributions to reasoning share one move: separate the logical form of a sentence from the surface grammar or the speech act that hides it, a habit he took from Frege's distinction between a sentence's content and the force with which it is uttered.[^s1][^s4]

**Against the doctrine of terms.** Traditional syllogistic treats subject and predicate as the same kind of item, a "term" that can swap places. Geach thought this was a deep mistake, because a name and a predicate do different logical jobs. Kenny records his quip that Aristotle was "logic's Adam" and the doctrine of terms his Fall.[^s1] He applied the same criticism to the rule of distributed terms used to check syllogisms, arguing that the idea of a term "referring to everything it denotes" is incoherent and arose from a sixteenth-century corruption of medieval supposition theory.[^s5] Terence Parsons answered in 2006 that the doctrine is older and can be made coherent.[^s5]

**Quantifier shift.** Geach used the label "quantifier shift" for the slide from "every A has some B" to "some one B serves every A", and the way he introduced the name suggests he coined it.[^s6] A workplace version: every team has a manager who approves its leave requests, so some manager approves everyone's leave. The premise allows a different approver per team; the conclusion needs one person for all. Predicate logic makes the difference visible as the order of the two quantifiers.[^s6]

**The embedding problem.** Geach argued that a moral sentence such as "lying is wrong" must mean the same thing whether it is asserted on its own or embedded in "if lying is wrong, then getting others to lie is wrong". Theories that explain moral words by what speakers do with them, such as commending or condemning, struggle here, because the embedded clause commends nothing. If its content shifted between premises, a modus ponens with it would equivocate.[^s4] Following Frege's split between a sentence's content and its assertoric force, this challenge became known as the Frege-Geach problem, and much later non-cognitivist work is an attempt to answer it.[^s3][^s4]

**Relative identity.** Geach also introduced the phrase "relative identity" for his thesis that there is no absolute sameness: two things may be the same F but different Gs.[^s2] His "surman" example treats men who share a surname as the same surman though they are different men. Critics such as Harry Deutsch argue that the conclusion does not follow and that absolute identity survives.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced the quantifier shift fallacy.** The Fallacy Files notes that Geach used the name in a way that suggests he created it; the attribution is plausible but not certain, so it carries low confidence.[^s6]
- **Introduced the Frege-Geach problem.** His charge of equivocation against expressivist accounts of moral language set the agenda for the embedding debate.[^s3][^s4]
- **Introduced relative identity.** He named the thesis and supplied its best-known arguments and examples.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Gottlob Frege.** He translated Frege and built on Frege's distinctions between names and predicates and between content and force.[^s1]
- **Influenced by Ludwig Wittgenstein.** *Mental Acts* drew on Wittgenstein alongside Aquinas.[^s1]
- **Challenged the distribution of terms.** Geach's critique of distribution is the main challenge to that syllogistic rule, and Parsons's reply is the main defence.[^s5]
