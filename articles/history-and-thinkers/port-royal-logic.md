# Port-Royal Logic

The *Port-Royal Logic* is the common name for *La Logique ou l'art de penser* (*Logic, or the Art of Thinking*), written by the theologians Antoine Arnauld and Pierre Nicole of the Port-Royal circle and first published in 1662.[^s1] Its Stanford Encyclopedia entry calls it the most widely read text in formal logic between Aristotle and the end of the nineteenth century, a reach shown by a 1981 critical edition that counted 63 French and 10 English editions; an 1818 English edition was used at Oxford and Cambridge.[^s1] For critical thinking it matters for two reasons: it treated logic as a practical art of judging well rather than a scholastic exercise, and it rewrote the inherited catalogue of fallacies.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Arnauld had written the Fourth Objections to Descartes's *Meditations*, and the book is Cartesian in its theory of ideas and its emphasis on clear and distinct perception.[^s1] Following Descartes, the authors ranked syllogistic technique below the methods used to discover knowledge, while still keeping the formal machinery.[^s1] The book went through four major revisions in the authors' lifetimes, with the last and most important in 1683; a Second Discourse defending a critical treatment of Aristotle was added in 1664.[^s1] It was a companion to the *Port-Royal Grammar* of 1660, and the authors claimed a reader could master it in a week or so, a pointed contrast with the long logic courses of the schools.[^s1]

## How it works

The book has four parts, matching four operations of the mind: conceiving ideas, judging, reasoning and ordering (method).[^s1] Part I introduces the distinction between the comprehension of a general idea (its attributes) and its extension (the things it applies to), and notes that the two vary inversely.[^s1] Part II covers categorical propositions, the square of opposition and conversion, and Part III restates syllogistic rules, including an influential version of the distribution rules, then treats sorites, dilemmas, topics and fallacies.[^s1] Part IV concerns method, and its closing chapter on judging future events warns against fearing a large harm that is very unlikely while ignoring a small one that is probable, an early statement of weighing outcomes by their likelihood.[^s1]

The chapter on dilemmas shows the practical bent. A dilemma divides a whole into parts and concludes about the whole what was shown of each part; the authors warn that it fails if the division is incomplete or if a conclusion does not really follow from one of the parts.[^s3] Their worked example is the old argument against marrying: a wife will be either beautiful and cause jealousy or plain and displease, so one should not marry. They answer that the split leaves out women who are neither, and that a beautiful wife need not cause jealousy.[^s3] A modern office version has the same shape: "either we launch this month or a rival takes the market" ignores a phased launch and assumes the rival is ready. Testing whether the listed options are really exhaustive is exactly the first critical question asked of a false dilemma today.[^s3]

## Fallacies

Dufour's 2019 study in *Argumentation* describes the book as the first serious break with the Aristotelian treatment of fallacies: the main novelty is the list itself, from which several Aristotelian items are dropped and, across its two fallacy chapters, more than ten new ones added.[^s2] The chapter on reasoning badly keeps seven Aristotelian fallacies, including begging the question, false cause, accident, composition and division, and equivocation, and adds two: imperfect enumeration and faulty induction.[^s2][^s3] Imperfect enumeration, inserted in the 1664 edition and traced by Dufour to Descartes's rule that enumerations be complete, is concluding too fast because not every way something could be or happen has been considered.[^s2][^s3] Faulty induction is generalising from too few cases.[^s2] A further chapter, attributed to Nicole, moves from logic to moral psychology: it lists errors driven by self-love, interest and passion, being swayed by eloquence or by the speaker's rank, and judging a decision only by its outcome.[^s2]

## Limitations

The book is a work of its time. Much of Part III reproduces traditional syllogistic, its examples are often theological, and the treatment of method assumes Cartesian views about clear and distinct ideas that few hold today.[^s1] The modern label "false dilemma" is later, and the link drawn here rests on the book's own account of incomplete division and imperfect enumeration rather than on a claim that the authors named the fallacy; Dufour treats imperfect enumeration as a new item without connecting it to the modern name.[^s2][^s3] Dufour also stresses that fallacy theory still lacks consensus, so the book's taxonomy is one influential proposal rather than a settled framework.[^s2]

## Connections

The book is **authored by Antoine Arnauld and Pierre Nicole**, and it is **influenced by René Descartes**, whose theory of ideas and preference for method over syllogism shape all four parts.[^s1] It is equally **influenced by Aristotle**, since most of its fallacies and much of its syllogistic come from the *Organon*, even where it criticises him.[^s2] It **introduces** an early analysis of the **false dilemma**, through its test for incomplete division in dilemmas and its new sophism of imperfect enumeration.[^s3] It is **studied in argumentation theory**, where historians of fallacy theory read it as a turning point between Aristotle and modern lists.[^s2] Related nodes include the **distribution of terms** and **illicit major**, whose process rules the book stated influentially.[^s1]
