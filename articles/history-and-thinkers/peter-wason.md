# Peter Wason

Peter Cathcart Wason (1924-2003) was a British psychologist who spent his career at University College London and invented two of the most studied puzzles in the psychology of thinking: the 2-4-6 rule-discovery task and the four-card selection task.[^s1] Both showed intelligent adults testing their ideas by looking for agreement rather than for refutation, and a 2016 retrospective credits his 1960 paper with setting the study of cognitive biases in reasoning in motion, years before the heuristics-and-biases program of Tversky and Kahneman.[^s2]

## Origin

Wason served as an army officer in the Second World War and was invalided out with serious injuries in 1945. He then read English at New College, Oxford, and taught English at Aberdeen before retraining in psychology at UCL, graduating in 1953.[^s1] He stayed at UCL, first in the Medical Research Council's Industrial Psychology Research Group and later as Reader in Psycholinguistics, spent 1963 at Harvard's Center for Cognitive Studies, and retired early in the 1980s; in later life he played correspondence chess at International Master level.[^s1] His obituarists, Jonathan Evans and Philip Johnson-Laird, describe an experimenter who ran his own participants, interviewed them closely and cared more about surprising phenomena than about statistics.[^s1]

## How it works

**The 2-4-6 task (1960).** Wason told 29 undergraduates that the triple 2, 4, 6 obeyed a rule and asked them to discover it by proposing triples and hearing whether each fitted. The rule was simply any ascending sequence, yet only 6 of the 29 named it correctly at their first announcement.[^s3] Most proposed only triples that fitted their own guess, such as "add two each time", and so never met the evidence that would have shown the guess too narrow. Citing Karl Popper, Wason argued that sound inference needs eliminative testing, deliberately trying triples one's hypothesis forbids, and that this attitude can be taught.[^s3] A workplace parallel: a manager who believes a promotion lifted sales and checks only the regions that ran it repeats the pattern; the informative test is a region where the hypothesis predicts no lift.

**The selection task (1966, 1968).** Participants see four cards (A, K, 4, 7), each with a letter on one side and a number on the other, and must say which cards to turn over to test the rule "if a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other".[^s5] The logically required choice is A and 7, since only those could reveal a vowel paired with an odd number, which is the pattern of modus tollens. Most people pick A and 4, or A alone; one early tally put the correct choice at under one in ten.[^s5] Wason took this as a further sign of a verifying rather than falsifying strategy, and Nickerson calls the triplet task "one of the most fertile paradigms in experimental psychology".[^s4]

## Reception and critique

Wason's tasks spawned decades of rival explanations. Familiar, rule-like content (such as transport or postage regulations) made the selection task much easier, which Wason himself reported and which later fed pragmatic-schema and social-contract theories.[^s5] Jonathan Evans proposed matching bias; Oaksford and Chater recast card choices as a sensible Bayesian strategy for gathering information (the optimal data selection model); and Stenning and van Lambalgen argued that many "errors" reflect reasonable readings of an ambiguous conditional rather than faulty logic.[^s5] On the 2-4-6 side, Nickerson notes that much of the confirmation participants received was weak, and that the bias label covers several distinct tendencies.[^s4] Wason with Evans also coined the "type 1" and "type 2" process labels in 1975, an early statement of dual-process theory.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Wason is **studied in cognitive psychology**, where Evans places his work at the origin of both bias research and dual-process accounts of reasoning.[^s2] He was **influenced by Karl Popper**, whose falsification criterion framed the 2-4-6 study,[^s3] and, according to his obituarists, by **W. V. O. Quine's** account of material implication when he designed the selection task.[^s1] His reading of the selection task is **challenged by the optimal data selection model**, which treats typical choices as rational information search.[^s5] He is related to **confirmation bias**, **hypothesis testing** and **falsifiability**, which his tasks probe; to the **Wason 1960 rule-discovery study** and the **Wason selection task**; to **modus tollens**, the inference the selection task requires; and to **dual-process theory** and **matching bias**, which grew from attempts to explain his results.[^s1][^s4][^s5]
