# Gordon Pennycook

Gordon Pennycook is a Canadian cognitive psychologist who studies reasoning errors and the factors that shape what people believe, working where cognitive and social psychology meet. He joined Cornell University in 2023 as an associate professor of psychology and Himan Brown Faculty Fellow.[^s1] His research has a consistent theme: many questionable beliefs, from empty-sounding profundities to false headlines, take hold less because people reason badly toward a goal than because they do not stop to reason at all. He also asks what cheap, scalable nudges can prompt that missing reflection.[^s3][^s4][^s2]

## Origin

Pennycook took his PhD in cognitive psychology at the University of Waterloo in 2016. He was then a Banting Postdoctoral Fellow at Yale University from 2016 to 2018, and a faculty member at the University of Regina's Hill/Levene Schools of Business from 2018 until his move to Cornell.[^s1] His early work belongs to the dual-process tradition and uses performance on the Cognitive Reflection Test and on heuristics-and-biases problems as measures of a person's willingness to think analytically instead of accepting an intuitive first answer.[^s3]

The 2015 paper "On the reception and detection of pseudo-profound bullshit", written with James Allan Cheyne, Nathaniel Barr, Derek Koehler and Jonathan Fugelsang in *Judgment and Decision Making*, made his name. The authors defined pseudo-profound bullshit as impressive-seeming statements, presented as true and meaningful, that are in fact vacuous. They then measured how profound people rated grammatical sentences assembled from random buzzwords.[^s3] Across four studies the resulting bullshit receptivity scale was internally consistent. Higher receptivity went with lower Cognitive Reflection Test scores (r = -.33 in Study 1), weaker heuristics-and-biases performance, and stronger paranormal and religious belief. The authors read this as a bias toward accepting claims as true, not just poor skepticism.[^s3]

## How it works

Pennycook's best-known argument concerns misinformation. In "Lazy, not biased" (*Cognition*, 2019), he and David Rand tested two accounts with 3,446 online participants. A motivated-reasoning account predicts that more analytic people use their skill to defend congenial falsehoods. A classical-reasoning account predicts that analytic thinking helps people reject implausible claims whatever their politics.[^s4] Higher Cognitive Reflection Test scores went with rating fake headlines as less accurate and telling real from fake news apart better, even for headlines that fit the participant's own ideology, which matched the classical account.[^s4]

That diagnosis implies a remedy. If people share falsehoods because accuracy is not on their mind, reminding them of accuracy should help. In a 2020 *Psychological Science* paper on COVID-19 claims with more than 1,700 U.S. adults, Pennycook and colleagues found that people discerned truth much worse when deciding what to share than when asked directly about accuracy. Asking participants to rate the accuracy of one unrelated headline first nearly tripled truth discernment in their later sharing intentions.[^s2] At Cornell he continued this line with field experiments on platforms such as Twitter and Facebook, where subtle reminders to think about accuracy modestly improved the quality of what people shared.[^s1]

A workplace example shows the idea. Before an internal newsletter reposts a striking statistic from social media, an editor who first asks "is this accurate?" invokes the reflection that, on Pennycook's account, people skip when they share on autopilot.[^s2]

## Legacy and limitations

Pennycook helped make "bullshit receptivity" a measurable construct and pushed the misinformation field toward light-touch interventions that can be tested at scale.[^s3][^s2] His conclusions remain debated. Batailler, Brannon, Teas and Gawronski reanalysed the "Lazy, not biased" data with signal detection theory, which separates discernment from a general tendency to call items fake. They found that cognitive reflection predicted better discernment, as Pennycook and Rand reported. But participants were also more likely to label politically uncongenial headlines fake regardless of truth, and reflection did not reduce this shift. On their reading, lazy thinking and partisan bias both contribute, through different parameters.[^s5] Accuracy-prompt effects in the field are modest, and the 2020 results come from stated sharing intentions in online surveys, so the technique is better seen as one small, cheap lever than a cure.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced accuracy prompts.** The 2020 *Psychological Science* study framed and tested the accuracy nudge as a scalable intervention against sharing false content.[^s2]
- **Introduced bullshit receptivity.** The 2015 paper defined pseudo-profound bullshit and built the scale that measures receptivity to it.[^s3]
- **Studied in cognitive psychology.** His PhD and research program sit in cognitive psychology, applied to belief and misinformation.[^s1]
- **Challenged by Batailler and colleagues (2022).** Their signal detection reanalysis disputes the "lazy, not biased" conclusion, finding a partisan response bias alongside the reflection effect.[^s5]
- **Related to the Cognitive Reflection Test, dual-process theory and motivated reasoning.** His work uses the test as its main measure of analytic thinking and pits the classical, dual-process view of reasoning against motivated-reasoning accounts.[^s4][^s3]
