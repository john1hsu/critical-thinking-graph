# Pyrrho of Elis

Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360-270 BCE) is the Greek philosopher after whom Pyrrhonian skepticism is named, one of the two main skeptical traditions of the ancient world alongside the skepticism of Plato's Academy.[^s2][^s1] He wrote nothing, so everything known about him comes through his pupil Timon of Phlius and later reporters, and scholars still argue about what exactly he held.[^s1][^s2] For critical thinkers he matters as the first Western figure to make withholding judgment a way of life rather than a debating move.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Pyrrho came from Elis in the western Peloponnese, where a statue of him later stood in the marketplace and where he is said to have served as high priest.[^s1] His most reliable reported teacher was Anaxarchus of Abdera, with whom he travelled on Alexander the Great's expedition to India; there he met the ascetics the Greeks called "naked wise men", though Bett judges real philosophical exchange unlikely given the language barrier.[^s1] He gathered admirers, chief among them Timon, whose satirical poems survive only in fragments.[^s1] After Timon the line seems to have lapsed: Pyrrhonism became a named, continuing movement only in the first century BCE, when Aenesidemus broke with an Academy he found too dogmatic and revived it under Pyrrho's name.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Sextus Empiricus, writing in the second or third century CE, likewise presented Pyrrho as the founder, or at least the inspiration, of the skepticism he set out.[^s2]

## How it works

The key evidence is a summary of Timon's account preserved by the Peripatetic Aristocles. It frames philosophy as three questions aimed at happiness: what are things like by nature, how should we be disposed toward them, and what follows for those so disposed?[^s1][^s2] The answers: things are indifferent, unmeasurable and undecidable; so our senses and opinions tell us neither truths nor falsehoods and we should trust neither; and the result is first speechlessness, then freedom from worry (ataraxia).[^s1][^s2] The recommended way of speaking says of each thing that it "no more is than is not", a formula Sextus later used to express suspended judgment.[^s1]

Scholars divide over the first answer. On a metaphysical reading, Pyrrho claimed that reality itself is indeterminate, which is a bold positive thesis rather than skepticism in the later sense; on an epistemological reading, he claimed only that we cannot determine how things are, which makes him a forerunner of later Pyrrhonism.[^s1][^s2] The surviving text slightly favours the metaphysical reading, but there is no consensus, and Vogt concludes that Pyrrho was arguably not a Pyrrhonian skeptic himself.[^s1][^s3]

A workplace illustration of the attitude his followers built on: two vendor reports reach opposite verdicts on the same product, each with plausible test data. A Pyrrhonian-style reviewer does not pick the report she prefers. She notes that the reasons on each side seem equally weighty, holds off on a verdict, keeps investigating, and finds that the pressure to decide eases once she stops forcing one.[^s3] Later Pyrrhonists made this structure explicit: setting appearances and arguments against each other until they balance, then suspending judgment, then reaching tranquillity.[^s3]

## Legacy and limitations

Pyrrho's own influence was modest; Cicero and Seneca already describe him as neglected, and the claim that he shaped Arcesilaus's skeptical turn in the Academy is disputed.[^s1][^s3] His lasting legacy came through the revival: Aenesidemus's ten modes and the five modes attributed to Agrippa, preserved by Sextus, became the toolkit of later skepticism and still frame the regress problem in epistemology.[^s3] Two cautions apply. First, much of the biography is unreliable: Antigonus of Carystus told stories of Pyrrho ignoring cliffs and carts that Bett treats as hostile invention, while better-attested anecdotes show him calm under surgery and in a storm at sea.[^s1] Second, the old charge that a skeptic cannot act was answered by later Pyrrhonists, not by Pyrrho: Sextus replied that skeptics live by appearances, nature, custom and practical skills without holding beliefs about how things really are.[^s3][^s1]

## Connections

- **Introduced philosophical skepticism** in the sense that the Pyrrhonian tradition took him as its founder; the attribution is medium-confidence because his own position may have been metaphysical rather than skeptical.[^s2][^s1]
- **Influenced by Anaxarchus**, his teacher and travelling companion, and probably by **Democritus**, whose ethics and doubts about the senses resemble his.[^s1]
- **Influenced Sextus Empiricus**, who claimed him as the inspiration for the skepticism recorded in the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with dogmatism**, the stance of those who claim to have found the truth, which Pyrrhonists defined themselves against.[^s3]
- **Related: suspension of judgment**, the attitude his followers turned into a method, and **Socrates**, whose profession of ignorance inspired the parallel Academic skeptical line.[^s3][^s2]
