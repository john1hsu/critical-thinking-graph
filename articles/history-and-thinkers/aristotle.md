# Aristotle

Aristotle (384-322 BCE) was the Greek philosopher who invented formal logic as a discipline: he built the syllogistic, the first worked-out system for deciding which inferences follow of necessity, and it remained the core of logic until the nineteenth century.[^s4][^s1] He also wrote the first catalogue of fallacies and a theory of persuasive argument, so much of the vocabulary of critical thinking, from "deduction" and "induction" to "begging the question", traces back to him.[^s2]

## Origin

Aristotle's father, Nicomachus, was physician to the Macedonian king Amyntas.[^s3] At seventeen Aristotle went to Athens and studied in Plato's Academy for about twenty years, until around Plato's death in 348 or 347 BCE.[^s3][^s4] After some years away, including a period as tutor to the young Alexander the Great, he returned to Athens and in 335 BCE founded his own school, the Lyceum; its members became known as the Peripatetics.[^s4][^s3] He left Athens about thirteen years later, possibly to escape a charge of impiety, and died at Chalcis in 322 BCE.[^s3][^s4]

He never called his work "logic". Ancient commentators later gathered six of his treatises into a manual called the *Organon*, Greek for "tool", reflecting the view that logic is an instrument for inquiry rather than a branch of it: the *Categories*, *On Interpretation*, *Prior Analytics*, *Posterior Analytics*, *Topics* and *Sophistical Refutations*.[^s2][^s3][^s1]

## How it works

Aristotle's central notion is the *sullogismos*, usually translated "deduction": an argument in which, once certain things are supposed, something different follows necessarily because of them. Smith notes that this is close to the modern idea of validity.[^s1] The syllogisms of the *Prior Analytics* have two premises and three terms. Each premise is one of four kinds of categorical statement, later labelled A (all S are P), E (no S are P), I (some S are P) and O (some S are not P), and the position of the shared "middle" term fixes the argument's figure.[^s2][^s1] A few first-figure forms, such as the one later named Barbara, are "perfect" because their validity is evident without further steps; he reduced the other valid forms to these.[^s1]

A workplace case shows the idea. "All invoices over the limit need a director's sign-off; every invoice in this batch is over the limit; so every invoice in this batch needs a sign-off" is Barbara, and it holds whatever the subject matter. To show that a premise pair does *not* yield a conclusion, Aristotle supplied concrete terms that make the premises true and the conclusion false, which is an early form of the method of counterexample.[^s1]

His logic reached beyond strict proof. The *Topics* gives a method for dialectic, arguing from widely accepted premises, which Smith describes as differing from demonstration in its premises rather than its form.[^s1] *Sophistical Refutations* sorts arguments that only appear to succeed into thirteen fallacies: six that depend on language, such as equivocation and amphiboly, and seven that do not, among them begging the question, false cause and affirming the consequent.[^s2] The *Rhetoric* adds that persuasion works through the speaker's character, the audience's emotions and the argument itself, and through looser forms such as the enthymeme and the example.[^s2]

## Legacy and limitations

In the Hellenistic period the Stoic logic of Chrysippus rivalled Aristotle's, but Aristotelian logic then became dominant and passed into the Arabic and Latin medieval traditions; Kant could still claim that it had gained little since Aristotle.[^s1] Modern mathematical logic exposed serious limits in a system built from four statement types and three-term arguments, and logicians largely set the syllogism aside.[^s2][^s1] Scholars have since come back to Aristotle with new respect for the rigour of his system.[^s1] For a practitioner the durable lessons are his: judge an inference by its form, test it by looking for a counterexample, and learn the recurring ways arguments only seem to work.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Influenced by Plato**, in whose Academy he studied for two decades.[^s3]
- **Studied in logic**, the field Britannica credits him with founding.[^s4]
- **Related: the *Organon* and *Sophistical Refutations***, his logical treatises and his fallacy catalogue.[^s2]
- **Related: the syllogism and the square of opposition**, his theory of categorical deduction and the relations among A, E, I and O statements.[^s2]
- **Related: deductive reasoning, the method of counterexample and affirming the consequent**, which existing articles trace to him; the reasoner records these as ideas he introduced.[^s1][^s2]
- **Related: Chrysippus**, whose Stoic logic of connectives was the main ancient rival to his logic of terms.[^s1]
