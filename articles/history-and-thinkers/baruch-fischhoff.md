# Baruch Fischhoff

Baruch Fischhoff is a decision scientist who has been at Carnegie Mellon University since 1987, where he is Howard Heinz University Professor in Engineering and Public Policy and the Institute for Politics and Strategy.[^s1] His doctoral experiments of 1975 began the study of hindsight bias, the tendency to see an outcome as more predictable once it is known.[^s2] With Sarah Lichtenstein and L. D. Phillips he also wrote the first broad review of how well people's confidence matches their accuracy, which found overconfidence in most tasks.[^s4] He is a member of the US National Academy of Sciences and a past president of both the Society for Judgment and Decision Making and the Society for Risk Analysis.[^s1]

## Origin

Fischhoff took a BS in mathematics and psychology at Wayne State University in 1967, then an MA (1972) and PhD (1975) in psychology at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.[^s1] A 2017 interview in *Memory Studies* places his first hindsight studies in Israel in the early 1970s, when Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases research was just beginning.[^s6] He then worked in Oregon with Lichtenstein, first at the Oregon Research Institute and later on a long Office of Naval Research program on the psychology of confidence.[^s4][^s5]

## Main contributions

**Hindsight bias (1975).** In "Hindsight ≠ foresight", Fischhoff gave people accounts of historical, political and clinical cases. Those told that a particular outcome had happened rated it as more likely than those who were not told. The effect held when people were told to ignore the outcome and when they were asked to judge as someone without it.[^s2] He called this "creeping determinism". In a second study with Beyth, people predicted the results of President Nixon's 1972 trips to China and the Soviet Union. When later asked what they had predicted, they recalled forecasts closer to what had actually happened.[^s2] A 2007 overview in *Social Cognition* calls the 1975 paper groundbreaking and the starting point of the field.[^s2] By 2012 the topic had more than 800 scholarly papers. Roese and Vohs describe it as among the most widely studied decision traps, found from age 3 to 95 and across cultures.[^s3]

A workplace example shows why this matters. After a product launch fails, a review board reads the early warning signs and concludes they were obvious. Fischhoff's results suggest the board is judging with outcome knowledge that the original team did not have. A fair review first asks what a reasonable team could have known at the time.[^s2][^s3]

**Overconfidence and calibration (1976-1983).** A judge is calibrated when, across many statements given 70% confidence, about 70% turn out true. Lichtenstein, Fischhoff and Phillips's 1976 report reviewed the studies available at the time. They found weather forecasters well calibrated but most other people overconfident. In judgments about uncertain quantities, between 20% and 50% of true values fell outside ranges meant to miss only about 2%.[^s4] They also described what is now called the hard-easy effect: overconfidence on hard items and underconfidence on easy ones.[^s4] The finding itself was older; the review drew on earlier work, including stock forecasts from the early 1970s.[^s4] The graph therefore credits Fischhoff with establishing the effect only at low confidence. The team's later Navy report found that warnings alone did not help. Personalized feedback did, and so did asking people to list reasons their answer might be wrong.[^s5]

**Debiasing.** The same report credits Fischhoff with a theory of debiasing that reanalyzed the overconfidence and hindsight literatures.[^s5] For hindsight, Roese and Vohs found that the most consistent remedy is to explain how outcomes that did not happen could have happened. They warn that generating too many alternatives can backfire, and recommend two or three.[^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

Hindsight bias is robust as a phenomenon, but it is not one thing. Roese and Vohs separate three levels: memory distortion ("I said it would happen"), inevitability ("it had to happen") and foreseeability ("I knew it would happen"). Each has partly different causes.[^s3] Some hindsight measures have low reliability, and debiasing has had limited success. Warnings mostly fail, and large reductions usually take considerable effort.[^s2] Expertise helps only where feedback is clear and frequent, as in weather forecasting.[^s3] The 1976 calibration review itself noted that tasks near the limit of knowledge, such as predicting stock movements, showed essentially no calibration, so its summary figures depend heavily on the kind of task studied.[^s4]

## Connections

Fischhoff **introduced hindsight bias**: the 1975 paper is credited with starting its study.[^s2] With Lichtenstein and Phillips he helped **establish the overconfidence effect** through the 1976 calibration review; this is recorded at low confidence because the review collected earlier findings.[^s4] His work is **studied in judgment and decision making**, a field whose society he led.[^s1] He is related to **debiasing** and its 1982 framing, to **consider-the-opposite**-style remedies for both biases, to **calibration**, to his collaborator **Sarah Lichtenstein**, and to the **heuristics-and-biases program** of Kahneman and Tversky, which formed the setting for his early work.[^s3][^s5][^s6] He later applied this research to **risk communication** and **intelligence analysis**, and chaired a National Research Council committee on the latter.[^s1]
