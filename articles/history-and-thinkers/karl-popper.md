# Karl Popper

Sir Karl Raimund Popper (1902-1994) was a Vienna-born philosopher who spent most of his career at the London School of Economics and became one of the most influential twentieth-century thinkers about how knowledge grows.[^s1][^s3] His central claim, that a theory counts as scientific only if some possible observation could refute it, gave critical thinkers a durable test for separating testable claims from ones that explain everything and therefore risk nothing.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Popper was born in Vienna on 28 July 1902 and studied at the University of Vienna, where he qualified as a teacher and completed a doctorate in 1928 under the psychologist Karl Bühler, with a dissertation on method in the psychology of thinking.[^s1][^s4] (One LSE catalogue record gives 1926 for the doctorate; the Stanford and Konstanz accounts give 1928.)[^s3] Before his academic career he trained as a cabinetmaker, worked as a social worker with young people and taught in Vienna schools.[^s1][^s4] He was never a member of the Vienna Circle, though he engaged closely with its concerns and criticised its account of how basic observation statements are justified.[^s1]

His first book, *Logik der Forschung* (1935), set out falsificationism; it appeared in English as *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* in 1959.[^s1][^s2] In 1937 he left Austria for a philosophy post at Canterbury University College in Christchurch, New Zealand, where he spent the Second World War; *The Poverty of Historicism* appeared in *Economica* in 1944-45 and *The Open Society and Its Enemies* in 1945.[^s1][^s3] He moved to the LSE after the war, becoming Professor of Logic and Scientific Method in 1949 and emeritus in 1969; he was knighted in 1965 and died on 17 September 1994.[^s1][^s3][^s4] Later works include *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963) and the intellectual autobiography *Unended Quest* (1976).[^s1]

## How it works

Popper's starting point was Hume's argument that no number of observed instances can justify a universal law. He accepted the argument but denied that science depends on induction at all.[^s1][^s2] Instead, scientists propose bold conjectures and try to refute them. The logic is asymmetric: a thousand white swans cannot prove that all swans are white, but one genuine black swan can show the claim false, an inference with the form of modus tollens.[^s2] A theory that survives severe tests, ones whose outcome was improbable given background knowledge, is "corroborated," which Popper insisted is a record of survival, not proof of truth.[^s2]

From this came his criterion of demarcation. Einstein's general relativity was scientific because the 1919 eclipse observations could have refuted it; psychoanalysis and Adler's psychology were not, in his view, because they could explain any behaviour whatever.[^s1][^s2] Marxism, he argued, began with testable predictions but was rescued from their failure by ad hoc reinterpretations.[^s1][^s2] Popper distinguished such ad hoc moves from legitimate auxiliary hypotheses, which save a theory by making new testable predictions, as the hypothesised planet that led to Neptune's discovery in 1846 did.[^s2]

A workplace version: a team lead says a new onboarding programme "is working." A Popperian colleague asks what result, agreed in advance, would show it is not working, for example new hires' support tickets in their first 30 days failing to fall below last year's level. If every outcome can be read as success, the claim is not being tested.[^s2]

Popper carried the same critical attitude into politics. *The Open Society and Its Enemies* traces an authoritarian strand of thought through Plato, Hegel and Marx and argues that the trial-and-error criticism that drives science should also govern public life.[^s5] He favoured piecemeal, reversible reform over sweeping plans for society.[^s1]

## Reception and limitations

Popper's account shaped later philosophy of science even where it was rejected. Thomas Kuhn argued in 1962 that science moves through paradigm shifts rather than steady refutation, and Kuhn's picture largely displaced Popper's in later debate.[^s1] Imre Lakatos held that scientists rationally keep a theory despite failed predictions when they lack a better rival, and critics in the Duhem-Quine tradition note that a failed prediction never tells you which of a theory's many assumptions is at fault.[^s1][^s2] Others argued that Freud's theory was in fact testable, that corroboration gives no reason to expect future success, and that Bayesian confirmation fits scientific practice better.[^s1][^s2] His formal definition of verisimilitude, meant to say when one false theory is closer to the truth than another, was shown to fail by David Miller and Pavel Tichý in 1974.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Studied in philosophy of science**, the field his demarcation criterion and falsificationism helped define.[^s1]
- **Studied in political philosophy** through *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, his defence of liberal democracy against historicist and totalitarian thought.[^s5]
- **Influenced by David Hume**, whose critique of induction Popper accepted and then sidestepped by denying that science needs induction.[^s1]
- **Challenged by Thomas Kuhn**, whose account of paradigms and scientific revolutions displaced falsificationism in much later debate.[^s1]
- **Challenged by Imre Lakatos**, who denied that single critical tests decide between theories.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with the verifiability criterion** of the logical empiricists, which Popper rejected as a test of meaning or science.[^s2]
- **Related:** he introduced **falsifiability** in **The Logic of Scientific Discovery**, which links to the **problem of induction**, **modus tollens**, **ad hoc hypotheses**, **pseudoscience**, **hypothesis testing** and the broader stance known as **critical rationalism**.[^s1][^s2]
