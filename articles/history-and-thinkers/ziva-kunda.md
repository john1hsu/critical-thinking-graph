# Ziva Kunda

Ziva Kunda was a social psychologist who gave the idea of motivated reasoning its modern scientific form. Writing from Princeton in 1990, she argued that what people want to conclude does shape what they conclude, but only by steering ordinary cognitive processes such as memory search and the construction of justifications, not by overriding them.[^s1] She later moved to the University of Waterloo, which holds an annual memorial lecture in her name after her death in February 2004.[^s5]

## Origin

According to her husband and collaborator, the philosopher Paul Thagard, Kunda had settled on the effect of motivation on inference as her doctoral topic by the early 1980s; she coined "motivated inference" in a 1987 paper and "motivated reasoning" in the 1990 review.[^s6] Thagard notes that the underlying idea is ancient, with forerunners from Thucydides and Aristotle to Francis Bacon, and that Arie Kruglanski separately spoke of motivated cognition; what was new was experimental evidence and an explicit theory of mechanism.[^s6] Her timing mattered. Many researchers then held that apparently self-serving judgments could be explained by purely cognitive factors such as prior expectations, so motivational explanations had fallen out of favour.[^s1][^s6]

## How it works

Kunda's central move was to separate two kinds of goal. With an **accuracy goal** a person wants to be right and tends to process more carefully and use the most appropriate strategies. With a **directional goal** a person wants a particular answer and tends to recruit whichever beliefs and rules best support it.[^s1] The key constraint is that people try to build a case a dispassionate observer would accept. They reach a wished-for conclusion only when they can assemble a plausible justification, and strong evidence can still force an unwelcome one, which is why the bias feels like objectivity from the inside.[^s1]

A workplace illustration: a manager who championed a new scheduling tool reads a pilot report. Faced with three teams whose turnaround improved and one whose turnaround worsened, she readily recalls reasons the struggling team was atypical, but searches for no such reasons in the successful teams. Each step looks reasonable, yet the search is lopsided, and a colleague with no stake would weigh the four teams evenly. Kunda's framework predicts both the lopsided search and the limit on it: if all four teams had slowed down, no plausible story would rescue the tool.[^s1]

Her best-known evidence is the 1987 caffeine study. Women who drank a lot of coffee found an article linking caffeine to health risks for women less convincing than light drinkers did, while men showed no such difference, and the gap appeared only when the stated risks were serious.[^s1] The 1990 review also drew together work on self-concepts, which shifted toward desired traits only within the bounds of what people already knew about themselves, and on biased evaluation of research that contradicted prior views.[^s1]

Beyond motivated reasoning, Kunda worked on how people form impressions of others. With Thagard she modelled the integration of stereotypes, traits and behaviours as parallel constraint satisfaction in a connectionist network, in which no source of information is privileged and the meaning of each depends on what else is active.[^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

Later work both built on and refined Kunda's account. Dunning, Leuenberger and Sherman (1995) noted that her 1987 studies were largely correlational and could not fully exclude a purely cognitive reading, so they randomly assigned people to motivated and unmotivated conditions; their results offered more direct support for her claims about self-serving causal theories.[^s2] A 2015 review by Patterson, Operskalski and Barbey adopted her accuracy-versus-directional distinction but argued that strong emotion can sometimes override her plausibility constraint, and that her memory-search mechanism is consistent with neuroscience findings yet incomplete.[^s3] Thagard reports that the 1990 paper had been cited more than 9,000 times by 2022, a figure that will keep growing.[^s6]

## Connections

Kunda is **studied in social psychology**, the field her 1990 review addressed,[^s1] and, through the connectionist impression-formation models she built with Thagard, in **cognitive science**.[^s4] Her account is **supported by** the experimental extension of Dunning, Leuenberger and Sherman (1995),[^s2] and **challenged** in part by Patterson, Operskalski and Barbey (2015), who loosen her plausibility constraint.[^s3] She is related to **motivated reasoning**, which she introduced in its modern form, and to the **Kunda 1987 caffeine study** that anchored it;[^s1][^s6] to **biased assimilation**, **confirmation bias** and **myside bias**, neighbouring effects her review discussed or that later work connects to it;[^s1][^s3] and to **cognitive dissonance**, the older motivational tradition whose findings she reinterpreted as constrained by prior attitudes.[^s1]
