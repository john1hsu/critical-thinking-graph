# Jonathan St B. T. Evans

Jonathan St B. T. Evans is a British cognitive psychologist who has spent his career on how people reason, and why their reasoning departs from logic in systematic ways. He was Professor of Cognitive Psychology at the University of Plymouth from 1985 to 2009, directed its Centre for Thinking and Language, headed its School of Psychology, and has been emeritus professor since 2009.[^s1] He helped found the journal *Thinking & Reasoning* and edited it from 1995 to 2011.[^s1][^s5] For critical thinkers his importance is twofold: he supplied much of the evidence that belief and surface features distort deduction, and he was a leading architect of the dual-process theory built on those findings.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Evans entered the field through Peter Wason's reasoning tasks. In his own 2008 review he credits the term "dual processes" to a 1975 paper he wrote with Wason. That paper explained *matching bias* on Wason's four-card selection task. People chose cards that matched the terms named in the rule, showed no awareness of doing so, and then offered other, logical-sounding reasons for their picks.[^s2] The gap between what drove the choice and the reasons people gave became the seed of a two-process account.[^s2]

In 1983 Evans, Barston and Pollard published the experiments that made **belief bias** the standard test case. They crossed validity with believability in syllogisms, controlled for rival explanations, and found that believable conclusions were accepted far more often, with the effect largest for invalid arguments.[^s4] Evans later called belief bias the paradigm case of dual processes, because time pressure and working-memory load raise belief-based answers while cutting logical ones.[^s2]

## How it works

Evans's ideas came in three steps. His **heuristic-analytic theory** (1989) held that preconscious heuristics first select which features of a problem seem relevant, and analytic reasoning then works only on that selection. Biases arise when relevant information is left out or irrelevant information is let in.[^s2] A 2006 revision allowed heuristic answers to control behaviour unless analytic reasoning steps in, the default-interventionist view.[^s2]

The third step was a cleanup of terminology. In 2008 he argued that a generic "System 1 versus System 2" story bundles together attributes that do not hang together, and proposed talking of Type 1 and Type 2 processes instead, with Type 2 defined by its reliance on limited working memory.[^s2] With Keith Stanovich in 2013 he narrowed the defining features to autonomy for Type 1 and cognitive decoupling for Type 2, treating the rest as mere correlates.[^s3]

*Workplace example.* A team lead reviews a proposal whose conclusion she already favours: "every vendor we kept had good references; this vendor has good references; so we should keep it." Evans's work predicts she will rate the argument as sound because the conclusion is welcome, not because it follows. The research points to practical checks: give the judgment time, since speed raises belief-based answers, and reason forward from the premises rather than back from the conclusion.[^s2][^s4]

## Reception and critique

Evans's dual-process framework is influential but disputed. In a 2013 commentary, Magda Osman questioned the proposed defining features, arguing the evidence may reflect quantitative differences rather than two distinct kinds of process.[^s6] Evans and Stanovich replied to five standard criticisms, including vague definitions, misaligned attribute lists and the claim that a continuum fits the data better.[^s3] Evans himself has been among the sharpest critics of the popular "received view," rejecting the idea that fast processes always cause bias and slow ones always reason correctly.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Studied in cognitive psychology,** where his Plymouth research covered deduction, mental models, decision making and dual processes.[^s1]
- **Introduced dual-process theory** in reasoning, with Peter Wason, through their 1975 account of matching bias.[^s2]
- **Introduced the heuristic-analytic theory** of reasoning in 1989.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Peter Wason,** whose selection task framed his early work.[^s2]
- **Related to belief bias,** the Evans, Barston and Pollard experiments, matching bias and the selection task.[^s2][^s4]
