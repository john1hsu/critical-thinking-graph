# Stephen Toulmin

Stephen Edelston Toulmin (1922-2009) was a British-born philosopher whose work spanned the philosophy of science, ethics, the history of ideas and, most influentially for critical thinking, the theory of argument.[^s1][^s2] His 1958 book *The Uses of Argument* attacked the assumption that any serious argument can be recast as a formal deduction, and the layout of argument it proposed became one of the most widely taught frameworks for analysing everyday reasoning.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Toulmin was born in London on 25 March 1922 and took a first degree in mathematics and physics at Cambridge in 1942, then worked as a junior scientific officer for the Ministry of Aircraft Production during the Second World War.[^s1][^s3] Returning to Cambridge, he earned a doctorate in moral sciences in 1948, studying under John Wisdom and Ludwig Wittgenstein; the USC archive describes him as a disciple of Wittgenstein.[^s1][^s3] He taught philosophy of science at Oxford from 1949, chaired the philosophy department at Leeds (1955-59) and directed a history-of-ideas unit at the Nuffield Foundation in the early 1960s.[^s2][^s3]

He then built a long American career at Brandeis, Michigan State, UC Santa Cruz, Chicago's Committee on Social Thought (1973-86), Northwestern and finally the University of Southern California, which he joined in 1993 and where he became University Professor in 2001.[^s1][^s3] He gave the National Endowment for the Humanities' Jefferson Lecture in 1997, titled "A Dissenter's Story," and the Gifford Lectures in Edinburgh in 2005.[^s3][^s7] He died in Los Angeles on 4 December 2009.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

Toulmin's contribution to critical thinking is a way of looking at arguments rather than a single rule. In *The Uses of Argument* he called for an "applied logic" that would judge arguments by how they actually work in practice, using comparative and historical methods instead of the abstract criteria of mathematical logic.[^s5] He separated the general layout of an argument, which he took to be the same in every field (data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal, claim), from the standards used to appraise it, which he held to be field-dependent because the backing for warrants differs in law, medicine, science or ethics.[^s5] He also treated warrants as licences to infer rather than hidden premises, and insisted that real conclusions are usually qualified and open to rebuttal.[^s5]

A workplace case shows the outlook. Suppose a facilities manager argues that an office lease should not be renewed because occupancy sensors show desks used 30 percent of the time. A purely deductive reading asks whether the conclusion follows necessarily, and it does not. A Toulmin-style reading asks what warrant connects low occupancy to non-renewal, what backing supports that warrant in property management, and which rebuttals (a planned hiring wave, a cheap break clause) would defeat it. The argument is judged by the standards of its own field, not by whether it is a valid syllogism.[^s5]

Looking back in the 2003 preface, Toulmin said his aim had been strictly philosophical: to challenge a Platonic and Cartesian tradition that took Euclidean demonstration as the model for all rigorous reasoning. He added that it was communication scholars, not he, who turned his layout into "the Toulmin model."[^s4] His later books *Cosmopolis* (1990) and *Return to Reason* (2001) extended the same theme, defending situated reasonableness against abstract rationality.[^s4][^s5]

The same preference for practical, case-based judgment runs through his ethics. With the bioethicist Albert Jonsen he wrote *The Abuse of Casuistry* (1988), a history of reasoning about moral problems case by case, tracing casuistry from antiquity to its height in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries and its later fall into disrepute.[^s6] Britannica notes that his early ethics treated moral language as a distinct mode of reasoning about duties and rights, irreducible to subjective or objective facts.[^s2]

## Reception and limitations

Philosophers were at first largely hostile to *The Uses of Argument*, but scholars of jurisprudence and psychology found it fitted their fields, US speech-communication textbooks adopted the layout, and it later became a post-war classic of informal logic.[^s5] Researchers in artificial intelligence and law adapted it for decision-support systems, building on his points that premises play different roles, that arguments are defeasible and that criteria are field-dependent.[^s5] The most persistent criticism is that field-dependent standards slide into relativism; several later theorists have tried to rescue his account from that charge, while Toulmin himself claimed no finality for his ideas.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced the Toulmin model** in **The Uses of Argument** (1958); the reasoner records this from the model's own article.[^s4][^s5]
- **Studied in argumentation theory**, where the book is treated as a founding text of the modern field and of informal logic.[^s5]
- **Studied in philosophy of science**, which he taught at Oxford and pursued in books such as *Foresight and Understanding* (1961) and *Human Understanding* (1972).[^s1]
- **Influenced by Ludwig Wittgenstein**, under whom he studied at Cambridge.[^s1][^s3]
- **Contrasts with deductive reasoning**: his work was explicitly a critique of treating all serious argument as formal deduction.[^s4]
- **Related: casuistry**, the case-based moral reasoning he and Jonsen historicised and helped revive.[^s6]
