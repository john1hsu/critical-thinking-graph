# Richard E. Nisbett

Richard E. Nisbett is an American social psychologist who spent most of his career at the University of Michigan. Three of his research lines matter directly for critical thinking: the limits of introspection, the teachability of statistical reasoning, and cultural differences in how people reason.[^s1] His biography lists a psychology degree from Tufts in 1962, a Columbia PhD in social psychology in 1966, five years at Yale, and a move to Michigan in 1971. There he became Theodore M. Newcomb Distinguished University Professor and co-directed the Culture and Cognition Program.[^s1] His books include *Human Inference* (1980, with Lee Ross) and *Rules for Reasoning* (1992). His honours include the American Psychological Association's Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award (1991) and election to the National Academy of Sciences (2002).[^s1]

## Origin

Nisbett trained as a social psychologist, and his early work, such as *The Actor and the Observer* (1971, with E. E. Jones), asked why people explain their own behaviour differently from other people's.[^s1] That interest in everyday causal explanation led to his best-known paper. In "Telling more than we can know" (1977), Nisbett and Timothy Wilson argued that people may have little or no direct introspective access to the processes behind their judgments and choices.[^s2] When asked why they acted, people seem to draw on plausible causal theories, the same theories an outsider would use, and are right only when such a theory happens to fit.[^s2]

## How it works

**Limits of self-report.** In one study Nisbett and Wilson cite, shoppers chose among four identical pairs of stockings. The rightmost pair was preferred by nearly four to one over the leftmost, yet no shopper mentioned position and most denied it when asked.[^s2] In another, observers who only read a description of an experiment predicted its effects about as well as the participants explained their own behaviour. That suggests both groups used the same shared theories rather than inner observation.[^s2] A workplace version is a hiring panel that sincerely says a candidate won on "communication skills" when the order of interviews or a first impression may have done more. The practical lesson for critical thinkers is to test stated reasons against the record, such as order effects or score changes, instead of treating confident explanations as evidence of what drove a decision.[^s2]

**Teaching reasoning rules.** With Geoffrey Fong and David Krantz, Nisbett tested a "formalist" view: people hold abstract, domain-free rules such as an intuitive law of large numbers, so teaching the rule should improve reasoning across topics.[^s3] In their 1986 *Cognitive Psychology* paper, 347 adults and high-school students received brief training. Statistical answers to everyday problems rose from 42% in controls to 64% with combined rule and example training. Training built on examples from one kind of problem carried over to other kinds.[^s3] A telephone survey disguised as a sports poll found that, on one problem, statistical answers rose from 16% to 37% over an introductory statistics course.[^s3] This work grounds what the graph calls statistical reasoning training. It also cut against the older belief that reasoning skills are too tied to specific content to be taught in general form.[^s3]

**Culture and thought.** In a 2001 *Psychological Review* paper, Nisbett, Kaiping Peng, Incheol Choi and Ara Norenzayan proposed two systems of thought. Holistic cognition, which they reported as more common among East Asians, attends to the whole field, explains events by context and reasons dialectically, tolerating contradiction. Analytic cognition, more common among Westerners, focuses on objects and their categories, explains behaviour by rules, and uses formal logic to avoid contradiction.[^s4] They traced the difference to social systems and argued it challenges the assumption that basic cognitive processes are universal.[^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

The training studies show real but partial gains. Even after training, many answers stayed non-statistical, and two of four problems in the course study showed no improvement.[^s3] The introspection thesis has been strongly debated. In a 2014 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* review, Ben Newell and David Shanks argued that weak tests of awareness, unexcluded artifacts in landmark studies and uncritical acceptance of intuitive findings had inflated claims about unconscious influences on decisions.[^s5] The cultural account rests on group-level contrasts that the 2001 authors present as tendencies, not fixed traits of individuals.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced statistical reasoning training.** The 1986 experiments by Fong, Krantz and Nisbett are the founding demonstration that brief rule training improves everyday statistical reasoning.[^s3]
- **Authored "Telling more than we can know".** The 1977 paper with Wilson made the unreliability of self-reported reasons a standard caution in judgment research.[^s2]
- **Introduced holistic versus analytic cognition.** The 2001 paper set out the distinction between holistic and analytic systems of thought.[^s4]
- **Studied in social psychology.** His training, appointments and early attribution work were in social psychology.[^s1]
- **Challenged by Newell and Shanks (2014).** Their review questions how strong the evidence for unconscious influences on decisions is, a line of argument his 1977 paper helped start.[^s2][^s5]
- **Related** to debiasing, the heuristics-and-biases tradition and the fundamental attribution error through *Human Inference* and his attribution work.[^s1]
