# Theaetetus

The *Theaetetus* is the dialogue in which Plato asks, more directly than anywhere else, "what is knowledge?"[^s1] Socrates questions a gifted young mathematician, Theaetetus, who offers three answers in turn: knowledge is perception, knowledge is true judgement, and knowledge is true judgement together with an account (*logos*).[^s1][^s2] Each is examined and rejected, and the dialogue ends in *aporia*, a confessed failure to reach a definition.[^s1] For critical thinkers it is the founding text on a question every evidence-based discipline still faces: what separates being right from knowing.[^s4]

## Origin

Plato probably wrote the dialogue around 369 BCE; scholars usually place it before the *Sophist* and the *Statesman*, and often after the *Parmenides*.[^s1][^s2] A frame opens it: Euclides of Megara has a record of a conversation that Socrates held with Theaetetus, introduced by his geometry tutor Theodorus, shortly before Socrates' trial in 399 BCE.[^s1] The SEP reads the frame as a tribute to the real Theaetetus and links it most plausibly to his return from the battle of Corinth in 369 BCE.[^s1] The closing line has Socrates leaving to meet his accuser Meletus at the porch of the King Archon.[^s3]

## How it works

Socrates describes himself as a midwife of ideas: he produces no theories of his own but helps others deliver theirs and then tests whether each offspring is sound.[^s1][^s3] The SEP calls this one of the most detailed descriptions of the maieutic method in Plato.[^s1]

The first definition, knowledge as perception, is tied to Protagoras' doctrine that each person is the measure of what is true for them.[^s1][^s2] Socrates' best-known reply turns the doctrine on itself: if every belief is true, then the belief of Protagoras' critics that he is wrong is true as well.[^s2] Scholars still argue over whether this *peritropē* proves strict contradiction or only self-defeat.[^s1]

The second definition, knowledge as true judgement, collapses with a law-court example. Skilled speakers can persuade a jury to a true verdict about events that only an eyewitness could know, so the jurors judge truly yet do not know.[^s2][^s3] Before reaching that point the dialogue spends long passages on how false belief is possible at all, using the images of the mind as a wax tablet and as an aviary of captured birds.[^s1][^s2]

The third definition adds an account to true judgement.[^s3] Socrates tries three readings of "account": putting a belief into words, listing a thing's elements, and naming the mark that sets it apart from everything else. The first is too weak, the second can be done without understanding, and the third either repeats the true belief it was meant to upgrade or presupposes the knowledge being defined.[^s1][^s2]

A workplace version of the jury case shows why the distinction matters. A team lead is told by a confident colleague that last night's outage was caused by a certificate expiry. The colleague happens to be right, but the lead has seen no logs. Her belief is true, yet she could not defend it if challenged, and the same confidence would have carried a wrong answer just as easily. The dialogue's lesson is that a correct conclusion reached without grounds is not yet knowledge.[^s2][^s4]

## Influence and limits

The SEP entry on the analysis of knowledge says the need for a justification condition is often traced to this dialogue, where Socrates shows that true opinion is in general insufficient for knowledge.[^s4] Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper, which attacked the justified-true-belief analysis, notes in its first footnote that Plato seems to consider such a definition at *Theaetetus* 201.[^s5] The same SEP entry warns that calling the analysis "traditional" is partly a convenient fiction, since it was mostly spelled out in the twentieth century by its critics.[^s4]

Interpretation is contested. Unitarian readers hold that the dialogue shows what knowledge is not, because it deliberately leaves out Plato's Forms; revisionist readers see it as evidence that Plato moved away from the Forms.[^s1] The SEP also argues that Plato accepts a version of the third definition if "account" means explanation, an answer to "why?"[^s1] The IEP traces later echoes in Descartes, in modern debates about relativism, and in Wittgenstein's reading of the dream theory as early logical atomism.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Authored by Plato.** It is one of his dialogues, written around 369 BCE.[^s1]
- **Introduces justified true belief** in its earliest form: true judgement plus an account, the ancestry the SEP and Gettier both point to.[^s4][^s5]
- **Introduces maieutics.** Socrates' self-portrait as a midwife of ideas is set out here in fuller detail than in any other dialogue.[^s1]
- **Studied in epistemology.** It is still a standard starting point for the analysis of knowledge.[^s4]
- **Related to the Gettier problem**, whose target definition Gettier traces back to this text.[^s5]
