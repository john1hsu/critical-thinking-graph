# Posterior Analytics

The *Posterior Analytics* is Aristotle's treatise on how an argument can produce knowledge rather than mere agreement. Its *Prior Analytics* companion asks when a conclusion follows from premises. The *Posterior Analytics* asks a further question: when does following from premises amount to *knowing* the conclusion? Aristotle's answer is the theory of demonstration (*apodeixis*), a deduction whose premises meet strict conditions of truth, priority and explanation.[^s2][^s3] It is the earliest sustained source for two ideas that critical thinkers still use daily: a correct form is not enough, because the premises must also be true, and justification cannot go back forever.[^s4]

## Origin

Aristotle wrote the work in two books in the fourth century BCE, around 350 BCE in the traditional dating. The standard English translation used online is G. R. G. Mure's.[^s1] Later editors grouped it with his other logical treatises in the collection known as the *Organon*, where it is the text on the nature of scientific knowledge.[^s3] It opens with the principle that all teaching and learning by argument starts from knowledge the learner already has. Aristotle then asks where that prior knowledge ultimately comes from.[^s5]

## How it works

**Demonstration versus deduction.** In Book I, chapter 2, Aristotle says we know something scientifically when we know its cause and know that it could not be otherwise. A demonstration is a deduction that produces this knowledge. Its premises must be true, primary and indemonstrable, better known than and prior to the conclusion, and causes of it. A deduction can exist without these conditions, Aristotle notes, but then it is no demonstration.[^s5] Commentators stress that "better known" means better known in itself, not merely learned first, and that the list has been read in many ways.[^s2] The separation of correct form from true, well-grounded premises is the ancestor of the modern distinction between a valid argument and a sound one.[^s2][^s5]

**The regress problem.** Chapter 3 confronts two rival positions. One says knowledge is impossible: every premise needs an earlier premise, so the chain is either infinite or rests on unproved starting points that are not really known. The other says everything can be proved, because proofs may run in a circle. Aristotle rejects both. Circular proof fails because a premise must be prior to and better known than what it proves; a circle ends up asserting only that if A is true, A is true. The regress, he argues, really does stop at immediate premises known in some way other than by demonstration.[^s5][^s2] The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats this as the classic argument for foundationalism: rule out the circle and the infinite chain, and basic beliefs remain.[^s4]

**Why, not just that.** Book II turns to definition and explanation. Aristotle distinguishes four questions: whether a thing is so, why it is so, whether it exists, and what it is. The "why" questions ask for the middle term of a deduction, and the middle term is the cause. His running example is the lunar eclipse: to ask why the moon is eclipsed is to ask for the cause that produces it.[^s6] Knowing a fact and knowing its reason are different achievements, and only the second counts as full scientific knowledge.[^s3]

**First principles.** The last chapter, II.19, explains how the starting points are known. Repeated perception leaves memories; many memories form experience; from experience a universal settles in the mind; induction brings it into view; and intellect (*nous*) grasps the principles themselves. Aristotle ranks it as more accurate than scientific knowledge.[^s6][^s3] The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that interpretations of II.19 vary widely and that merely asserting such a faculty would not by itself solve the regress.[^s2]

A workplace illustration shows why this still matters. An analyst argues: "All our enterprise clients renew; this client is an enterprise client; so it will renew." The deduction is fine. Aristotle would ask three more things. Is the first premise true? Is it better grounded than the conclusion, or was it inferred from last year's renewals? Does it explain renewal, or merely correlate with it? Asking where a premise's own support comes from, and refusing to let a conclusion secretly support itself, is the *Posterior Analytics* method in miniature.[^s5][^s4]

## Connections

- **Authored by Aristotle.** The treatise is Aristotle's, written in the fourth century BCE.[^s1]
- **Part of the Organon.** It is one of the six logical works later collected under that name, the one concerned with scientific knowledge.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with the Prior Analytics.** The *Prior Analytics* studies deduction in general. The *Posterior Analytics* adds the conditions under which a deduction becomes a demonstration that yields knowledge.[^s2]
- **Studied in epistemology.** Its regress argument is the standard historical starting point for foundationalism in the theory of justification.[^s4]
- **Origin of soundness.** Its demand for true, prior and explanatory premises, on top of a correct form, is the earliest version of the idea now called soundness.[^s5][^s2]
- **Related to induction.** Chapter II.19 gives induction from perception and experience the job of supplying the universals that demonstration starts from.[^s6][^s3]
