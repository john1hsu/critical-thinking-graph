# Douglas Walton

Douglas Neil Walton (1942-2020) was a Canadian philosopher who did more than almost anyone to turn the study of everyday argument into a working toolkit.[^s1] Over a career of more than fifty books and some four hundred papers he catalogued the recurring patterns people use to argue under uncertainty, paired each with the questions a critic should ask, and showed that many arguments textbooks brand as fallacies are often reasonable, becoming fallacious only when misused in a particular conversation.[^s1][^s2] His schemes are now used in critical-thinking teaching and in artificial-intelligence models of legal reasoning.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Walton was born in Hamilton, Ontario, took a BA at Waterloo in 1964 and a PhD at Toronto in 1972 under John Woods.[^s1] He taught at the University of Winnipeg from 1969 until he retired in 2008, then held the Assumption University Chair in Argumentation Studies at the University of Windsor and stayed on as a Distinguished Research Fellow at its Centre for Research in Reasoning, Argumentation and Rhetoric (CRRAR) until his death in January 2020.[^s1][^s2] Inspired by Charles Hamblin's 1970 critique of how logic textbooks treated fallacies, Woods and Walton spent the 1970s and 1980s analysing individual fallacies one by one, an effort later called the Woods-Walton approach.[^s1][^s5] Key books include *Informal Logic: A Handbook for Critical Argument* (1989), *Commitment in Dialogue* with Erik Krabbe (1995), *Argumentation Schemes for Presumptive Reasoning* (1996), *The New Dialectic* (1998) and *Argumentation Schemes* with Chris Reed and Fabrizio Macagno (2008), which collected 96 schemes.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

**Schemes plus critical questions.** Walton treated many everyday arguments as presumptive: they give a reason to accept a conclusion for now, open to defeat by new information. Each scheme comes with critical questions that act as its defeasibility conditions.[^s2][^s4] Some questions put the burden on the arguer to back an assumption; others put the burden on the challenger to show an exception.[^s2] The best-known example is argument from expert opinion, whose questions ask about the source's expertise, field, trustworthiness and consistency with other experts.[^s2]

**Argument from consequences.** In his 1996 book Walton set out the scheme in its now-standard form: if A is brought about, good (or bad) consequences will plausibly follow, so A should (or should not) be brought about. He paired it with three questions: how likely the consequences are, what evidence shows they will follow, and whether consequences of the opposite value were left out.[^s3] A workplace case: a manager argues that moving the team to a four-day week would hurt customer response times, so the change should be dropped. A critic using the scheme asks for the response-time data, how likely the slowdown really is, and whether gains such as lower turnover were weighed.[^s3] Later work distinguished positive and negative forms and treated fear appeals as a subtype of the negative one.[^s4]

**Dialogue types and fallacies.** With Krabbe, Walton sorted conversations into types such as persuasion, inquiry, negotiation, deliberation, information-seeking and eristic quarrel, each with its own goal and rules about commitments.[^s1][^s2] An argument becomes fallacious when it is used against the goal of the dialogue it sits in, often through an illicit shift from one type to another.[^s1] Walton's 2009 analysis applied this to consequences: weighing outcomes is fine when deciding what to do, but irrelevant when the dispute is about whether a factual or ethical claim is true.[^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

Walton's contribution is normative and conceptual rather than experimental, so its value is judged by usefulness and coherence, not replication.[^s5] His scheme lists grew large and overlapping, which led him and Macagno to propose a classification system in 2015; they note that students can still confuse neighbouring schemes, such as a slippery slope and a simple negative-consequences argument.[^s4] Much of his influence runs through formal and computational work, including the Carneades argument model he built with Thomas Gordon and Henry Prakken.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced argument from consequences (as a scheme).** The positive/negative consequences pattern is ancient, but Walton's 1996 formulation and critical questions are the standard reference point; medium confidence reflects that he codified rather than discovered it.[^s3]
- **Introduced the dialogue-type typology.** Developed with Erik Krabbe in *Commitment in Dialogue* (1995).[^s1]
- **Influenced by Charles Hamblin.** Hamblin's *Fallacies* (1970) inspired the Woods-Walton programme.[^s1]
- **Influenced by John Woods.** His doctoral supervisor and long-time co-author on fallacies.[^s1][^s5]
- Formulated schemes for expert opinion, analogy and ad hominem used across this graph, and engaged with the pragma-dialectical school after 1986.[^s1][^s4]
