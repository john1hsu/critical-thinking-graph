# Iain Chalmers

Sir Iain Chalmers is a British health services researcher best known for turning the review of research evidence into a disciplined, reproducible method. After seven years as a clinician in the UK and the Gaza Strip, he became a full-time researcher. He was founding director of the National Perinatal Epidemiology Unit in Oxford (1978-1992) and then of the UK Cochrane Centre (1992-2002), which convened the meeting at which the Cochrane Collaboration was inaugurated. Since 2003 he has coordinated the James Lind Initiative, and he was knighted in 2000 for services to health care.[^s4]

## Origin

Chalmers traced his turn to evidence to his clinical work in Gaza, where he felt his training gave him no way of telling which treatments actually helped. Archie Cochrane's 1972 book on randomized trials gave him a direction; Cassels reports that he likened it to finding a compass in the jungle.[^s2] From the mid-1970s Chalmers and colleagues set out to find every controlled trial in pregnancy and childbirth. They searched databases, hand-searched journals and, before the internet, wrote to some 60,000 obstetricians and paediatricians to locate unpublished work.[^s2]

The result was *Effective Care in Pregnancy and Childbirth* (1989), a two-volume work edited by Chalmers, Murray Enkin and Marc Keirse with more than 100 contributors, kept current by the electronic Oxford Database of Perinatal Trials.[^s3] Sheble's 2017 bibliometric history of research synthesis credits this project with advocating and demonstrating the benefit of a systematic approach to reviewing evidence just before the Cochrane Collaboration and the evidence-based medicine movement took shape.[^s1] Cochrane's Editor in Chief later described these works as the model from which the Collaboration developed; its pregnancy and childbirth group was the first review group when it began in 1992.[^s3]

## How it works

Chalmers's core idea is that a review of evidence is itself a piece of research and needs methods that guard against bias. The perinatal project did more than summarize favourite studies. It tried to find all relevant trials, published or not, appraise them and combine their results.[^s1][^s2] A 1992 study he co-authored with Antman, Lau and colleagues compared textbook advice on heart-attack treatment with cumulative syntheses of trials. It found that sound, lifesaving advice had reached textbooks more than a decade late.[^s1]

A workplace parallel shows why this matters. A team deciding whether a new onboarding process works might cite the two pilot sites that went well. Chalmers's approach asks first which pilots were run in total, including the ones nobody wrote up, and only then what they show together, the same move his team made when it chased unpublished trials.[^s2]

He later turned the same thinking toward wasted research. In a 2009 *Lancet* paper, defended in a 2016 blog post, Glasziou and Chalmers estimated that about 85% of health research effort is avoidably wasted. Their reasoning runs as follows: about half of trials are never fully published, about half of published reports cannot be used, and about half of studies have avoidable design flaws, so only about 12.5 in 100 survive all three filters.[^s6]

## Legacy and limitations

Chalmers widened the audience for these ideas from researchers to the public. The Informed Health Choices Key Concepts, published by Chalmers, Oxman and colleagues in 2018, list 36 concepts in three groups for judging claims about treatments, such as recognising an unreliable basis for a claim and asking whether comparisons are fair.[^s5] A randomized trial of over 10,000 Ugandan primary school children in 120 schools found that children could learn to apply 12 of the concepts. The authors also say any benefit beyond health remains uncertain and rests on indirect evidence.[^s5]

His role should not be overstated. Pooling of studies predates him, and Sheble's history treats systematic review as a methods innovation that spread through many fields and authors.[^s1] The 85% figure is a rough estimate, not a measurement: it treats the three sources of waste as roughly independent and leaves out other forms of waste.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Introduced systematic review (with colleagues).** Sheble credits the Oxford perinatal project that Chalmers led with demonstrating the systematic approach to review.[^s1]
- **Introduced the Informed Health Choices Key Concepts.** He is lead author of the framework for teaching people to assess treatment claims.[^s5]
- **Introduced the research-waste estimate.** With Glasziou he proposed the widely cited 85% figure.[^s6]
- **Authored *Effective Care in Pregnancy and Childbirth*.** He co-edited the two volumes with Enkin and Keirse.[^s3]
- **Influenced by Archie Cochrane.** Cochrane's book on randomized trials shaped his career, and Cochrane praised the perinatal reviews.[^s2]
- **Related to publication bias and suppressed evidence.** Seeking out unpublished trials was a direct response to the selective reporting that distorts the published record.[^s2][^s6]
