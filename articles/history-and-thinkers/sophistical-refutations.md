# Sophistical Refutations

*On Sophistical Refutations* is Aristotle's study of arguments that look like refutations but are not.[^s1][^s2] It is the sixth and last treatise of the *Organon* and works as a final section, or appendix, to the *Topics*.[^s3] Its catalogue of thirteen fallacies is the earliest systematic theory of fallacious argument. Later logic textbooks kept reproducing its divisions, often after the reasons behind them had been forgotten.[^s2][^s5] For critical thinkers it is where words like *begging the question*, *equivocation* and *false cause* first got a theory.[^s4]

## Origin

Aristotle wrote the work in the fourth century BCE, against the background of dialectical debate in Plato's Academy and of the paid teachers of eristic, or contentious argument.[^s1][^s3] He opens with an analogy: some reasonings are real and others only seem real, just as some metals only look like gold or silver.[^s1] Sophists, he says, trade on that gap because they want to seem wise without being wise.[^s1]

In the closing chapter Aristotle claims to have had no predecessors. Rhetoric, he notes, had grown through generations of teachers. On reasoning, by contrast, nothing at all existed before him.[^s1] Teachers of eristic had given pupils set speeches to memorise. Aristotle compares this to handing someone shoes instead of teaching shoemaking. He asks readers to pardon the work's gaps and to be grateful for what it discovered.[^s1]

## How it works

The setting is a dialectical exchange. An answerer defends a thesis, and a questioner tries to extract answers from which the thesis's contradictory follows. That deduction is a refutation.[^s2][^s3] A sophistical refutation is one where either the deduction or the contradiction is only apparent.[^s2] Smith adds that some sophistical arguments are valid but use premises that only seem acceptable.[^s3]

Aristotle sorts apparent refutations into two families.[^s1][^s4] Six depend on language, and the Latin tradition called them *in dictione*: equivocation, amphiboly, combination, division, accent and form of expression.[^s4] Seven do not, called *extra dictionem*: accident, taking a qualified claim as unqualified (*secundum quid*), ignorance of what a refutation is (*ignoratio elenchi*), the consequent, begging the question, non-cause as cause, and many questions treated as one.[^s4][^s2] Chapter 6 then reduces all thirteen to failures to meet the definition of a refutation. Chapters 19 to 30 coach the answerer on how to resolve each kind.[^s1][^s2]

A workplace exchange shows one of them. Suppose a manager argues: "Our policy is to approve every reasonable expense. Is a client dinner reasonable? Then this client dinner at the most expensive restaurant in town must be approved." This is Aristotle's *secundum quid*. A claim that holds in one respect, that dinners of that kind are reasonable, is taken to hold without qualification.[^s1] Aristotle's own example is an Indian who is black but has white teeth: he is not, without qualification, both white and not white.[^s1] The fix he recommends is the answerer's: say exactly in what respect the concession was made.[^s1]

Aristotle also says why the study is worth doing. It shows how many senses a term can have. Someone easily misled by another person's argument will mislead themselves the same way. And a person who objects to an argument but cannot locate the flaw looks merely awkward.[^s1]

## Influence and limits

Hamblin's history traces how medieval logicians such as William of Sherwood developed the list, and how later writers added the *ad*-fallacies.[^s6] In *Fallacies* (1970), Charles Hamblin attacked the textbook "standard treatment" as debased and dogmatic. He went back to Aristotle's list, arguing that a muddled tradition had to be understood from its source, and proposed dialectic as the proper home for fallacy theory.[^s6][^s2] Evans argued in 1975 that later books followed Aristotle's divisions long after the logical and metaphysical reasons behind them had been dropped.[^s5]

The work's concept of a fallacy is not the modern one. Following Hintikka, Plantin argues that several Aristotelian fallacies are not bad inferences at all. They are methodological mistakes in a rule-governed question-and-answer game. Accident, for instance, is an error about definition, and *ignoratio elenchi* is a misunderstanding of what is in dispute.[^s4] This dialectical reading fed into pragma-dialectics, which treats a fallacy as a violation of a rule for critical discussion.[^s4] Hansen notes that many of Aristotle's examples turn on answers an answerer must stand by in dialogue, so the list does not map neatly onto one-way arguments in reports or articles.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

- **Authored by Aristotle; part of the Organon.** It is the sixth of the logical works the commentators grouped together, and it reads as a closing section of the *Topics*.[^s3]
- **Introduces informal fallacy (as a theory).** Hansen treats it as the founding systematic account of fallacies, from which the modern category descends. Its language-independent fallacies are the ancestors of the modern informal fallacies.[^s2]
- **Introduces begging the question and *ignoratio elenchi*.** Both appear by name among the seven fallacies outside language.[^s1][^s4]
- **Influenced Charles Hamblin.** Hamblin's critique of the standard treatment rebuilt fallacy theory by returning to "Aristotle's list".[^s6]
- **Influenced pragma-dialectics.** Its dialectical framing underlies the view that fallacies break rules of a critical discussion.[^s4]
- **Studied in argumentation theory.** Scholars of argument continue to reconstruct how Aristotle codified false refutations.[^s5]
