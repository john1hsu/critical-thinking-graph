# Amos Tversky

Amos Tversky (1937-1996) was a cognitive psychologist whose experiments showed that people judging probabilities and making choices under uncertainty depart from the rules of probability and rational choice in systematic, predictable ways. Much of this work was done with Daniel Kahneman, and it reshaped how psychologists, economists, physicians and lawyers think about judgment.[^s1][^s2] For critical thinkers his legacy is a catalogue of specific, testable failure modes, plus the idea that these errors come from ordinary mental shortcuts rather than from carelessness.[^s3]

## Origin

Tversky was born in Haifa in 1937. He served as a paratroop officer in the Israeli army, earned a bachelor's degree at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem in 1961 and a doctorate at the University of Michigan in 1965.[^s1][^s2] He taught at Michigan, Harvard and the Hebrew University, spent 1970 at Stanford's Center for Advanced Study in the Behavioral Sciences, and joined the Stanford faculty in 1978, where he later helped found the Stanford Center on Conflict and Negotiation.[^s1][^s2] He received a MacArthur fellowship in 1984 and was elected a foreign associate of the US National Academy of Sciences in 1985. He died of metastatic melanoma in June 1996, at 59.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

Tversky's contributions can be grouped into four lines of work.

**Heuristics and biases.** In their 1974 *Science* paper, Tversky and Kahneman argued that people judge uncertain quantities with three shortcuts: representativeness (judging by resemblance), availability (judging by how easily examples come to mind) and adjustment from an anchor. These shortcuts are economical and usually serviceable, but they generate systematic errors.[^s3] That paper is the origin of the anchoring effect and of the research program that names many of the biases in this graph.[^s3]

**Prospect theory.** In 1979 the pair published in *Econometrica* a descriptive alternative to expected utility theory. People evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point rather than as final wealth. Losses weigh more than equal gains. People are risk-averse for sure gains and risk-seeking for sure losses, and they overweight small probabilities.[^s4] The Nobel committee cited this work when it gave Kahneman the 2002 economics prize, noting that he had developed prospect theory together with Tversky, who had died in 1996.[^s7]

**Framing.** Tversky showed that logically equivalent descriptions can lead to different choices. A familiar example from his work: a price gap presented as a cash discount is accepted more readily than the same gap presented as a credit-card surcharge. With physician Barbara McNeil, he found that describing a treatment's risks in survival or mortality terms shifted patients' and doctors' preferences.[^s1]

**The conjunction fallacy.** In the 1983 "Linda" studies, participants read a description of a socially engaged philosophy graduate and judged "bank teller and feminist" more probable than "bank teller" alone. In one version, 85% of 142 undergraduates ranked the conjunction higher, violating the rule that a conjunction cannot be more probable than either of its parts.[^s5]

A workplace illustration of the framing line: a team asked to approve a project that has "a 70% chance of success" is often more willing than a team told it has "a 30% chance of failure." The two descriptions convey the same information, but they produce different decisions.[^s1][^s4]

## Reception and critique

Gerd Gigerenzer argued in 1996 that the program judged answers against narrow statistical norms that ignore context and the everyday meaning of words like "probable." He also argued that its heuristics were too vague to predict when they would operate.[^s6] Kahneman and Tversky replied that he misrepresented their positions. In their view, base-rate neglect, conjunction errors and overconfidence persist in frequency formats too, and the disagreement is mainly about which norms count as rational.[^s8] The exchange is a standard reference point in the debate over whether human reasoning is fundamentally flawed or adapted to its environment.[^s6][^s8]

## Connections

- **Studied in** judgment and decision making, the field the MacArthur Foundation credits him with shaping through his studies of inference, prediction and choice under uncertainty.[^s2]
- **Applied in** behavioral economics, through prospect theory and the Nobel-recognized integration of psychology into economics.[^s4][^s7]
- **Challenged by** Gerd Gigerenzer, whose "narrow norms and vague heuristics" critique targeted the heuristics-and-biases program.[^s6]
- **Related to** Daniel Kahneman, his principal collaborator, and to the heuristics-and-biases program, the anchoring effect, the representativeness and availability heuristics, the framing effect, the conjunction fallacy and prospect theory, all of which came out of their joint work.[^s3][^s4][^s5]
