# Sarah Lichtenstein

Sarah Lichtenstein was an American psychologist who helped create the field of behavioral decision research. Two of her contributions became standard tools for anyone who thinks about judgment: the discovery that people's preferences can reverse depending on how they are asked, and the measurement of whether people's stated confidence matches how often they are right.[^s2][^s3] In 1976 she co-founded the Eugene, Oregon research institute Decision Research with Paul Slovic and Baruch Fischhoff.[^s1]

## Origin

Lichtenstein and Slovic both trained under Ward Edwards, whose 1954 and 1961 reviews of decision theory set the agenda for their research.[^s7] By the mid-1970s she was at the Oregon Research Institute in Eugene, where she wrote her early calibration work with Fischhoff.[^s3] With Slovic and Fischhoff she wrote the 1977 *Annual Review of Psychology* chapter on behavioral decision theory, which surveyed more than 1,000 publications from 1971 to 1975 and cited 319 of them. Slovic later attributed its wide use to the many new findings it reported that challenged existing theories.[^s7]

## How it works

**Preference reversals.** In three experiments published in 1971, Lichtenstein and Slovic asked people to choose between pairs of bets, one with a high chance of winning a modest sum and one with a lower chance of a larger prize, and then to state a price for each bet separately. People often chose the safer bet but put a higher price on the long shot.[^s2] The authors argued that this pattern breaks the assumptions of standard models of risky choice such as expected utility. Their explanation was about information processing: when pricing, people start from the amount to win and adjust downward, while choosing has no such starting point.[^s2] Decades later, Lichtenstein and Slovic edited *The Construction of Preference* (2006), which argued that people often build preferences while answering, rather than reading off fixed ones. On this view, reversals across logically equivalent methods such as choosing and pricing violate procedure invariance.[^s6]

A workplace version: a hiring panel ranks two candidates head to head and prefers the steady performer, but when each candidate is scored separately on a salary-offer scale, the one with a single standout achievement gets the higher number. The method of asking, not the candidates, has changed the answer.[^s2][^s6]

**Calibration.** In a 1976 report for DARPA and the Office of Naval Research, Lichtenstein, Fischhoff and Lawrence Phillips defined a judge as well calibrated when, across many judgments, the share that come true matches the probability assigned. Someone who says 80% should be right about 80% of the time; being right only 70% of the time signals overconfidence.[^s3] They traced the idea to weather forecasting, including Cooke's 1906 confidence-weighted forecasts and Brier's 1950 scoring rule, and then reviewed the psychological evidence.[^s3] They concluded that weather forecasters were generally well calibrated while most other judges were not. Overconfidence was common, and people were overconfident on hard items and underconfident on easy ones. For uncertain quantities, 20-50% of true values fell outside ranges that should have missed only 2%.[^s3] An updated review, "Calibration of probabilities: The state of the art to 1980", appeared as a chapter in the 1982 volume *Judgment under Uncertainty*, where it became a standard reference.[^s4]

**Risk perception.** In a 1978 study with Slovic, Fischhoff and others, Lichtenstein found that people's estimates of how often various causes of death occur were consistent with one another but systematically off: rare causes were overestimated and common ones underestimated. The authors linked these errors to exposure, memorability and imaginability, and found that people could not correct them even when warned.[^s5]

## Evidence and limitations

Lichtenstein's findings rest on experiments with people judging bets, general-knowledge questions and frequencies; how far they carry over to experts working in their own domain varies, and the 1976 review itself noted that weather forecasters were an exception and that evidence on training was thin.[^s3] Preference reversals became one of the most discussed anomalies in decision theory, and the 2006 volume collects later work from psychology, economics, marketing and law that tested and extended them.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Introduced calibration research in psychology.** The 1976 report defined calibration for probability judges and set out the evidence that later work built on; the concept itself came from meteorology, so the attribution is to its psychological study.[^s3][^s4]
- **Introduced preference reversals.** Her 1971 experiments with Slovic first reported the choice-versus-pricing reversal.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Ward Edwards.** She and Slovic were Edwards's students, and his reviews shaped their interests.[^s7]
- **Studied in judgment and decision making.** Her reviews, experiments and the institute she co-founded made her part of the field's founding generation.[^s1][^s7]
- **Related to Baruch Fischhoff, Paul Slovic, the overconfidence effect and the availability heuristic.** Her calibration and lethal-events work with them documented overconfidence and frequency misjudgments tied to how memorable events are.[^s3][^s5]
