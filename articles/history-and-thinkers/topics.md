# Topics

The *Topics* is Aristotle's handbook for dialectic: a structured debate in which one person defends a thesis and another tries to refute it using premises the defender will grant.[^s2][^s3] It is one of the six logical treatises later collected as the *Organon*, and *On Sophistical Refutations*, the first theory of fallacies, works as its closing section or appendix.[^s2] For critical thinkers the *Topics* matters because it treats argument as something people do with each other, and it offers practical advice on how to find, frame and test arguments, not only how to check their form.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

Aristotle wrote the *Topics* in the fourth century BCE against the background of the debating exercises practised in Plato's Academy. The Eleatics had argued dialectically before him, and Plato had theorised the short back-and-forth exchange that Socrates favoured.[^s3] The *Topics*, together with the *Sophistical Refutations*, is likely the first systematic account of how such exchanges should be conducted.[^s3] The *Topics* and the *Analytics* cross-refer often, and Aristotle's later syllogistic was built on this dialectical background.[^s2][^s3]

Book I opens by defining a deduction as an argument in which, once certain things are laid down, something else follows from them of necessity.[^s1] It then sorts arguments by their starting points. Demonstrations start from true and primary premises. Dialectical arguments start from opinions accepted by everyone, by most people, or by the wise (*endoxa*). Contentious arguments only appear to start from accepted opinions or only appear to follow.[^s1] Aristotle says the study is useful for three things: intellectual training, everyday encounters, and the philosophical sciences.[^s1]

## How it works

In the debating game, the answerer takes a position, and the questioner may only ask questions that can be answered yes or no. The questioner's aim is to collect concessions from which a contradiction of the thesis follows. The answerer's aim is to avoid being refuted.[^s2][^s3] Book I supplies the toolkit. It sorts every predication into four kinds, called the predicables: definition, property, genus and accident.[^s1][^s2] It also lists four means of getting arguments: securing premises, distinguishing the senses of a word, finding differences, and finding likenesses.[^s1] It also contrasts deduction with induction, which it describes as a move from individual cases to a universal.[^s1]

Most of Books II–VI is a collection of *topoi*, or "locations": general strategies for arguing about a given kind of claim, such as arguments from opposites or from "more and less".[^s2] Aristotle never defines a *topos*, and interpreters still disagree over what one is. The IEP reads them as recommended argumentative strategies rather than subject headings.[^s2][^s4]

A workplace example shows the method. A manager defends the thesis "the new onboarding course is the best way to train analysts". A colleague, as questioner, uses a strategy from opposites and the "more and less" family. First she asks whether the best training gives the most practice on real cases, and the manager agrees. Then she asks whether shadowing a senior analyst gives more practice on real cases than the course does. If the manager concedes that too, the thesis is refuted out of his own answers. The answerer's defence is to look for an ambiguity in "best", which matches Aristotle's second means: distinguishing senses.[^s1][^s2]

## Influence and limits

Aristotle's *Rhetoric* draws openly on the *Topics*, and Porphyry and later commentators expanded the four predicables into five.[^s2] Late ancient logicians, partly to meet the needs of Roman lawyers, turned the *topoi* into a system of topical argument. Boethius' treatise on topical differentiae (about 522–3) reconciled the lists of Cicero and Themistius, and twelfth-century logicians, Abelard among them, used it as a framework for building and analysing arguments.[^s6] Topical arguments in this tradition were usually plausible rather than formally valid, and their strength varied.[^s6] Walton, Reed and Macagno trace modern argumentation schemes back to this tradition. By one count the *Topics* holds roughly 300–400 *topoi*. Modern schemes mainly take over their role as warrants that license a conclusion, though they can also help an arguer find one.[^s5]

The work has limits. Dialectic shows what follows from what an opponent accepts, so it does not by itself prove a thesis true.[^s2] Its starting points are opinions, and the IEP notes that interpreters still dispute whether dialectic can reach first principles at all.[^s4][^s2]

## Connections

- **Authored by Aristotle and part of the Organon.** It is one of the six treatises that the commentators grouped together.[^s2]
- **Introduces argument (in the sense of a systematic account).** Its opening definition and its theory of dialectical exchange are the earliest surviving systematic treatment of arguing as a practice. Earlier dialecticians argued, but did not theorise it this way.[^s3][^s1]
- **Has part the Sophistical Refutations.** That treatise serves as the *Topics*' final section, on apparent refutations.[^s2]
- **Influenced argumentation schemes.** The *topoi* are the historical source of modern schemes and their warrant function.[^s5]
- **Contrasts with the Prior Analytics.** Dialectical and demonstrative deductions share one logical structure but differ in their premises: conceded opinions versus truths a demonstrator simply takes.[^s2]
- **Studied in argumentation theory.** Historians of argumentation place it at the start of the theory of arguing well in debate.[^s3]
