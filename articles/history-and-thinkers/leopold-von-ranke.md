# Leopold von Ranke

Leopold von Ranke (1795-1886) was a German historian, trained as a classical philologist, who is usually credited with turning history into a discipline built on the critical reading of sources.[^s2][^s3] For critical thinking his lasting contribution is a habit of mind: before believing an account of the past, ask who wrote it, whether that writer saw the events, and whether the account is original or copied from someone else.[^s1] That habit is the root of the modern distinction between primary and secondary sources.

## Origin

Ranke was born on 21 December 1795 in Wiehe, Thuringia, into a family of Lutheran pastors and lawyers, and studied theology and classics at the University of Leipzig, where he concentrated on philology, the translation and exposition of texts.[^s2] While teaching ancient languages at a secondary school in Frankfurt an der Oder (1818-1825) he turned to history, influenced by Barthold Georg Niebuhr, Walter Scott and Johann Gottfried Herder.[^s2]

In 1824 he published a history of the Latin and Germanic peoples from 1494, whose preface announced the aim of showing the past "wie es eigentlich gewesen" rather than judging it or instructing the present.[^s1] Its appendix, *Zur Kritik neuerer Geschichtschreiber* ("On the critique of modern historians"), argued that critically examining the tradition is the historian's basic task, and won him a post at the University of Berlin, where he taught from 1825 and held a full chair from 1834 to 1871.[^s1][^s2] He later mined Venetian ambassadors' reports and other unpublished documents, and wrote major histories of the popes, of Reformation Germany, of Prussia, France and England, and finally a world history begun at 82; he was ennobled in 1865.[^s2] Scholars still argue over the famous phrase: read as "what actually happened" it sounds like a call for colourless fact-listing, but Georg Iggers noted that *eigentlich* can also mean "essentially", so "how things really were" may be closer.[^s3]

## How it works

The 1824 appendix spends most of its length on Francesco Guicciardini's *Storia d'Italia*, then the standard history of Italy's wars.[^s1] Ranke applied a sequence of tests. First, was the author a witness? Guicciardini began his story in 1492, when he was ten, so his early chapters had no firsthand value; only after he entered Medici service in the 1510s did his account become evidence. Second, is the report original, and if it is borrowed, from whom and through what research? Third, does it hold up against internal evidence and other accounts, especially when compared line by line with the earlier texts it reproduces?[^s1] Ranke found speeches that Guicciardini had borrowed, altered or invented, and concluded he could not be trusted, while a less celebrated writer, Paolo Giovio, who relied on eyewitnesses, came out better.[^s1] He modelled the whole procedure on the philologist's reconstruction of ancient texts and called for historians who would travel to archives, something he had not yet done himself.[^s1]

A workplace parallel: a team is asked why a product launch failed and is handed a polished retrospective deck. Ranke's questions apply directly. Was the deck's author in the launch meetings? Which of its claims trace back to tickets, emails and dashboards from the time, and which repeat an earlier summary? Where the deck and the original records disagree, the records win, just as Ranke preferred contemporary witnesses over later retellings.[^s1]

From 1834 Ranke ran exercises in which students criticised primary sources on medieval Saxon rulers and assessed each other's papers.[^s1] Britannica credits him with establishing the first historical seminar, and his students, among them Georg Waitz and Jacob Burckhardt, carried the method to other universities until it became the standard training of professional historians.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

Ranke did not invent source criticism from nothing. Enlightenment scholars and the historical sceptics of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries had already doubted narrative historians and turned to "relics" such as charters and official records; Ranke's distinctive contribution was teaching historians how to read narrative sources critically.[^s1] His 1824 book did not found a school by itself, and its second volume never appeared.[^s1] His objectivity was also limited. His judgments of earlier historians were moral as well as technical, his political sympathies were conservative and pro-Prussian, and his focus on states and diplomacy left economic and social history thinly covered.[^s1][^s2] Later critics also accused him of copying earlier works and of writing a "victor's history".[^s3] Applying his own method to him is a useful exercise: a careful reader checks even a celebrated source.

## Connections

- **Introduced** the primary/secondary distinction in its methodological form: his 1824 tests separated firsthand witnesses from writers who merely repeated others.[^s1]
- **Introduced** the historical seminar, where students learned criticism by working on primary sources together.[^s2]
- **Authored** *Zur Kritik neuerer Geschichtschreiber* (1824), the appendix that set new standards for historical criticism.[^s1]
- **Influenced by** Barthold Georg Niebuhr, among the influences on his turn from philology to history.[^s2]
- **Studied in** historiography, where he is regarded as the founder of the "scientific" historical school.[^s3]
- **Related to** source credibility and lateral reading, which carry his questions about witnesses and borrowed reports into everyday evaluation of information.[^s1]
