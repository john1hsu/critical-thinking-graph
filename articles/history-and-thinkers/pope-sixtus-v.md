# Pope Sixtus V

Sixtus V (born Felice Peretti, 1520 or 1521, died 1590) was a Franciscan friar who became pope in April 1585 and reigned for just over five years.[^s1][^s5] He matters to the history of critical thinking for one institutional idea: he is credited with making organised opposition a required part of a high-stakes decision. The Catholic Encyclopedia dates to 1587, under Sixtus, the establishment of the Promoter of the Faith (*promotor fidei*), the official popularly called the devil's advocate, whose duty was to argue against people proposed for sainthood.[^s2]

## Origin

Peretti was born at Grottammare in the March of Ancona, joined the Franciscans as a boy and was ordained at Siena in 1547.[^s1][^s3] He built a reputation as a preacher, served with the Inquisition in Venice until his severity led to his recall in 1560, became a bishop in 1566 and a cardinal in 1570, and was known as Cardinal Montalto.[^s3] Elected on 24 April 1585, he took his name in honour of Sixtus IV, another Franciscan pope.[^s3][^s5]

Britannica places his lasting importance in his reform of the church's central administration, which historians see as key to putting the decrees of the Council of Trent into effect.[^s1] A 1586 bull capped the College of Cardinals at 70 members, and the constitution *Immensa aeterni Dei* of February 1588 set up 15 permanent congregations of cardinals, a structure that stayed largely intact until after the Second Vatican Council.[^s1][^s3] One of them, the Congregation of Rites, was charged with regulating worship and with the causes of saints.[^s4] He also completed the dome of St Peter's and replanned Rome's streets, while his edition of the Latin Vulgate was withdrawn for its errors after his death.[^s1][^s5]

## How it works

The devil's advocate turned scrutiny into a job description. As described in 1907, the Promoter of the Faith had to prepare, in writing, every objection to a candidate's cause, however slight, so that no one was honoured without proof; he also protested when required procedures were skipped and insisted that objections be answered.[^s2] Under Urban VIII in 1631 the promoter's presence, at least by deputy, became necessary for any act in a cause to be valid, so the critic could not simply be left out of the room.[^s2] The office therefore separated two roles that usually blur: the postulators argued for the candidate, and a sanctioned opponent argued against.

The attribution needs a caveat. The Catholic Answers version of the entry also notes that a promoter-like officer is first formally mentioned earlier, in a canonization under Leo X (1513-1521), so Sixtus is better described as the pope who formalised the office within his new congregational system than as its sole inventor.[^s7]

*Workplace example.* A hospital board is about to approve a costly new scheduling system. Borrowing the Sixtine design, the chair names one board member as "promoter of the record" for the decision: she must list in writing every weakness in the vendor's evidence, and the vote is invalid until her memo has been tabled and answered. The device works for the same reasons as the original office: the critic's role is official, written and impossible to skip.[^s2]

## Influence and limitations

The model outlived its setting. In 1977 Theodore Herbert and Ralph Estes proposed a "corporate devil's advocate" in the *Academy of Management Review*, citing the church's sainthood procedure as a precedent for formalised dissent that critiques decisions without attacking the people who made them.[^s6] By 1984 a review in the *Journal of Management Studies* could survey field and laboratory research on using devil's advocates in top-level organisational decisions, and the debate about their value.[^s8] The Congregation of Rites itself was split in 1969 into separate bodies for worship and for the causes of saints, the latter keeping an office of the Promoter General of the Faith, and John Paul II's 1983 reforms reorganised the procedure around a college of relators.[^s4]

Two limitations temper the story. The office was a church legal role, not a general theory of reasoning, and its duties were defined and extended by later popes such as Urban VIII rather than fixed by Sixtus alone.[^s2] The modern decision technique, likewise, was shaped by twentieth-century management researchers who treated the canonization procedure as an analogy, not a blueprint.[^s6][^s8]

## Connections

- **Introduced devil's advocacy (the institution).** The Catholic Encyclopedia credits Sixtus V with establishing the Promoter of the Faith in 1587, while noting an earlier mention under Leo X; hence medium confidence.[^s2]
- **Introduced the Congregation of Rites.** The Holy See's own history of the Congregation for the Causes of Saints dates its predecessor to Sixtus V's *Immensa aeterni Dei* of 1588.[^s4]
- **Influenced by the Council of Trent.** Britannica describes his curial reforms as the means of enforcing Trent's decrees, making him a founder of the Counter-Reformation.[^s1]
- **Related to** devil's advocacy as a modern group decision technique, which management writers explicitly modelled on the church office to improve executive decisions.[^s6] It is also loosely related to groupthink, the premature consensus that such formalised dissent aims to interrupt.
