# The Periodic Health Examination (1979 report)

*The Periodic Health Examination* is the first report of the Canadian Task Force on the Periodic Health Examination, published in the *Canadian Medical Association Journal* on 3 November 1979 (volume 121, pages 1193-1254).[^s6] It asked a practical question, which parts of a routine check-up actually prevent disease, and answered it by ranking the research behind each practice instead of polling experts.[^s2] Reviews of evidence-based medicine credit it as the place where levels of evidence were first set out.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The Task Force was created in September 1976 by the Conference of Deputy Ministers of Health of the ten Canadian provinces, with Walter O. Spitzer as its founding chair.[^s2] Between 1976 and 1978 it worked out a way to judge whether a preventive intervention works that, unusually for the time, put evidence ahead of consensus.[^s2] The 1979 report applied that method to 78 target conditions and named 21 research priorities.[^s3] Its headline advice was to drop the undefined "annual checkup" and replace it with age-specific packages of proven manoeuvres offered during ordinary visits.[^s2]

## How it works

The report ranked evidence by study design, from at least one properly randomised controlled trial at the top down to the opinions of respected authorities at the bottom, and graded each recommendation in five categories, from good evidence to include a manoeuvre in the check-up to good evidence to exclude it.[^s2] In the form later reproduced by the U.S. task force, level I is a properly randomised trial; II-1 a controlled trial without randomisation; II-2 a well-designed cohort or case-control study; II-3 multiple time series or dramatic uncontrolled results; and III expert opinion, descriptive studies, case reports or committee reports.[^s4]

The point of the ladder is that designs differ in how well they rule out alternative explanations, so a recommendation should say which rung it stands on. Workplace example: an employer considering annual on-site health screening for all staff would ask, test by test, whether any trial shows that screening healthy adults of that age improves outcomes, or whether the case rests only on clinicians' impressions; the report's method makes that difference visible in the grade itself.[^s2][^s4]

## Influence

The CMAJ later called the classification of research into levels and grades of recommendation an innovation that "persists to this day", and linked the report to the subsequent boom in clinical practice guidelines.[^s3] When the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force was set up in 1984 it adopted the Canadian method essentially unchanged, and the two groups shared chapters for years.[^s2] David Sackett expanded the levels in a 1989 paper on antithrombotic therapy, and later schemes, such as the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine levels, adapted the idea to other kinds of clinical question while keeping randomised trials at the top.[^s1]

## Limitations

The main criticism is that a ranking by design says nothing about how well a given study was done. The U.S. task force's 2001 methods review noted that the old hierarchy paid too little attention to internal validity and that a good cohort study can be more convincing than a weak or underpowered trial; it added good, fair and poor quality ratings and separated the size of a benefit from the quality of the evidence.[^s4] Swanson observed in 2013 that the level-of-evidence classification is itself a product of experience and expert opinion, the very kind of evidence it places lowest.[^s5] The renamed Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care itself now rates evidence with the GRADE approach.[^s7]

## Connections

The report **introduces** the **hierarchy of evidence**, the design-based ranking that Burns, Rohrich and Chung trace to it.[^s1] It **influenced** the **U.S. Preventive Services Task Force**, which took over its method,[^s2] and **David Sackett**, who expanded its levels in 1989.[^s1] It is **challenged by** the 2001 USPSTF methods review, which faulted design-only grading for ignoring study quality.[^s4] It places **randomised controlled trials** at the top and **expert opinion** at the bottom, and later grading systems, including the **GRADE approach** its own successor body adopted, refine rather than replace its idea.[^s1][^s7]
