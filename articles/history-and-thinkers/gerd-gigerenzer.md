# Gerd Gigerenzer

Gerd Gigerenzer is a psychologist who spent decades as a director at the Max Planck Institute for Human Development in Berlin and now directs the Harding Center for Risk Literacy at the University of Potsdam.[^s1][^s2] His work turns a common assumption around: instead of treating mental shortcuts as sources of error, he asks when simple rules that ignore information actually do as well as, or better than, elaborate calculation.[^s3] For critical thinkers he is the main counterweight to the heuristics-and-biases tradition, and a practical guide to presenting statistics so that people can reason with them.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Gigerenzer earned his doctorate in psychology at the University of Munich in 1977 and his habilitation there in 1982.[^s1] He later taught as a professor of psychology at the University of Chicago and directed the Center for Adaptive Behavior and Cognition (ABC), an affiliation the Harding Center lists with both the Max Planck Institute for Psychological Research in Munich and the Max Planck Institute for Human Development, where he is now director emeritus.[^s1][^s2] He has also held visiting posts at the University of Virginia's law and business schools, served on the European Research Council, and trained judges, physicians and managers in understanding risk.[^s1][^s2] His academic books include *Simple Heuristics That Make Us Smart* (1999), *Rationality for Mortals* (2008) and *Bounded Rationality* (2001, edited with Reinhard Selten); his popular books include *Calculated Risks*, *Gut Feelings* and *Risk Savvy*.[^s1]

## How it works

Three lines of work define his contribution.

**Fast-and-frugal heuristics.** In a 1996 *Psychological Review* paper, Gigerenzer and Daniel Goldstein built on Herbert Simon's idea of satisficing and proposed "Take The Best": to decide which of two options scores higher, check cues one at a time in order of validity and stop at the first cue that tells them apart.[^s3] A first step, the recognition principle, simply picks the option you recognise when you know only one of them.[^s3] In simulations on all 3,403 pairs of the 83 largest German cities, Take The Best judged which city was bigger about as accurately as multiple regression (roughly 69% at full knowledge) while looking up fewer than a third of the available cue values.[^s3] The authors presented this as an existence proof that successful reasoning need not satisfy classical norms, and argued that bounded rationality has an ecological side as well as a cognitive one, so a strategy should be judged by how well it fits the structure of real environments.[^s3]

**Natural frequencies.** With Ulrich Hoffrage, he showed in 1995 that people solve diagnostic problems much better when information is stated as counts ("8 of the 10 women with cancer test positive, and so do 95 of the 990 without") than as percentages and conditional probabilities.[^s4] In their first study, the share of Bayesian answers rose from 16% with probabilities to 46% with frequencies.[^s4] A workplace version: a fraud screen that flags 9 of 10 bad transactions and 50 of 1,000 good ones is easier to judge as "about 9 of every 59 flags are real" than as a 90% hit rate and a 5% false-alarm rate.[^s4]

**Critique of heuristics and biases.** In a 1996 exchange with Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, Gigerenzer argued that their program judged answers against narrow, content-blind statistical norms and relied on heuristics such as representativeness that were too vague to predict when they would operate.[^s5] He called instead for precise, testable models of the processes behind judgment.[^s5]

## Reception and limitations

His models have drawn sustained empirical scrutiny. Critics of the recognition heuristic showed that a high rate of choosing the recognised option does not prove people ignore other knowledge, and developed measures suggesting many people do not follow recognition strictly.[^s6] Thorsten Pachur of the University of Basel granted that these tests are an advance but argued in 2011 that the heuristic still compared well against rival models and that violations matter mainly when a better model is offered.[^s6] Readers can take from the debate that "heuristic" need not mean "bias": whether a shortcut helps or misleads is an empirical question about the task.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

- **Studied in** judgment and decision making: his research centres on bounded rationality, decisions under uncertainty and time pressure, and the strategies of managers, judges and physicians.[^s1]
- **Influenced by** Herbert Simon, whose satisficing and bounded rationality the 1996 Take The Best paper explicitly extends.[^s3]
- **Applied in** risk communication through the Harding Center and his training of judges, doctors and managers.[^s2]
- **Related to** fast-and-frugal heuristics, the recognition heuristic, bounded rationality and natural frequencies, which he co-developed with Goldstein and Hoffrage, and to the heuristics-and-biases program, Kahneman, Tversky and the representativeness heuristic, the targets of his critique.[^s3][^s4][^s5]
