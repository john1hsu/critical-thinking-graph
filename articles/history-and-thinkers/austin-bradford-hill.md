# Sir Austin Bradford Hill

Austin Bradford Hill (1897–1991) was an English medical statistician whose work shaped two questions every critical thinker meets: how to run a fair test of a treatment, and when an observed association is good enough evidence of cause. He had no degree in medicine or statistics; he studied economics as an external London University student while recovering from tuberculosis, later earning a PhD and DSc.[^s1] He is the person most closely tied to the modern randomized controlled trial and to the "Bradford Hill criteria" for causal inference.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Hill worked for the Medical Research Council (MRC) on industrial health, became reader in epidemiology and vital statistics at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine in 1933, and in 1945 took the chair of medical statistics and the directorship of the MRC's statistical research unit.[^s1] In 1937 he published a series of *Lancet* articles, later the book *Principles of Medical Statistics*, which taught doctors basic statistical reasoning; at that stage it recommended allocating patients alternately to treatment and control.[^s1][^s2] He was knighted in 1961, the year he retired, and became one of the first non-medical honorary fellows of the Royal College of Physicians.[^s1]

## How his ideas work

**Fair allocation.** According to the Royal College of Physicians, Hill persuaded MRC committees in 1946 to adopt randomisation, and the resulting 1948 streptomycin trial for tuberculosis is often credited as the first randomized trial.[^s1] Iain Chalmers' 2011 history stresses why: Hill had seen alternation break down in an earlier MRC study, where unequal group sizes showed the rule had not been followed, and the streptomycin team, which also included Marc Daniels and Philip D'Arcy Hart, sealed the allocation sequence in envelopes so clinicians could not foresee or steer the next assignment.[^s2] Chalmers argues that the common story deriving clinical trials from R. A. Fisher's statistical theory is unproven; the driving idea was the simpler one of an unbiased test.[^s2]

**Reasoning from observation.** Where experiments are impossible, Hill showed how far careful observation can go. In 1950 he and Richard Doll compared 709 lung-cancer patients from 20 London hospitals with 709 matched patients who had other diseases; non-smokers were far rarer among the cancer patients, and heavier smoking was more common.[^s5] They acknowledged that association alone did not prove causation but judged the alternative explanations unlikely, concluding that smoking was an important factor.[^s5]

**Nine viewpoints on causation.** In a 1965 presidential address to the Royal Society of Medicine's section of occupational medicine, Hill listed nine aspects of an association to weigh before calling it causal: strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment and analogy.[^s3][^s4] He presented them as aids to judgment, not rules: none is strictly required, smoking itself shows low specificity, and significance tests say only how far chance might explain a result, not whether an effect matters.[^s4]

A workplace example: a team notices that sites using a new safety app report fewer injuries. Hill's questions ask whether the drop is large (strength), whether it recurs at other sites and times (consistency), whether the app came before the decline (temporality), whether heavier use goes with fewer injuries (gradient), and whether a pilot that assigned the app to randomly chosen sites (experiment) shows the same thing.[^s2][^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

Hill's criteria remain widely used, but they predate modern genetics, molecular biology and exposure science. Fedak and colleagues (2015) argue that each criterion now needs reinterpreting when evidence comes from many data types, such as biomarkers, epigenetics and mechanistic toxicology.[^s6] Hill himself warned against treating the list as a checklist and noted that waiting for complete evidence can be a decision too, since incomplete knowledge may still justify action.[^s4] The priority claim for the randomized trial is also contested: fair comparisons by alternation are far older, and Fisher promoted random allocation in agriculture by 1935.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced the randomized controlled trial (in clinical medicine).** He pushed the MRC to adopt random allocation, leading to the 1948 streptomycin trial.[^s1]
- **Introduced the Bradford Hill criteria,** his 1965 viewpoints for moving from association to causation.[^s3][^s4]
- **Authored *Principles of Medical Statistics*,** first published as 1937 *Lancet* articles.[^s1][^s2]
- **Related to correlation versus causation, confounding and selection bias,** the problems his allocation methods and causal viewpoints were designed to handle, and to significance testing, whose limits he flagged.[^s2][^s4]
- **Related to inference to the best explanation.** His smoking work argued that causation was the most credible explanation once alternatives were judged unlikely.[^s5]
