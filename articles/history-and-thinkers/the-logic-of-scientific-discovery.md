# The Logic of Scientific Discovery

*The Logic of Scientific Discovery* is Karl Popper's first detailed account of how science works and how it should work. Its central claim is that what separates empirical science from non-science is not that scientific theories can be proved, but that they can be refuted: a theory is scientific only if some conceivable observation would show it false.[^s1][^s2] For critical thinkers the book is the origin of a practical habit, asking of any confident claim "what would show this wrong?", and of a set of rules against rescuing a theory by explaining away every awkward result.[^s3]

## Origin

Popper wrote the book in Vienna in dialogue with, and largely against, the logical positivists of the Vienna Circle.[^s1][^s2] He was never invited to join the Circle; Otto Neurath called him its "official opposition".[^s2] The book appeared in German as *Logik der Forschung*, dated 1934 in Popper's own account and carrying a 1935 imprint from Julius Springer in Vienna.[^s2][^s1] Popper later translated it himself, and the English version, *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, was published in London in 1959.[^s1][^s2] He later claimed, with some self-advertisement, that the book had sounded the death knell of positivism.[^s2]

## How it works

The book's argument has four linked parts.

- **No induction.** Popper accepts Hume's point that no number of confirming instances can prove a universal law, and concludes that science does not rest on induction at all. Theories are bold conjectures, tested by deducing consequences from them.[^s2][^s1]
- **Asymmetry and falsifiability.** Although no observation can verify "all copper conducts electricity", a single well-established non-conducting sample can refute it. A theory is falsifiable if the class of observation statements it forbids is not empty; tautologies and purely existential claims forbid nothing and so are not empirical.[^s2][^s3]
- **Corroboration, not confirmation.** A theory that survives a severe test, especially one predicting something improbable on background knowledge, is corroborated. Corroboration is a report on past testing, not evidence that the theory is true.[^s1][^s2]
- **Basic statements and decisions.** Tests end in accepted observation reports ("basic statements"), and accepting them is in the end a decision of the scientific community, which is how the book stops an infinite regress of testing.[^s1][^s2]

The part most used in everyday reasoning is chapter 4. Popper lists the moves by which anyone can save a theory from any refutation: add an *ad hoc* hypothesis, quietly change a definition, doubt the reliability of the experimenter, or question the competence of the theorist. He calls these conventionalist stratagems.[^s3][^s4] Against them he proposes a methodological rule: an auxiliary hypothesis is acceptable only if it does not lower the theory's falsifiability, and ideally raises it, by making new testable predictions of its own. His examples contrast the Fitzgerald–Lorentz contraction hypothesis, which in his view only restored agreement with one result, with Pauli's exclusion principle, which added new testable content.[^s3] A footnote adds that the degree to which a hypothesis is ad hoc can be judged by how testable it is.[^s3]

A workplace example shows the rule at work. A product team predicts that a redesigned checkout page will raise conversions. The A/B test shows no lift. One response is "the test ran in a slow week, the wrong users saw it, and the analyst set it up badly", with no way to check any of these. Popper's rule asks whether the excuse makes a new, checkable prediction: "returning mobile users were misrouted, so their logs will show the old page" can be tested; a vague appeal to a bad week cannot, and should be treated as an ad hoc rescue.[^s3][^s1]

## Influence and limits

The book shaped twentieth-century philosophy of science and is still the usual starting point for the demarcation problem.[^s1][^s2] It also drew lasting criticism. Thomas Kuhn argued that observation is theory-laden and that real science works within paradigms that are not given up after a single failed prediction.[^s1] Imre Lakatos argued that there are no crucial experiments in Popper's sense: scientists rightly keep a theory through anomalies, and research programmes are abandoned when they stop making progress, not after isolated refutations.[^s2] Hilary Putnam and others noted that theories are always tested together with auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction does not by itself say which part is at fault.[^s2][^s1] Philosophers of physics still use the book's rules as a diagnostic; in 2017 David Merritt applied its account of conventionalist stratagems to the role of dark matter and dark energy in standard cosmology.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Authored by** Karl Popper, who wrote it in Vienna and later translated it into English.[^s2]
- **Introduces** the modern methodological treatment of the ad hoc hypothesis: earlier scientists already called some hypotheses ad hoc, but the book's sections 19–20 turned the label into a test, accept an auxiliary only if it keeps or raises testability.[^s3][^s4]
- It is also where the falsifiability criterion and the rule against conventionalist stratagems first appeared, and it argues from Hume's problem of induction; its refutation logic is the valid form modus tollens.[^s2][^s3]
- **Challenged by** Imre Lakatos (no crucial experiments; research programmes)[^s2] and Thomas Kuhn (paradigms, theory-laden observation).[^s1] The Duhem–Quine thesis, that hypotheses are never tested in isolation, is the standard objection to its picture of decisive refutation.[^s1]
- **Studied in** the philosophy of science, where it remains a core text on demarcation and theory testing.[^s1]
