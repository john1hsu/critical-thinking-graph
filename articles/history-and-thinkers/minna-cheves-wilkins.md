# Minna Cheves Wilkins

Minna Cheves Wilkins was an experimental psychologist who published *The Effect of Changed Material on Ability to Do Formal Syllogistic Reasoning* as number 102 of the *Archives of Psychology* in 1928.[^s1][^s2] The monograph asked a question that still shapes the study of critical thinking: does a person's skill at deduction depend on what the argument is about, or only on its form? Her answer, that content matters a great deal, makes her study the usual starting point in histories of **belief bias** and of content effects in reasoning.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Wilkins's study belongs to the early period of laboratory work on reasoning in psychology, when syllogisms borrowed from traditional logic were used as test items.[^s2] Later reviews list her monograph first among a series of studies, running through the 1940s to the 1960s, that claimed people judge syllogisms by whether the conclusions are believable.[^s3][^s4] Very little else about her career is recorded in the reasoning literature; she is known almost entirely through this one work.

## How it works

The design held logical form constant and changed only the material. A cognitive neuroscientist's review summarises three kinds of content: familiar sentences (for example, that all apples are red), content-free letters (all A are B), and sentences that clash with belief (all apples are poisonous).[^s1] Participants did better with the familiar versions than with the letter versions, and worse still when the content contradicted what they believed.[^s1] An encyclopedia survey of reasoning research makes the same first point, citing Wilkins for the finding that syllogisms in familiar terms are easier than the same forms in letters or unfamiliar terms.[^s2]

The two results pull in opposite directions, which is why the study still matters. Familiar content can help by making the relations concrete, but when the world and the logic disagree, knowledge gets in the way of judging validity.[^s1] Her data also recorded errors on simple one-premise inferences. A 2012 meta-analysis cites her, with Sells (1936), as early evidence that people often convert premises they should not, such as reading "some A are not B" as implying "some B are not A".[^s5]

*Workplace example.* An analyst checks two arguments with the same invalid form. One concludes that the team's favourite supplier is reliable; the other concludes that a disliked supplier is reliable. Wilkins's pattern predicts the first will slip through and the second will be scrutinised. A practical check is to restate the argument with letters, as in her symbolic condition, and judge the form before the topic.[^s1][^s2]

## Reception and limitations

Later researchers treated Wilkins's work as an important precursor rather than a settled finding. In 1983 Evans, Barston and Pollard cited her among authors who had claimed a belief effect, but noted that much of that early literature reported weak effects without significance tests, used poorly worded problems or failed to control other influences on reasoning.[^s3] Their own controlled experiments, crossing validity with believability, are what established belief bias as a robust effect.[^s3] Revlin and colleagues (1980) also placed her in the tradition that sees belief as distorting logic, and argued that some apparent belief effects may arise from how people encode the premises rather than from faulty inference.[^s4] Neither critique disputes her core observation that content changes performance; they question how cleanly early studies isolated its cause.

## Connections

- **Introduced belief bias** in the sense of being the earliest demonstration usually cited: belief-conflicting content lowered syllogistic performance in her study.[^s1] The term itself and the controlled paradigm came later, with Evans, Barston and Pollard.[^s3]
- **Authored** the 1928 *Archives of Psychology* monograph on changed material in syllogistic reasoning.[^s1][^s2]
- **Studied in psychology,** as part of early experimental research on reasoning and logic.[^s2]
- **Related to the syllogism and deductive validity,** the forms her tasks used, and to the later work of Jonathan Evans, which built on and tightened the claims of her generation.[^s3][^s4]
