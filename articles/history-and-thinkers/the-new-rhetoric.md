# The New Rhetoric

*The New Rhetoric: A Treatise on Argumentation* (*La nouvelle rhétorique: Traité de l'argumentation*, 1958) is a book by the Belgian philosopher Chaïm Perelman and Lucie Olbrechts-Tyteca. It was published in French in 1958 and in English in 1969, and the standard handbook of the field calls it a seminal work in argumentation theory.[^s1] Its central claim is that argumentation does not aim to demonstrate self-evident truths but to gain or strengthen the adherence of an audience to a thesis, so every argument has to be understood relative to the people it addresses.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The project grew out of dissatisfaction with logical empiricism, which left choices, decisions and actions outside the reach of reason because they could not be settled by formal proof or empirical verification.[^s1] Perelman wanted a "logic of value judgments" for practical affairs, and legal reasoning, where judges must justify decisions without conclusive proof, served as a model.[^s1] Rather than inventing a new formalism, the authors went back to classical rhetoric and dialectic and collected real arguments from law, philosophy, politics and literature; according to the handbook, Perelman supplied most of the theoretical framework and Olbrechts-Tyteca most of the examples, and they wrote it together.[^s1] They also departed from Aristotle on one point: values, which Aristotle mostly confined to ceremonial praise-and-blame speech, are treated as running through all argument.[^s2]

## How it works

The book is organised around three ideas.[^s1]

- **Audience.** A *particular audience* is the actual person or group being addressed; a *universal audience* is an idealised gathering of reasonable people that the arguer imagines and appeals to. Arguing to the universal audience is associated with *convincing*, arguing to a particular audience with *persuading*, though the authors admit the line is often blurry.[^s1][^s2]
- **Starting points.** Arguments begin from premises the audience already accepts: premises about *the real* (facts, truths, presumptions) and about *the preferable* (values, hierarchies of values and *loci*, the authors' Latin term for the classical *topoi*).[^s1]
- **Techniques.** Argument schemes divide into *association* and *dissociation*. Associative schemes include quasi-logical arguments, which borrow the look of formal or mathematical proof; arguments based on the structure of reality, such as cause and effect; and arguments that establish the structure of reality, such as example and analogy. Dissociation splits a concept into two, typically appearance versus reality.[^s1][^s3]

A workplace example shows how the pieces fit. A manager proposing a four-day week tells the finance team that output per hour rose in a pilot (an argument from the structure of reality, aimed at a particular audience that values cost), then tells the whole staff that "fairness means rewarding results, not hours" (a dissociation of real productivity from apparent busyness, aimed at a broader, near-universal audience). The book's lens asks which premises each audience actually grants and whether the arguer would still stand by the case before any reasonable listener.[^s1][^s2]

## Influence and limitations

The universal audience became the book's most debated idea, interpreted and applied by rhetoric scholars and used as a critical yardstick for legal arguments, and its *loci* and schemes have been applied to definitions, the press, group decision making and political communication.[^s1] Later theorists, including pragma-dialectics, connected its rhetorical insights to models of critical discussion.[^s1] Crosswhite argued that Perelman's tie between rationality and universality offers a different way to think about fallacies than rule-breaking accounts, and he reread *ad baculum*, equivocation and composition/division in that light.[^s1][^s4]

Critics point to weaknesses. The list of schemes was gathered from incidental examples and cannot claim to be complete; categories are loosely defined and hard to apply consistently; and the theory makes few testable predictions about which scheme caused a persuasive effect.[^s1] A 2019 analysis found the category of quasi-logical arguments ambiguous, covering three different notions of "similarity" to formal proof, with two competing explanations of why such arguments work on audiences.[^s3] Readers have also noted a tension between the book's relativism about standards of reasonableness and the normative distinctions it still draws, for example between eristic debate and cooperative discussion.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Authored by Chaïm Perelman and Lucie Olbrechts-Tyteca.** The handbook describes the division of labour between the two co-authors.[^s1]
- **Studied in argumentation theory.** It is one of the field's founding texts and a standing subject of its handbooks.[^s1]
- **Influenced by Aristotle.** It revives classical rhetoric and dialectic, including *topoi* and audience-relative premises, while revising Aristotle's view of epideictic speech.[^s1][^s2]
- **Contrasts with logical empiricism.** It was written to restore a place for reasoning about values that logical empiricism treated as non-rational.[^s1]
- **Related to quasi-logical arguments, argumentation schemes and the informal fallacies.** Its typology of schemes and its universality-based view of reasonableness feed later work on both.[^s3][^s4]
