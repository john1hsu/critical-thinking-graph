# Monroe Beardsley

Monroe Curtis Beardsley (1915-1985) was an American philosopher best known for his aesthetics, but he also wrote some of the earliest textbooks of the modern critical thinking movement.[^s1] Born and raised in Bridgeport, Connecticut, he took his B.A. (1936) and Ph.D. (1939) at Yale, taught at Mount Holyoke and Yale, and spent most of his career at Swarthmore College (22 years) and Temple University (16 years).[^s1] Michael Wreen's Stanford Encyclopedia entry describes his *Practical Logic* (1950) as one of the first informal logic or critical thinking texts of the contemporary era, and notes that its companion *Thinking Straight* ran through four editions over 25 years.[^s1]

## Origin

Beardsley's reasoning books appeared a generation before informal logic organised itself as a field in the 1970s. Their aim was practical: to teach readers and writers to find the reasons in ordinary prose, lay out how those reasons bear on a conclusion, and judge whether they are good enough.[^s1][^s2] The same analytic habits run through his aesthetics. Wreen stresses his demand for clarity and precision and his eye for identifying, exposing and evaluating arguments, and his insistence that criticism should rest on publicly checkable reasons rather than on private impressions.[^s1] With W. K. Wimsatt he co-wrote "The Intentional Fallacy" (1946), which argued that a work's meaning is not settled by what its author intended, and "The Affective Fallacy" (1949), which argued that readers' emotional reactions do not fix a work's properties.[^s1]

## How it works

Beardsley's lasting contribution to reasoning pedagogy is the argument diagram. In *Practical Logic* he drew arrows from each reason to the claim it supports and introduced three names for argument structures: convergent, divergent and serial.[^s2] A convergent argument, in his definition, is one where several independent reasons support the same conclusion, and he drew each with its own arrow.[^s2] David Hitchcock's 2014 history of the linked-convergent distinction traces how Stephen Thomas took over Beardsley's term "convergent" in 1977, coined "linked" for premises that work only together, and added a diagram convention for them.[^s2] That pairing is still the core of how textbooks and software map arguments today.[^s4]

A workplace example shows what the scheme adds. Suppose a manager argues that the team should move its weekly meeting to Tuesday because more people are in the office then, and also because Tuesday avoids the Monday release crunch. Each reason supports the conclusion on its own, so in Beardsley's terms the argument is convergent: knocking out one reason weakens it but does not collapse it. If instead the case were "everyone must attend, and only Tuesday has a room large enough", the two premises would be linked, since neither supports the conclusion alone, a refinement that Thomas later made explicit.[^s2][^s4]

Beardsley's texts also named formal slips. Brian Kirby's 1986 paper in *Informal Logic* quotes *Thinking Straight* (4th edition, 1975) treating "the fallacy of affirming a disjunct" as a standard error, as an example of the textbook view that he set out to qualify.[^s3] Whether Beardsley coined the name is not established by the sources opened for this article; he is the earliest named textbook user the graph records.[^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

Hitchcock argues that Beardsley's definition of convergence was ambiguous: "independent reasons" could mean separate arguments for the same claim or separately relevant reasons inside one argument. Beardsley also never explained "divergent" or "serial" in detail, and the convergence idea had largely dropped out of *Thinking Straight* by its 1966 and 1975 editions.[^s2] Leo Groarke's Stanford entry notes that argument diagrams themselves go back at least to Richard Whately (1826) and John Henry Wigmore (1913), so Beardsley popularised and named structures rather than inventing diagramming outright.[^s4] Kirby, for his part, argued that the textbook verdict on affirming a disjunct ignores exclusive "or", under which the form is valid.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced convergent argument.** Hitchcock credits *Practical Logic* (1950) with the terms convergent, divergent and serial argument, later adopted by Thomas.[^s2]
- **Introduced the intentional fallacy.** Wreen records the 1946 paper, written with Wimsatt, that named it.[^s1]
- **Named affirming a disjunct (low confidence).** Kirby shows Beardsley's textbook using the name, though not that he coined it.[^s3]
- **Studied in informal logic.** Wreen places *Practical Logic* among the first informal logic and critical thinking texts of the contemporary era.[^s1]
- **Related to argument mapping.** His arrow diagrams are an ancestor of today's linked/convergent maps.[^s2][^s4]
