# Organon

The *Organon* is the traditional name for Aristotle's six treatises on logic: the *Categories*, *On Interpretation*, *Prior Analytics*, *Posterior Analytics*, *Topics* and *On Sophistical Refutations*.[^s1][^s3] It is the earliest formal study of logic that survives, and it was taught with almost no rival from late antiquity until the nineteenth century.[^s1][^s2] For a critical thinker it is the source of much of the basic vocabulary still in use: premise and conclusion, deduction, contradiction, definition, and the first catalogue of fallacies.[^s3]

## Origin

Aristotle never used the word *Organon*, and he gives little sign that he thought of these treatises as one group, although the *Topics* and the *Analytics* often refer to each other.[^s1] The works are largely his lecture notes from different stages of his career in the fourth century BCE. Later followers edited them and gathered them into a single collection.[^s3] Later Peripatetics, the school that continued Aristotle's teaching, arranged the works in a systematic rather than chronological order and gave the set its name, Greek for "tool".[^s2]

The title took a side in an ancient debate. The Stoics held that logic is one of the parts of philosophy. The later Peripatetics held that it is only an instrument that philosophy uses, and calling the collection the "tool" expressed their view.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

The six books move, roughly, from words to statements to arguments to the misuse of arguments.[^s3]

- **Categories.** A classification of the kinds of things that can be said about something: substances and their properties, in ten basic categories.[^s3]
- **On Interpretation.** The structure of simple assertions and denials, the relations between opposed statements later drawn as the square of opposition, and the "sea battle" puzzle about whether statements about the future are already true or false.[^s3]
- **Prior Analytics.** The theory of the syllogism: what it is for a conclusion to follow of necessity, organised into figures and moods.[^s3][^s1]
- **Posterior Analytics.** When a deduction gives real knowledge: demonstration from true, prior and explanatory premises, the difference between knowing a fact and knowing its cause, and how first principles are grasped.[^s3]
- **Topics.** A handbook for dialectical debate, with argument strategies (*topoi*) for attacking or defending a thesis.[^s3]
- **On Sophistical Refutations.** The first theory of fallacies, split into errors that depend on language and errors that do not.[^s3]

A workplace example shows how the collection's tools fit together. A team lead argues: "Every vendor that failed our security review was later breached; this vendor failed the review; so it will be breached." The *Categories* and *On Interpretation* ask what kind of claim each premise is and what it would take to contradict it: one vendor that failed review but was never breached refutes the universal. The *Prior Analytics* checks whether the conclusion follows from the form alone. The *Posterior Analytics* asks whether the premises are true and explain the conclusion. The *Topics* suggests how a colleague might attack the thesis, and *On Sophistical Refutations* checks whether a slide in wording, such as two meanings of "failed", produced only an apparent proof.[^s3][^s1]

## Influence and limits

The ancient Greek commentators, including Alexander of Aphrodisias, Porphyry and Simplicius, wrote extensive commentaries on these works. Through them Aristotle's logic came to dominate late antiquity, while Stoic logic faded from view.[^s2][^s1] Boethius later translated the *Organon* into Latin in a literal, systematic style and wrote commentaries on part of it. His versions and commentaries formed the core of the medieval "old logic" taught in the Latin West.[^s5][^s2] Medieval logicians added mnemonic names for the valid syllogisms, such as Barbara, and Latin labels for the fallacies.[^s3] Kant could still claim that Aristotle had found everything there was to know about logic.[^s1]

The collection's authority also drew pushback. In 1620 Francis Bacon titled his *Novum Organum*, or "new instrument", after Aristotle's collection and meant it to replace it. Bacon complained that the syllogism leaps from a few particulars to the most general axioms. He proposed a gradual, eliminative induction based on tables of presence, absence and degrees.[^s4] Modern mathematical logic, from Frege and Russell onward, exposed serious limitations in the syllogistic, and from the nineteenth century mathematical logic largely replaced it, although scholars also note real affinities between Aristotle's approach and modern logic.[^s1][^s3] Its standing today is historical. Even so, its distinctions between form and content, demonstration and debate, and real and apparent proof remain at the centre of how critical thinking is taught.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

- **Authored by Aristotle.** All six treatises are Aristotle's, though the collection and its name came from later commentators.[^s1]
- **Has parts including the Posterior Analytics, Prior Analytics and Sophistical Refutations.** These are among the six books, covering knowledge, deduction and fallacies.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with the Novum Organum.** Bacon named his inductive method after the *Organon* explicitly to supplant it.[^s4]
- **Contrasts with Stoic logic.** The rival Hellenistic logic of Chrysippus treated logic as part of philosophy rather than its tool, and it lost out to the Aristotelian tradition in late antiquity.[^s1][^s2]
- **Studied in logic.** Historians of logic treat it as the founding text of the discipline and of the syllogism.[^s2][^s1]
