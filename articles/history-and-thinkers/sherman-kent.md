# Sherman Kent

Sherman Kent (1903-1986) was a Yale historian who became the leading architect of analytic practice in American intelligence. Jack Davis, writing for the CIA centre that bears Kent's name, says that if intelligence analysis as a profession has a founder, the honour belongs to him.[^s1] His lasting contributions for critical thinkers are two: the idea that analysis of foreign affairs should follow the disciplined methods of scholarship, and the demand that analysts say how likely their judgments are in words that readers will understand the same way.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Kent was born in California, took his BA (1926) and PhD in history (1933) at Yale, and taught there from 1935.[^s1] In 1941 he joined the Research and Analysis branch of the Office of Strategic Services, where his rapid 1942 studies of North African ports and railways supported planning for the Allied invasion; by 1943 he ran the branch's Europe-Africa division.[^s1] Back at Yale after the war, he wrote *Strategic Intelligence for American World Policy* (Princeton University Press, 1949), which argued that intelligence needed independent, university-trained analysts and mattered as much to keeping the peace as to winning wars.[^s1][^s3] Princeton's current edition presents it as the classic statement of the principles and methods of modern intelligence analysis.[^s3]

In 1950 the Director of Central Intelligence, Walter Bedell Smith, recruited Kent to the new Office of National Estimates; he led that office and chaired its Board of National Estimates from 1952 until he retired at the end of 1967.[^s1] He was the first chairman of the editorial board of the CIA's journal *Studies in Intelligence* (1955), and in 2000 the agency named its analyst training school after him.[^s1]

## How it works

Kent never wrote a single checklist, but Davis, following Frans Bax, distils nine working principles from his memos and essays: focus on what the decision maker needs; keep no personal policy agenda; apply intellectual rigour; make a conscious effort to avoid bias; be willing to consider other judgments; use outside experts systematically; take collective responsibility for the judgment; communicate effectively; and admit mistakes candidly.[^s1] Kent also wanted analysts to state their working assumptions openly and test them, and he distrusted hedges such as "may" or "could", because no outcome can ever prove them wrong.[^s1]

His best-known idea came from a communication failure. A 1951 national estimate called a Soviet move against Yugoslavia a "serious possibility". Kent himself meant odds of roughly 65 to 35 in favour, yet when he later polled the board members who had agreed the wording, their private readings ran from about 20-80 to 80-20, and policy staff took the phrase to mean much lower odds.[^s2] In his essay "Words of Estimative Probability" Kent proposed a short chart that ties each phrase to a band of odds: "almost certain" around 93 percent, "probable" around 75, "chances about even" at 50, "probably not" around 30, and "almost certainly not" near the bottom, with "possible" kept unmodified for cases where no odds can be assigned.[^s2] He cast the debate as "mathematicians", who want precise odds, against "poets", who prefer flexible prose, and sided with the mathematicians.[^s2]

The same habit works outside government. Suppose a product team's risk memo says a supplier failure is "a real possibility". One reader plans for a near-certain outage, another ignores it. Following Kent, the author would write "unlikely, about one chance in four" and name the assumption behind the number, so readers can act on the same estimate and later check whether it was well calibrated.[^s2][^s1]

## Legacy and limitations

Kent's own office failed to anticipate the Soviet deployment of offensive missiles to Cuba in 1962, an episode Davis says chastened him even though the analysts had followed the standards he promoted.[^s1] He also left tensions unresolved, such as brevity against credibility, speed against thoroughness, and close support for policymakers against objectivity.[^s1] Much of what is credited to him is reconstructed from unpublished memos and colleagues' recollections, and Davis acknowledges his own sympathy for the wartime research tradition.[^s1]

His proposal on probability words was not adopted by the CIA in his time.[^s4] Later research supports his diagnosis. In a 2018 study in *Intelligence and National Security*, M. K. Dhami cites work in which 119 CIA analysts used 170 different phrases for uncertainty, and reports her own spinner task with 26 UK analysts, in which "possible" was mapped to values anywhere from 10 to 60 percent.[^s4] Dhami credits Kent as the first to advocate a standard lexicon tied to numbers, while noting that the official lexicons later adopted do not always match how analysts actually read the words.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced intelligence analysis as a profession.** Davis names Kent as the profession's founder, and Kent's 1949 book set out its doctrine and methods.[^s1][^s3]
- **Introduced words of estimative probability.** Kent's essay proposed tying verbal likelihood terms to numerical odds, and later research credits him as the first to argue for such a lexicon.[^s2][^s4]
- **Authored *Strategic Intelligence for American World Policy*.** His 1949 Princeton book is the founding text of his approach.[^s3]
- **Related to Richards Heuer, the Tradecraft Primer and structured analytic techniques.** These later CIA contributions built on the professional ideal Kent set, adding attention to the cognitive limits that he wrote less about.[^s1]
- **Related to calibration and risk communication.** Stating judgments as odds makes them checkable against outcomes and clearer to the people who must act on them.[^s2][^s4]
