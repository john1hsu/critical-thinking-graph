# Philip Tetlock

Philip E. Tetlock is a Canadian-born psychologist and political scientist who turned the vague question "how good is expert judgment?" into something that can be measured. He studied at the University of British Columbia, took his PhD at Yale in 1979, held endowed chairs at Ohio State and at Berkeley's Haas School of Business, and is now Annenberg University Professor at the University of Pennsylvania, with appointments in the Wharton School, psychology and political science.[^s1] His university biography describes his work as assessing good judgment in laboratory and real-world settings, and also examining the standards social scientists use when they declare a judgment biased or mistaken.[^s1]

## Origin

Tetlock's best-known project began in the 1980s. Over roughly two decades he asked 284 people who made a living commenting on or advising about political and economic trends to put probabilities on future events, both inside and outside their specialties, and recorded how they reasoned and how they handled being wrong; by 2003 he had tens of thousands of forecasts to score against what actually happened.[^s3] The results, published by Princeton University Press in 2005 as *Expert Political Judgment*, were sobering: specialists barely beat well-informed non-specialists, and neither group did well against simple statistical rules.[^s3] Borrowing Isaiah Berlin's image, Tetlock found that "foxes", who drew on many ideas and tolerated uncertainty, forecast better than "hedgehogs" who applied one big theory, though foxes too fell short of the simple models.[^s3] Tetlock and colleagues later described the book as the first large-scale study of expert forecasting in world politics, one that documented overconfidence and hindsight bias but was not run as a competition and promised its participants anonymity.[^s2]

## How it works

The second phase removed those limits. In 2011 Tetlock and Barbara Mellers launched the Good Judgment Project as one of five research teams in a forecasting tournament sponsored by the US intelligence community, with thousands of volunteers answering more than 200 geopolitical questions scored by Brier score.[^s2][^s7] The project won; its best aggregated forecasts beat the control group's simple average by about 60 percent and the other teams by about 40 percent.[^s2] The authors attributed the edge to four levers: selecting and retaining good forecasters, training in probabilistic reasoning and cognitive debiasing, teamwork, and statistical aggregation that "extremized" the pooled forecast, the last contributing the largest share.[^s2] The top 2 percent of each year's forecasters were placed in elite teams of "superforecasters", who kept outperforming rather than regressing to the mean.[^s2][^s7] Superforecasters scored higher on intelligence measures and, tellingly for critical thinking, were more willing to revise their views in light of new evidence.[^s7]

A workplace version of the method is easy to picture. A strategy team that habitually says a competitor "may" enter its market can instead write down a number, such as 30 percent within a year, revisit it as news arrives, and score the forecast when the year ends. Over many such questions the team learns whether its 30 percent claims come true about 30 percent of the time, which is exactly the feedback Tetlock argues vague verbal forecasts let people dodge.[^s2]

Tetlock's earlier work was on accountability: the expectation that one must justify one's views to others. His social contingency model predicted that accountable people take the path of least resistance and shift toward a known audience's views; engage in pre-emptive self-criticism, thinking in more complex and balanced ways, when the audience's views are unknown; and bolster positions they have already committed to.[^s4] An experiment with 325 participants supported all three predictions.[^s4] With Mellers he later applied this lens to intelligence agencies, weighing accountability for following sound analytic process against accountability for accurate outcomes and concluding that each design fails in different ways.[^s5] A third strand, the sacred-value-protection model, studies how people respond with moral outrage and symbolic "cleansing" to taboo trade-offs, forbidden base rates and heretical counterfactuals.[^s6]

## Evidence and limitations

Tetlock's findings are about aggregate accuracy, not a verdict that expertise is worthless: in the forecasting studies, the gap between forecasters with different cognitive styles mattered more than credentials.[^s3] Reviewers have stressed that simple, structured combinations of forecasts are the practical lesson, and that unstructured expert committees can raise confidence without raising accuracy.[^s3] Tetlock himself acknowledges serious objections to scoring forecasts, including self-fulfilling and self-negating prophecies, arbitrary time frames and conditional forecasts, and notes that both experts and models are poor at anticipating shifts between stable and unstable periods.[^s5] The Good Judgment volunteers were mostly well-educated American men, which limits how far the tournament samples generalize.[^s7]

## Connections

Tetlock is the originator of geopolitical forecasting as a research field: his collaborators describe his book as its first large-scale study, and the tournament programme grew directly from it.[^s2] He authored *Expert Political Judgment*, the landmark statement of the fox–hedgehog result.[^s3] He introduced the social contingency model of accountability[^s4] and, with colleagues, the sacred-value-protection model.[^s6] His work belongs to judgment and decision making, with appointments spanning psychology, management and political science.[^s1] It also bears directly on calibration, the overconfidence effect, hindsight bias, base-rate neglect and the wisdom of crowds, and it informed debates over standards in intelligence analysis.[^s2][^s5][^s7]
