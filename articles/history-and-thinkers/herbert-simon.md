# Herbert Simon

Herbert A. Simon (1916-2001) was an American social scientist whose single lifelong question, how people actually make decisions and solve problems, carried him through political science, management, economics, psychology and computer science.[^s1][^s5] For critical thinking he matters because he replaced the picture of an all-knowing optimiser with a mind that works under limits of time, knowledge and computing power, and because he showed that such a mind gets by with heuristics: rules that prune a search rather than exhaust it.[^s4][^s3]

## Origin

Simon was born in Milwaukee in 1916 and studied at the University of Chicago from 1933 to 1936, where his teachers included the philosopher Rudolf Carnap and the economist Henry Schultz.[^s1] An undergraduate field study of the Milwaukee recreation department started his interest in organisational choice; he later recalled that its managers could not apply the marginal calculus that economic theory assumed.[^s2][^s1] After posts at Berkeley and the Illinois Institute of Technology he moved in 1949 to the new Graduate School of Industrial Administration at the Carnegie Institute of Technology (later Carnegie Mellon), and from 1965 held a chair in computer science and psychology there.[^s1][^s5] He met Allen Newell at the RAND Corporation in 1952, and around 1954 they decided to study problem solving by simulating it in computer programs, which became the centre of his research.[^s1] He received the 1975 Turing Award with Newell and the 1978 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, and died in Pittsburgh in 2001.[^s5][^s1]

## Main ideas

**Bounded rationality and satisficing.** In his 1978 Nobel lecture Simon explained that bounded rationality began, in *Administrative Behavior* (1947), as a residual notion: rationality that falls short of omniscience.[^s2] By the mid-1950s he had turned it into a positive account of how choices get made.[^s4] Its best-known piece is satisficing: instead of comparing every option, a decision maker takes the first one that meets an aspiration level, and that level drifts up when good options are easy to find and down when they are scarce. Simon credited the aspiration-level idea to Kurt Lewin's psychology.[^s2] He described behaviour as cut by a pair of scissors, one blade the structure of the task environment and the other the computational abilities of the actor; neither blade alone explains the cut.[^s4]

**Heuristic search.** With Newell and J. C. Shaw, Simon argued in 1958 that their Logic Theorist program, which found proofs for 38 of the first 52 theorems of chapter 2 of *Principia Mathematica*, succeeded not by brute force but by heuristic processes of the kind people use, such as working backward from the goal and trying only theorems that resemble the problem.[^s3] The paper set out what a theory of human problem solving should do, predicting performance and explaining the processes behind it, and offered heuristic search as that theory's core.[^s3] This is the route by which "heuristic", earlier a word for methods of discovery, entered psychology as a name for mental shortcuts.[^s3][^s5]

**Procedural rationality.** Simon held that subjective expected utility theory fails as a description of human choice, even as an approximation, and urged economists to study procedural rationality, the quality of the process used to decide, rather than only the substantive optimum.[^s2]

## Applying his ideas: a workplace example

A hiring manager facing 300 applications cannot rank them all. A Simon-style approach states an aspiration in advance (must hold the certification, at least three years in a similar role), screens in arrival order, interviews the first five who pass, and lowers or raises the bar if the pool proves thinner or richer than expected.[^s2] The critical-thinking lesson is to judge such a procedure by how well it fits the environment and the cost of search, not by whether it would have found the single best candidate under unlimited time.[^s4]

## Legacy and limitations

Hertwig and Kozyreva (2021) trace three later readings of his work. Economists often recast it as optimisation under constraints, a move Simon criticised because it brings back heavy demands for information and computation. The heuristics-and-biases program of Tversky and Kahneman catalogued departures from normative standards. Gigerenzer's ecological-rationality program took up the scissors image and studies when simple heuristics fit their environment well.[^s4] That the same writings support both an error-focused and an adaptive reading shows that "bounded rationality" names a research question more than a settled theory.[^s4] His computer models also carry a built-in caveat: the 1958 paper itself warned that Logic Theorist was not fitted to human data, so not every detail of it should be expected to match how people think.[^s3]

## Connections

Simon **introduced** the heuristic into the psychology of problem solving through the 1958 theory he wrote with Newell and Shaw, which places him in **cognitive psychology** as well as economics.[^s3] He was **influenced by** Kurt Lewin's work on aspiration levels and set his account in **contrast with** subjective expected utility theory.[^s2] He **influenced** both the **ecological rationality** program and, more loosely, the **heuristics-and-biases program**.[^s4] He is the originator of **bounded rationality** and **satisficing**, and a forerunner of **fast-and-frugal heuristics** and **behavioral economics**.[^s2][^s4]
