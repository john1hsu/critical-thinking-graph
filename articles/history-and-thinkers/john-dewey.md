# John Dewey

John Dewey (1859-1952) was an American philosopher and educational reformer, and a leading figure of pragmatism, the school that treats knowledge as arising from an organism's active adjustment to its surroundings rather than from passive contemplation.[^s3] For critical thinking his importance is specific: his 1910 book *How We Think* gave the first widely used account of good everyday reasoning as a teachable habit, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy credits him with introducing critical thinking as an educational aim.[^s2] He usually called it "reflective thinking" rather than critical thinking.[^s2]

## Origin

Dewey was born in Burlington, Vermont, on 20 October 1859.[^s4] He graduated from the University of Vermont in 1879 and took a doctorate at Johns Hopkins in 1884.[^s3] After about ten years at the University of Michigan he moved in 1894 to the University of Chicago, where in 1896 he founded a laboratory school to test his ideas about learning; in 1904 he went to Columbia University, where he taught until retiring in 1930.[^s3][^s4] He died in New York in 1952.[^s4]

*How We Think*, published by D. C. Heath with a preface dated December 1909, set out to connect children's natural curiosity with the scientific attitude of mind.[^s1][^s2] A heavily revised edition followed in 1933, subtitled as a restatement of how reflective thinking relates to education.[^s2] His other main works on the theme are *Democracy and Education* (1916), his central book on schooling, and the logical works *Studies in Logical Theory* (1903) and *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938), which recast logic as the theory of how inquiry resolves problems.[^s3]

## How it works

Dewey defined reflective thought as active, persistent and careful consideration of a belief in the light of the grounds that support it and the further conclusions it leads to.[^s1] It begins in a state of doubt or perplexity and proceeds through inquiry aimed at finding facts that confirm or undermine a suggested belief.[^s1] The opposite, "uncritical" thinking, accepts the first suggestion that comes to mind.[^s1] Its core discipline is restraint: reflection means "judgment suspended during further inquiry", which Dewey admitted is often uncomfortable, and he counted the habit of suspended conclusion, together with methods for searching out evidence, as the heart of training the mind.[^s1]

He then analysed a complete act of thought into five phases: a felt difficulty; locating and defining it; suggesting a possible solution; reasoning out what the suggestion implies; and further observation or experiment that leads to accepting or rejecting it.[^s1] His examples are deliberately ordinary. In one, he notices the time, realises a surface tram will make him late for an appointment, compares the subway with the elevated railway and picks the faster route. In another, he forms a hypothesis about why soap bubbles move in and out of upturned tumblers and tests it by cooling and warming the air inside them.[^s1]

A workplace version: a support team sees refund requests jump (felt difficulty). They pin the rise to one product line shipped since a certain date (definition). Someone suggests a packaging change (suggestion). If that is the cause, damage reports should cluster in parcels from the new supplier (reasoning). They check the reports, and keep or drop the idea according to what they find (test). Dewey's point is that jumping from the first suggestion straight to action skips the middle steps that make the conclusion trustworthy.[^s1]

## Legacy and limitations

Dewey's account shaped how the field defined its subject. The Progressive Education Association's Eight-Year Study of the 1930s applied his view that people learn through meaningful activity and reflection, and its evaluators grouped many of their objectives under "clear thinking" or "critical thinking".[^s2] In 1941 Edward Glaser defined critical thinking in his dissertation the way Dewey had defined reflective thinking; his experimental classes improved more than controls and kept the gains for at least six months.[^s2] Bloom's 1956 taxonomy noted that its intellectual skills were what Dewey called reflective thinking.[^s2]

The influence has limits. Dewey himself dropped the words "critical" and "uncritical" from the 1933 edition without explanation and treated being critical as only one part of reflection.[^s2] Later theorists such as Robert Ennis started from other conceptions, defining critical thinking as the correct assessment of statements rather than as a problem-solving cycle.[^s2] His five phases also describe how inquiry should go rather than reporting how people actually reason, and he grew critical of progressive schools that gave children freedom without guidance.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced critical thinking education.** The SEP history of critical thinking traces the educational aim to his 1910 book.[^s2]
- **Introduced reflective thinking.** *How We Think* defines the term and analyses its phases.[^s1]
- **Authored *How We Think* and *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry*.** The first is his book on reasoning and schooling; the second extends the same view of inquiry into a general logic.[^s1][^s3]
- **Influenced Edward Glaser,** whose 1941 definition of critical thinking followed Dewey's definition of reflective thinking.[^s2]
- **Related** to the scientific method and hypothesis testing, which his five-phase analysis generalises to everyday problems, and to Bloom's taxonomy, whose authors acknowledged that their intellectual skills matched what he called reflective thinking.[^s1][^s2]
