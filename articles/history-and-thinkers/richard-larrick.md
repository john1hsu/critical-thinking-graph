# Richard P. Larrick

Richard P. Larrick is an American psychologist who studies judgment and decision making and how to improve it. He holds the Hanes Corporation Foundation Professorship at Duke University's Fuqua School of Business, with appointments in management and organizations. He is a Fellow of the Association for Psychological Science and served as president of the Society for Judgment and Decision Making in 2017.[^s1] His work runs through one practical question: once a reasoning error is documented, what actually helps people avoid it? His answers range from teaching rules, through restructuring how decisions are framed, to changing the numbers people are shown.[^s2][^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Larrick took a BA in psychology and economics at the College of William and Mary and a PhD in social psychology at the University of Michigan in 1991.[^s1] At Michigan he worked in Richard Nisbett's research program, which held that people reason with abstract rules, such as statistical and causal rules, and that formal instruction can sharpen them.[^s2] In 1990, while still a graduate student, Larrick was first author, with the economist James Morgan and Nisbett, of "Teaching the use of cost-benefit reasoning in everyday life" in *Psychological Science*. The paper carried the rule-training program into economics. It asked whether people use microeconomic choice rules, chiefly ignoring sunk costs and counting forgone alternatives, and whether a short lesson could improve that use.[^s2] It also took sides in an old debate. Economists assume general choice rules, Thorndike-style accounts treat reasoning as domain-specific, and Piaget-influenced views hold such rules can only be self-discovered.[^s2]

## How it works

The 1990 study shows Larrick's method. A survey of 126 Michigan professors found that economists' reasoning and reported behavior followed cost-benefit rules more than that of biologists or humanists, scoring about .61 on the authors' index against roughly .45.[^s2] Undergraduates then received about half an hour of training on the sunk cost and extra cost principles. Trained students chose and reasoned more normatively than controls, and training with financial examples transferred to nonfinancial problems and back. In a phone follow-up four to six weeks later, trained students still answered policy questions more normatively, though their self-reported behavior differed only marginally (p = .10).[^s2] In workplace terms, a manager who has learned the sunk cost rule should ask of a stalled project only whether its remaining costs are worth its remaining benefits, not how much has already been spent.

His 2004 chapter "Debiasing", in the *Blackwell Handbook of Judgment and Decision Making*, became a standard map of the field.[^s3] Later researchers use its three families. Motivational strategies use incentives or accountability. Cognitive strategies change how a person frames or approaches a problem. Technological strategies use external aids such as quantitative models, checklists and decision architecture.[^s4] Aczel and colleagues note that the motivational route has mixed results. Accountability, for example, can push people toward justifying a choice rather than reasoning better.[^s4]

Two later findings with Jack Soll fit the technological route. In a 2006 *Management Science* paper, four experiments found that many people believe averaging two judges' estimates is no more accurate than the typical judge. In fact, when estimates fall on opposite sides of the truth, averaging must beat the average judge, and the misconception eased when that "bracketing" was made visible.[^s6] Their 2008 *Science* paper named the MPG illusion: people treat fuel saved as rising in a straight line with miles per gallon, so they undervalue replacing the least efficient vehicles. Going from 12 to 14 MPG saves more fuel than going from 28 to 40.[^s5] In a third study with 171 online participants, presenting the same choice as gallons per 100 miles raised selection of the more fuel-saving option from 25% to 64%.[^s5]

## Legacy and limitations

Larrick's work helped shift debiasing from the pessimistic view that biases resist correction to a toolkit of interventions matched to the error.[^s4] The limits are those of the field. Most training studies, including the 1990 one, measure effects soon after instruction and rely partly on self-report. Aczel and colleagues, testing an analogical training in 2015 with 154 students, found improvement four weeks later on statistical biases only.[^s2][^s4] Reframing remedies such as gallons per 100 miles change what people see but leave the faulty intuition in place.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced cost-benefit reasoning training.** The 1990 paper with Morgan and Nisbett designed and tested brief instruction in the sunk cost and extra cost rules, the origin of this debiasing technique.[^s2]
- **Introduced the MPG illusion.** Larrick and Soll named and demonstrated this framing error in 2008 and proposed a gallons-per-distance metric as its fix.[^s5]
- **Introduced the misappreciation of the averaging principle.** The 2006 *Management Science* experiments showed people undervalue averaging opinions, a finding later work on the wisdom of crowds builds on.[^s6]
- **Authored the "Debiasing" chapter (2004).** It set out the motivational, cognitive and technological families that later reviews use.[^s3][^s4]
- **Influenced by Richard Nisbett.** The 1990 study explicitly extended Nisbett's program on teachable inferential rules, related to statistical reasoning training.[^s2]
- **Studied in judgment and decision making.** His listed research areas are judgment and decision making, social psychology and organizational behavior.[^s1]
