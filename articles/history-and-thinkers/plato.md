# Plato

Plato (c. 428/427-348/347 BCE) was the Athenian philosopher who learned from Socrates, taught Aristotle and founded the Academy, the school Britannica calls the distant ancestor of the modern university.[^s3][^s2] For critical thinking his legacy is less a doctrine than a practice: his dialogues show claims being put to the test by questioning, and they asked, in a form philosophers still use, what separates knowing something from merely believing it truly.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Plato came from a high-status Athenian family; his parents were Ariston and Perictione, and his relatives included Critias and Charmides, members of the oligarchy of the Thirty in 404 BCE.[^s2] His given name seems to have been Aristocles, with "Plato" possibly a nickname.[^s2] He was about 28 when Socrates was executed in 399 BCE.[^s1] Some years later he founded the Academy in Athens, in the 380s BCE, and it remained his base for the rest of his life; Aristotle studied there for about twenty years.[^s3][^s2]

Socrates wrote nothing, so his method is known mostly through Plato.[^s3] Plato in turn almost never wrote treatises or spoke in his own voice: nearly all his works are dialogues, usually with Socrates as the main speaker.[^s1] Kraut reads the form as a deliberate device to draw readers into weighing the arguments themselves, and notes Socrates' remark in the *Phaedrus* that books serve best as reminders of live discussion.[^s1] Besides Socrates, he drew on earlier thinkers including Heraclitus, Parmenides and the Pythagoreans.[^s2]

## How it works

In the early dialogues Socrates asks people who claim expertise, about courage or piety for example, to say what the thing is; by question and answer he draws out their own commitments until they contradict one another, while professing no wisdom of his own.[^s2][^s3] This refutation by questioning, the elenchus, gave these works the name "elenctic dialogues".[^s5] In later works Plato favoured a different method, collection and division: gather the instances of a kind, then split it into sub-kinds.[^s2]

The *Theaetetus*, written around 369-367 BCE, is the clearest case for critical thinkers.[^s4] Socrates casts himself as a midwife who helps others deliver their ideas and then checks whether they are sound.[^s4] Three definitions of knowledge are tried and rejected. Knowledge as perception fails because the soul, not the senses, grasps common features such as sameness and number.[^s4] Knowledge as true judgment fails on a courtroom example: jurors persuaded by what they were told can reach the true verdict about events it did not witness without knowing what happened.[^s4] Knowledge as true judgment with an account (*logos*) fails because none of three readings of "account" does the work.[^s4] The dialogue ends unresolved, and Socrates counts clearing away false beliefs as its gain.[^s4]

A workplace version of the jury case: a project lead hears from a confident colleague that a vendor will miss its deadline, and the vendor does miss it. The lead's belief was true, but it rested on persuasion rather than evidence, so the lead did not know it. The lesson Plato draws is to ask what grounds a true belief, not only whether it turned out right.[^s4]

## Legacy and limitations

How far Plato's Socrates reports the historical Socrates is disputed: Gregory Vlastos's influential view took the early elenctic dialogues as Socratic, but later scholars rejected the developmental chronology it relied on.[^s5] The standard early/middle/late ordering of the dialogues is itself contested, partly resting on stylometry.[^s1] His best-known doctrine, the theory of Forms, which treats changeless paradigms as the true objects of knowledge, is criticised severely in his own *Parmenides*.[^s1] Aristotle often defined his views against Plato's, yet Britannica sees his programme as continuing his teacher's.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Influenced by Socrates**, his teacher and the chief speaker of his dialogues.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Parmenides**, among the earlier thinkers whose ideas he absorbed.[^s2]
- **Studied in epistemology**: the *Theaetetus* is an extended inquiry into what knowledge is.[^s4]
- **Related: justified true belief and the Gettier problem**, since the *Theaetetus* tests the proposal that knowledge is true belief with an account, the ancestor of the JTB analysis.[^s4]
- **Related: Aristotle**, his student at the Academy for two decades.[^s3]
- **Related: the Socratic method and the method of counterexample**: the elenchus refutes a claim by showing it clashes with the speaker's other commitments, and the *Theaetetus* rejects a definition with a single counterexample case.[^s2][^s4]
