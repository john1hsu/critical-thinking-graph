# Rhetoric (Aristotle)

The *Rhetoric* is Aristotle's treatise on persuasive speech. He defines rhetoric not by the speech it produces but as the ability to see what is possibly persuasive in a given case, much as a doctor's skill is judged by seeing every available remedy rather than by always curing.[^s1] The art is neutral between true and false or just and unjust causes, though Aristotle holds that it is easier to persuade people of what is true and good.[^s1] For critical thinkers the book matters because it is the first sustained attempt to say what makes everyday, non-expert argument good, and because it gives the earliest systematic account of the argument from example, the ancestor of the modern argument from analogy.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The surviving work has three books. Christof Rapp's Stanford Encyclopedia entry reports that the core of Books I and II is usually dated to Aristotle's first stay in Athens, and its close ties to the *Topics* suggest it predates his theory of the syllogism. A few historical references point later, which scholars explain as updates made for repeated lectures.[^s1] Book III, on style and arrangement, may have been added later; ancient catalogues list two books on rhetoric and two on style as separate works.[^s1] Book I covers the three kinds of speech (deliberative, judicial and epideictic), Book II covers the emotions, types of character and the general argument forms, and Book III covers style and the parts of a speech.[^s1]

## How it works

Aristotle names three proofs supplied by the speaker's art. *Ethos* is the character the speaker displays in the speech itself (practical wisdom, virtue and goodwill). *Pathos* is putting the audience in a frame of mind, since emotions change judgment. *Logos* is the argument, the only one of the three that proves anything.[^s1] Argument in turn has two forms that mirror dialectic. In the Roberts translation, Book I states that the example is a rhetorical induction and the enthymeme a rhetorical syllogism.[^s2] The enthymeme, which Aristotle calls the body of persuasion, argues from accepted opinions (*endoxa*), probabilities and signs. Its having fewer premises reflects choosing what a lay audience needs to hear, not a logical gap, though scholars still debate how strictly enthymemes count as deductions.[^s1] The general argument patterns (*topoi*) under which enthymemes fall make the book an early catalogue of argument schemes.[^s1]

The example (*paradeigma*) is the part that most shapes modern work on analogy. Book II, chapter 20, divides examples into real past events and invented ones, which are either illustrative comparisons or fables.[^s2] The historical case runs: earlier Persian kings seized Egypt before crossing to attack Greece, so if the present king takes Egypt he will cross too and must be stopped. The invented comparison is attributed to Socrates: choosing officials by lot is like choosing athletes or a ship's steersman by lot, when the job should go to the person with the skill.[^s2] Aristotle judges fables easier to invent but real precedents more useful in deliberation, because the future usually resembles the past. He advises using examples after an enthymeme, as supporting witnesses, where one good example suffices, and to use several if examples must carry the argument alone.[^s2]

A workplace version: a project lead argues that the last vendor who demanded full payment up front delivered late and then stopped answering, so a new vendor making the same demand is a risk. That is a paradeigma, moving from one known case to a similar one, and, as Kraus's reading implies, it is only as strong as the unstated general rule linking the two cases.[^s4]

## Influence and interpretation

Andrzej Stefańczyk (2021) argues that although the paradeigma is filed under induction, it does not generalize from many cases. It moves from one particular to another of the same kind, which he reads as analogy, so that Aristotle in effect recognizes three types of inference: deduction, induction and reasoning by example.[^s3] Manfred Kraus (2014) reads the example as a hidden chain, an inductive step to a general rule followed by a deductive step back to the new case, and argues that analogies are therefore always defeasible and come in degrees of strength. He adds that the closing deductive step can make a weak analogy look conclusive.[^s4]

Roman authors such as Cicero and Quintilian built on the *Rhetoric*, but for about two thousand years it was read mainly as rhetorical history. Philosophical interest revived in the late twentieth century, especially in ancient logic and argumentation theory, metaphor and the analysis of emotions.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Authored by Aristotle.** Rapp places the core of the work in his first Athenian period.[^s1]
- **Introduces the argument from analogy.** The paradeigma of Book II, chapter 20, is the first systematic treatment of arguing from one similar case to another, which Stefańczyk identifies as analogy rather than induction proper.[^s3][^s2]
- **Introduces the enthymeme.** The book's account of the rhetorical syllogism built from accepted opinions, probabilities and signs is the origin of the term's use in argument analysis.[^s1]
- **Influenced by the Topics.** Its dependence on the dialectical *Topics*, including the notion of a *topos*, is one reason scholars date its core early.[^s1]
- **Influenced argumentation theory.** Late twentieth-century argumentation theorists returned to it for its account of everyday persuasive argument.[^s1]
- **Related:** the later *New Rhetoric* of Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca, stasis theory, dialectic and inductive reasoning.[^s1][^s4]
