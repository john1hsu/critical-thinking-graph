# Intellectual humility

Intellectual humility is the disposition to notice and own the limits of what one knows: gaps in knowledge, mistaken beliefs, unreliable methods and skill deficits. Philosophers Dennis Whitcomb, Heather Battaly, Jason Baehr and Daniel Howard-Snyder define it as proper attentiveness to, and owning of, one's intellectual limitations.[^s1] Psychologists describe a similar metacognitive core: recognising that one's knowledge is bounded and that any particular belief might be wrong, which then shows up in how people treat evidence and other people's views.[^s2][^s3]

It is a virtue of the middle. Too little produces intellectual arrogance, which under-owns one's limitations; too much produces servility or diffidence, which over-owns them and defers to anyone.[^s1] Reviewers trace this mean-between-extremes framing back to Aristotle's Golden Mean.[^s2]

## Origin

Humility about knowledge is an old theme, but systematic study is recent. In 2015 Whitcomb and colleagues published the "limitations-owning" account in *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, arguing against rival views that equated the virtue with low concern for one's intellectual status (Roberts and Wood) or with simply assessing one's abilities accurately.[^s1] Their argument: someone can care little about status yet reflexively defend every mistake, and someone can rate their strengths accurately while staying blind to their weaknesses.[^s1] They add that the trait counts as a *virtue* only when it is driven by caring about truth and understanding, not by, say, a manager's wish to look approachable.[^s1]

Psychological measurement took off in the same decade. Mark Leary and colleagues at Duke published a six-item Intellectual Humility Scale in 2017, built around accepting that one's beliefs and attitudes may be wrong.[^s3] A 2022 review in *Nature Reviews Psychology* counted 14 competing definitions and measures, spanning trait, state and belief-specific questionnaires.[^s2]

## How it works

The limitations-owning account breaks the disposition into two parts. *Attentiveness* means the relevant limitation comes to mind when it matters. *Owning* means believing you have it, admitting it to others, caring about its consequences and responding with regret rather than defensiveness.[^s1]

Workplace example: a team lead presents a demand forecast built on last year's figures. A colleague points out that a large customer left in the spring. An intellectually arrogant reply blames "market noise" and keeps the number. A servile reply abandons the forecast and adopts whatever the loudest person suggests. The humble reply says, in effect, "I missed that, and my model has no way to account for customer churn; let's re-run it and flag the uncertainty." The lead keeps confidence in the parts of the analysis that were sound while owning the specific gap.[^s1][^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Name the limit concretely.** List "known unknowns" for a decision; brief exercises like this produced short-term gains in experiments.[^s2]
2. **Try explaining, not just asserting.** Writing a step-by-step explanation of how a policy or system works reduced people's overestimates of their own understanding.[^s2]
3. **Take a distanced view.** Reflecting on a disagreement from a third-person or outside perspective raised measured humility; this self-distancing effect is one of the few that has replicated.[^s2]
4. **Treat ability as improvable.** In one experiment, making a growth mindset of intelligence salient increased humility, which in turn increased openness to opposing views.[^s4]
5. **Credit good arguments from the other side.** Ask whether you would rate an argument as strong if it supported your own view.[^s3]

Pitfalls: humility is not indecision. The 2022 review notes it may help less where fast, decisive action is required, and the philosophical account warns that over-owning limits is a failure of its own.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

Leary and colleagues ran four online studies with roughly 1,190 participants. Higher scorers were more open and curious and less dogmatic, judged people with opposing religious views less harshly, and separated strong from weak arguments more sharply; the effects were often small, and the scale was unrelated to religiosity or party.[^s3] Porter and Schumann's four studies (2018) found higher humility predicted greater willingness to learn about, and seek out, the opposing side in political disagreements.[^s4]

The evidence base has clear limits. The 2022 review stresses that most benefits are correlational, that self-reports are vulnerable to social-desirability bias, that no behavioural task has yet been fully validated, that samples are mostly WEIRD, and that most experimental boosts come from small studies not yet replicated; only the self-distancing and illusion-of-understanding effects have replications, with mixed results.[^s2] Hence the status here is *mixed*: a real, measurable trait with promising but not yet causally established benefits.

## Connections

- **Broader:** it is one of the thinking dispositions and a central virtue in virtue epistemology, the approach that analyses knowledge in terms of the knower's character.[^s1]
- **Counteracts:** humbler people show less myside bias and less overestimation of their own knowledge, so the disposition works against the overconfidence effect; the review flags this evidence as largely cross-sectional.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with:** the IH scale correlates negatively with dogmatism and intolerance of ambiguity.[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by:** the Leary et al. (2017) scale studies and the Porter and Schumann (2018) openness experiments.[^s3][^s4]
- **Related:** it overlaps with actively open-minded thinking and correlates positively with need for cognition; reviewers distinguish it from general open-mindedness because its core is metacognitive, which is why metacognition is a prerequisite.[^s2] It is also listed among the intellectual traits of the Paul–Elder framework and discussed in virtue argumentation theory.
- **Studied in:** epistemology and social psychology, alongside cognitive, educational and organizational psychology.[^s1][^s2]
