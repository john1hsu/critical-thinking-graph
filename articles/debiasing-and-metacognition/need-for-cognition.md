# Need for cognition

Need for cognition (NFC) is a person's characteristic tendency to take on, and take pleasure in, effortful thinking.[^s1] People high in NFC seek out information and work through it to make sense of a question; people low in NFC lean more on shortcuts, such as going along with what an expert or a familiar source says.[^s4] It is a motivation, not an ability: it describes how much someone likes to think, not how well they think.[^s3]

## Origin

The social psychologists John Cacioppo and Richard Petty introduced the construct and its measuring scale in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* in 1982.[^s1][^s4] The original instrument had 34 statements rated from "extremely uncharacteristic" to "extremely characteristic" of oneself, including items such as preferring complex to simple problems and the reverse-scored claim that thinking is not one's idea of fun.[^s1] In 1984 Cacioppo, Petty and Kao published an 18-item short form with comparable measurement properties, which is now the usual choice in research.[^s1][^s2] A 1996 review by Cacioppo, Petty, Feinstein and Jarvis characterised NFC as a stable individual difference in cognitive motivation.[^s3]

## How it works

NFC matters most when people decide how much effort to spend on a message. In the persuasion research that grew out of Petty and Cacioppo's work, high scorers scrutinise arguments and are moved most by strong, well-supported ones, while low scorers are swayed more by cues such as the communicator's expertise.[^s4] High scorers also recall more of what they read and spontaneously generate explanations when something unexpected happens.[^s4]

A workplace example: two managers read the same vendor proposal. The high-NFC manager works through the cost model, notices that the savings depend on an optimistic adoption rate, and asks for the assumption to be tested. The low-NFC manager notes that a well-known consultancy endorsed the vendor and signs off. Neither reaction is fixed. When a situation clearly demands careful thought, such as a decision the low-NFC manager will personally be held to, low scorers can think as hard as high scorers; when little seems to be at stake, high scorers also coast.[^s4]

## How to apply it

1. **Treat it as a tendency, not a guarantee.** A thoughtful colleague can still be fooled by how a choice is framed (see Evidence), so build checks into the process rather than relying on who is in the room.[^s2]
2. **Raise the stakes for effort.** Asking people to explain or own a judgment can bring low scorers up to the level of careful processing that high scorers show by default.[^s4]
3. **Show both frames.** Thinking harder helps most when people can see that two descriptions refer to the same problem, so present gains and losses side by side.[^s2]
4. **Write for both audiences.** Put strong arguments in documents meant for scrutiny, and do not assume a credible source alone will carry readers who think hard.[^s4]

## Evidence

NFC is a well-established individual difference with modest correlates. Reviews cited by Grass and colleagues put its correlation with academic performance at roughly 0.17 to 0.22 and its links to intelligence at no more than a medium size; it overlaps strongly with the openness-to-ideas facet of personality.[^s3] In their 2017 survey of 396 German university students, NFC correlated 0.19 with university grades and 0.40 with satisfaction with one's studies, though the direct path to grades did not hold once other variables were modelled.[^s3]

Its protective value against bias is limited. LeBoeuf and Shafir (2003) gave seven framing problems to 365 Princeton students: framing effects appeared in every problem, and neither high NFC nor a request to justify choices reduced them.[^s2] In a second study with 292 students who later saw both frames, high scorers were more often consistent across frames (52% versus 33% on both problems), which the authors read as noticing the equivalence rather than resisting the initial frame.[^s2]

The best-known persuasion interaction has also weakened on replication. Many Labs 3 (2016, N = 2,365) confirmed that strong arguments persuade more than weak ones but found no NFC-by-argument-quality interaction.[^s5] A nine-site follow-up (N = 1,219) of Luttrell, Petty and Xu's revised protocol did recover the interaction, but at a much smaller size than earlier estimates.[^s6] Hence the status here is mixed.

## Connections

- **Introduced by** John Cacioppo and Richard Petty, whose 1982 scale defined the construct.[^s1]
- **Counteracts** the fundamental attribution error: high scorers attend more to situational causes of behaviour.[^s4]
- **Demonstrated by** LeBoeuf and Shafir's 2003 framing studies, which showed that NFC improves consistency across frames but not resistance to a single frame.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** the Many Labs 3 replication project, which failed to reproduce NFC's moderation of argument-quality effects.[^s5][^s6]
- **Studied in** social psychology, especially persuasion and attitude research.[^s4]
- **Related to** the elaboration likelihood model, which uses NFC as a marker of motivation to think, and to actively open-minded thinking, numeracy and the cognitive reflection test as other thinking-disposition measures.[^s4][^s3]
