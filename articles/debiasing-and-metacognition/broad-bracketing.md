# Broad bracketing

Broad bracketing means judging a choice as one of a series of similar choices and weighing their combined consequences, rather than deciding each one on its own.[^s1] Its opposite, narrow bracketing, treats every decision as a separate event. Because a single small bet can easily lose, narrow bracketing gives loss aversion its full force; seen as part of a bundle, the occasional loss is offset by the other outcomes and the bundle's overall value comes into view.[^s1][^s2] For a critical thinker it is a practical way to stop repeated, favourable risks from being refused one at a time.

## Origin

Daniel Kahneman and Dan Lovallo argued in a 1993 *Management Science* paper that decision makers tend to treat each problem as unique, isolating it from future opportunities, and that this produces overly timid choices because people miss how pooling many risks shrinks the relative impact of any one of them.[^s2] Daniel Read, George Loewenstein and Matthew Rabin named the general phenomenon in "Choice Bracketing" (1999), distinguishing broad from narrow bracketing, reviewing evidence from many decision contexts, and arguing that broad bracketing generally yields better choices because it takes all consequences into account, even though people often bracket narrowly when they could do otherwise.[^s1]

## How it works

Gneezy and Potters cite a classic example from Paul Samuelson: a colleague turned down a single coin flip that would win $200 or lose $100, yet said he would accept many such flips played together.[^s3] Taken alone, the possible $100 loss dominates; taken as a bundle, a net loss becomes unlikely.

A workplace version: a marketing team is asked to approve a series of small test campaigns, each with a modest chance of a sizeable return and a larger chance of losing its small budget. Reviewed one by one, each campaign looks like a likely loss and gets rejected. Reviewed as a quarterly programme, the expected value of the set is clearly positive and the worst case is tolerable, so the team approves the programme and sets a total budget cap. This is the pattern Kahneman and Lovallo describe in organisations, where risk aversion that is sensible for one isolated project becomes costly across many.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Spot the repetition.** Ask whether this decision is one of many similar ones you or your organisation will face, such as hiring, product bets, insurance or investments.[^s1][^s2]
2. **Evaluate the policy, not the instance.** Decide on a rule for the whole class of choices (accept every bet of this type up to a set size) and judge the rule by its total outcome.[^s1]
3. **Take the trader's perspective.** Remind yourself that this outcome is one draw in a portfolio; in a 2009 study, coaching people to reappraise gambles this way cut their measured loss aversion and their physiological arousal to losses.[^s4]
4. **Check results less often.** Where feedback can be pooled, review aggregate outcomes at longer intervals instead of after every trial.[^s3]

**When not to use it.** Broad bracketing assumes the choices are genuinely similar and independent and that you can survive the bad runs. Read, Loewenstein and Rabin note that narrower bracketing sometimes produces better decisions,[^s1] and a one-off, ruinous risk is not made safe by imagining a portfolio that does not exist.

## Evidence

In Gneezy and Potters's 1997 experiment with 83 students, people who chose one bet for a block of three rounds and saw only the combined result bet about 67% of their endowment, against about 50% for those who decided and saw feedback after every round, and they earned more overall.[^s3] A later high-powered *Management Science* stress test, which changed the design in several ways including smaller returns, compounding and longer horizons, found myopic loss aversion in every condition, answering doubts that the effect was confined to artificial lab settings.[^s6]

Sokol-Hessner and colleagues showed in *PNAS* (2009) that a perspective-taking strategy, framed as thinking like a trader, reduced behavioural loss aversion and the extra arousal people felt per dollar lost compared with per dollar gained; the study was a single laboratory sample, so the size of the effect outside such settings is less certain.[^s4] Hadar, Danziger and Morwitz (2021) found across four studies that prompting broad bracketing made people more likely to choose the option that was better on average in experience-based choices, but they attributed the gain to a focus on averages rather than to reduced loss aversion.[^s5] The evidence that broad framing changes choices is consistent; how much of that runs through loss aversion, and how well it lasts outside the lab, is still open, so the status here is mixed.[^s4][^s5]
