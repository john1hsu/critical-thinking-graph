# Metacognition

Metacognition is cognition about cognition: what a person knows about how thinking works, together with the moment-to-moment monitoring and steering of their own thinking while they read, remember, solve problems or communicate.[^s1] In critical thinking it is the layer that asks "do I actually understand this?", "how sure should I be?" and "is my approach working?", and then acts on the answers.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The developmental psychologist John Flavell introduced the term and framed it as a research field in a 1979 *American Psychologist* article.[^s1] His starting point was a series of studies with children: younger children who said they had memorized a list often could not recall it, and children asked to judge instructions frequently failed to notice obvious gaps and believed they could follow them.[^s1] Flavell argued that people generally monitor their own thinking too little, and he speculated that better monitoring might help people evaluate persuasive messages by appraising the source and the quality of the appeal, while noting that this was untested.[^s1]

## How it works

Flavell divided the field into four interacting parts.[^s1] *Metacognitive knowledge* is stored belief about cognition, about persons (my own strengths, how people in general reason), tasks (what makes a problem hard) and strategies (what works for which goal). *Metacognitive experiences* are conscious feelings during a task, such as puzzlement or a sense of being close to the answer. These interact with *goals* and with *actions*: cognitive strategies make progress, and metacognitive strategies check progress, as when you paraphrase what someone said to confirm you understood.[^s1]

Thomas Nelson and Louis Narens gave the idea a precise architecture in 1990.[^s2] An *object level* does the thinking, while a *meta level* holds a model of it. Information flows up as **monitoring** (for example, a judgment that an item has been learned, or a feeling of knowing an answer you cannot yet recall) and down as **control** (keep studying, stop searching, withhold an answer whose confidence is below a threshold).[^s2] Their framework treats introspective reports as imperfect data rather than a window on the mind, and notes that confidence after recall is typically too high.[^s2]

A workplace example: an operations analyst is asked how long a data migration will take. Her object-level work is estimating; her meta level notices that she has never migrated this kind of database (task knowledge), that her last two estimates ran long (person knowledge), and that her confidence feels higher than her track record supports (monitoring). The control response is to break the job into parts, ask a colleague who has done it, and quote a range instead of a single date.[^s1][^s2]

## How to apply it

- **Plan:** before starting, state the goal, what a good answer looks like and which strategy you will try.[^s4]
- **Monitor:** pause at checkpoints to test understanding, for instance by explaining the argument in your own words or predicting the next step.[^s1]
- **Evaluate:** afterwards, compare your confidence with how things turned out and note which strategies helped.[^s4][^s3]
- **Separate confidence from accuracy:** metacognitive ability is about whether confidence tracks being right, not about how confident you are overall.[^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

Measurement is subtle. Stephen Fleming and Hakwan Lau (2014) distinguish metacognitive *bias* (generally high or low confidence), *sensitivity* (how well confidence separates one's correct from incorrect judgments) and *efficiency* (sensitivity relative to how well one performs the task), and warn that simple confidence-accuracy correlations can mislead because they are affected by response bias.[^s3]

Links to critical thinking are suggestive rather than conclusive. In a 2010 think-aloud study, Kelly Ku and Irene Ho found that university students who performed well on critical thinking tasks showed more metacognitive activity, especially high-level planning and evaluation, than matched weaker performers; the sample was only ten students.[^s4] Teaching metacognitive and self-regulation strategies does improve school performance on average: a 2012 University of Groningen meta-analysis of 55 studies reported an overall effect of about g = 0.66.[^s5] Effects varied widely by subject, and were larger on researcher-made tests than on independent ones and when researchers rather than teachers delivered the training, so the classroom benefit is real but context-dependent.[^s5]

## Connections

Metacognition was introduced by John Flavell, who framed it as monitoring and regulation of one's own cognition.[^s1] It was formalized by the Nelson-Narens object-level/meta-level framework and is studied in cognitive psychology through memory judgments such as feelings of knowing.[^s2] The Ku and Ho think-aloud study supports a link between metacognitive strategy use and critical thinking performance.[^s4] It is applied in critical thinking and study-skills education, where strategy instruction shows average gains with large moderators.[^s5] Its emphasis on confidence tracking accuracy ties it to calibration and the overconfidence effect,[^s3][^s2] and debiasing techniques such as deliberate reflection and actively open-minded thinking can be read as structured ways of exercising metacognitive control.[^s1]
