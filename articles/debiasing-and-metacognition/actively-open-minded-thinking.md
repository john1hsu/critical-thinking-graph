# Actively open-minded thinking

Actively open-minded thinking (AOT) is a disposition to think well when a question is in doubt: to look hard enough for alternative possibilities, evidence and goals, to treat options you dislike as fairly as the one you favour, and to be only as confident as that search justifies.[^s1] The word "actively" matters. Being willing to hear another view is not enough; the thinker goes looking for reasons they might be wrong and resists wishful thinking about a preferred answer.[^s1]

## Origin

The psychologist Jonathan Baron at the University of Pennsylvania named and developed the idea in work published in 1985, 1988 and 1993, as part of a normative account of good thinking as search plus inference.[^s3] In a 1996 essay for Penn's *Almanac*, Baron summarised three standards: search should be thorough in proportion to the importance of the question, search and inference should be fair to every option on the table, and confidence should match how much searching and careful inference were done.[^s1] He illustrated the failure mode with Irving Janis's account of groupthink before the Bay of Pigs invasion.[^s1]

Keith Stanovich and Richard West turned the idea into a self-report scale in 1997, covering reflectiveness, willingness to weigh contrary evidence and other opinions, and tolerance of ambiguity; later versions grew to 41 items before being trimmed.[^s3] Baron and colleagues maintain a shorter scale that measures beliefs about how one ought to think, on the rationale that people's thinking tracks their beliefs about good thinking.[^s5]

## How it works

A product manager hears that a competitor's new feature is causing customers to leave. The closed-minded route is to accept the story because it fits what the team already suspected and to start copying the feature. The actively open-minded route has three moves. First, search for other possibilities: a price rise last quarter, a support backlog, or a seasonal dip. Second, search fairly: pull the cancellation reasons and look for evidence that would count against the competitor story as hard as for evidence that supports it. Third, calibrate: if only ten exit surveys mention the competitor, hold the conclusion loosely and say so in the briefing.[^s1][^s2]

Haran, Ritov and Mellers found that the first move drives much of the benefit. In their 2013 experiments, people who scored higher on AOT chose to gather more information before estimating, and that extra information accounted for their better accuracy and less overconfident intervals; when the amount of information was fixed by the experimenter, AOT no longer predicted performance.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Name the doubt.** State the question and your current favourite answer before searching.[^s1]
2. **Search for alternatives on purpose.** List the less obvious answers and objections, not only the obvious ones; Baron's reflective-essay assignment asks students to raise and then answer objections.[^s1]
3. **Gather enough before you commit.** Treat the urge to stop early as a cue to look once more, since the AOT advantage in the lab came mainly from collecting more evidence.[^s2]
4. **Match confidence to the search.** Report uncertainty that reflects how much you actually checked.[^s1][^s2]
5. **Make it a team norm.** Ask for alternative views in meetings and credit people who raise a good criticism, as Baron recommends for classrooms.[^s1]

**Pitfall:** more information is not always better. In Haran and colleagues' football-forecasting study, extra facts made people more coherent but slightly less accurate on games that ended in upsets, where the available information was misleading.[^s2]

## Evidence

Self-reported AOT is a well-established correlate of good reasoning. Stanovich and Toplak's 2023 review reports that the 30-item scale correlated with all 20 subtests of their Comprehensive Assessment of Rational Thinking and predicted variance beyond cognitive ability on 17 of them, and that it correlated negatively with superstitious thinking (roughly -0.38 to -0.59 across samples) and, more weakly, with conspiracy beliefs.[^s3] In a two-year geopolitical forecasting tournament with 743 forecasters and more than 150,000 forecasts, Mellers and colleagues (2015) found open-mindedness among the dispositional predictors of accuracy, alongside cognitive ability and political knowledge.[^s4]

There are important limits, which is why the evidence status here is mixed. Most findings are correlations with a questionnaire, not experiments that raise AOT and observe better judgment.[^s2][^s3] Older scale versions leaned heavily on items about changing one's "beliefs", which religious respondents may read as their core convictions; when one research team swapped "belief" for "opinion", the scale's correlation with religiosity fell from -0.42 to -0.20.[^s3] Stanovich and Toplak also judge that some items carried an ideological tilt, and they recommend a revised 13-item scale.[^s3] Most strikingly, AOT scores do not reliably predict myside bias, the tendency to evaluate evidence in favour of one's own side, which the authors call a paradox for the construct.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Jonathan Baron, whose 1980s and 1990s work named the disposition.[^s3]
- **Counteracts** the overconfidence effect: higher AOT predicted better-calibrated intervals, largely through more information gathering.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** Haran, Ritov and Mellers's 2013 experiments on information acquisition, accuracy and calibration.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with** need for cognition, a related trait that, unlike AOT, did not predict both information gathering and accuracy in those experiments.[^s2]
- **Studied in** judgment and decision making, where the Society for Judgment and Decision Making's individual-differences inventory catalogues the AOT scale.[^s5]
- **Applied in** geopolitical forecasting, where open-mindedness helped identify the most accurate forecasters.[^s4]
- **Related to** consider the opposite, a technique that puts the search for alternatives into a single step, and to myside bias, which AOT measures fail to predict.[^s3]
