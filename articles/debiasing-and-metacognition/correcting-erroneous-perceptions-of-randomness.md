# Correcting erroneous perceptions of randomness

Correcting erroneous perceptions of randomness is a corrective technique that targets mistaken beliefs about chance at the moment they arise. The person learns that each outcome of a random process is independent of the ones before it and that no strategy can control it. They then learn to notice their own faulty thoughts, such as "a win is due", while they are making the decision, and to replace them with accurate ones.[^s2] It was built as the cognitive core of a treatment for problem gambling, but it addresses the same errors that the gambler's fallacy and the illusion of control produce in any setting where people read patterns into chance.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The technique comes from Robert Ladouceur and colleagues at Université Laval in Québec. Their treatment work started from the observation that gamblers' mistaken beliefs about randomness, and above all the difficulty of applying the principle that events are independent, help keep them playing.[^s2] A 1997 controlled study by Ladouceur and Sylvain, later republished in Spanish, laid out the cognitive-correction component in four steps.[^s2] A larger randomized trial followed in 2001.[^s1] In 2004 Benhsain, Taillefer and Ladouceur tested a lighter version for ordinary players: reminders about the independence of events shown during play.[^s3][^s6]

## How it works

In the Laval protocol the therapist works through four objectives in order.[^s2]

1. **Understand randomness.** Each event is independent of the last, and no strategy or skill can control the outcome.
2. **Understand the erroneous beliefs.** The person learns how the illusion of control keeps them playing, and how hard it is to hold on to the idea of independence in the middle of a game.
3. **Identify inadequate thoughts.** Because the faulty thoughts take over during play, the person practises telling accurate statements about the game from inaccurate ones.
4. **Correct them in the moment.** In an imagined session, the therapist catches and corrects each mistaken statement as it is spoken. The paper's own example is the belief that after four losses in a row, the next try is certain to win.[^s2]

The same moves transfer to work settings. Suppose a sales manager says, "We've lost the last five bids, so this next one is bound to come through." Step one asks whether the bids really are independent. Step three flags the word "bound" as a sign that the manager is treating the long-run win rate as a quota. Step four rewrites the claim: the chance of winning this bid is roughly the usual win rate, adjusted for the merits of this bid, not for the streak.[^s2]

## How to apply it

- **State the independence rule out loud before the decision**, not after it. Benhsain and colleagues gave reminders that events are independent *during* play to 31 occasional gamblers. The reminders reduced erroneous perceptions and lowered motivation to keep playing.[^s3]
- **Capture the thoughts as they happen.** Writing down or saying the reasoning behind a sequential choice brings out phrases such as "due", "overdue" and "my system", which can then be checked one by one.[^s2]
- **Match the method to the goal.** Teaching probability alone improves knowledge more reliably than it changes behaviour, so use in-the-moment correction when the aim is a different choice, not just a right answer on a test.[^s4]
- **Pitfall:** the whole protocol rests on the premise that outcomes are independent and uncontrollable. Where a real mechanism links one outcome to the next, that premise fails, and the dependence has to be analysed rather than corrected away.[^s2]

## Evidence

The clinical results are encouraging but come from packages that bundle the technique with other treatment parts. In the 1997 study, 10 of 14 treated gamblers (71%) reached reference-level functioning on five measures, compared with 1 of 15 wait-list controls. Gains mostly held at 6 and 12 months, but the sample was small, attrition was high, and the authors could not separate cognitive correction from problem solving and relapse prevention.[^s2] The 2001 trial randomized 66 people diagnosed with pathological gambling. Correcting mistaken beliefs about randomness came first, followed by relapse prevention. Treated participants improved significantly on every outcome, including perceived control and urge to gamble, and kept those gains at 12 months.[^s1] A 2012 Cochrane review of 14 trials found that cognitive-behavioural therapy beat control conditions right after treatment in nine studies, but few trials followed people long enough to show whether the benefit lasts.[^s5]

Teaching alone has a weaker record. Williams and Connolly (2006) taught probability using gambling examples to 198 statistics students, compared with 134 students given generic probability teaching and 138 given none. Six months later the gambling-examples group calculated odds better and resisted gambling fallacies more strongly, yet gambled no less.[^s4] That gap between knowing and doing is why the evidence status here is *mixed*, and why the Laval method relies on catching thoughts in the moment.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

- **Counteracts** the gambler's fallacy and the illusion of control. Both are named targets of the protocol, which uses the four-losses-then-a-win belief as its example.[^s2]
- **Introduced by** Robert Ladouceur and his Université Laval group.[^s2]
- **Part of** cognitive-behavioural therapy for gambling problems, the family of treatments the Cochrane review assessed.[^s5]
- **Supported by** the 2001 randomized trial of cognitive treatment and the 2004 independence-reminder study.[^s1][^s3]
- **Contrasts with** plain statistical reasoning training. Teaching the mathematics of chance improved knowledge and resistance to fallacies but not behaviour, whereas this technique corrects specific thoughts during the decision itself.[^s4]
- **Broader:** a form of debiasing; **related to** the representativeness heuristic and deliberate reflection, which deal with the same intuitive pull and the effort to override it.[^s2][^s3]
