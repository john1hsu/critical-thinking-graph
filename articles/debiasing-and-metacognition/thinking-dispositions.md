# Thinking dispositions

Thinking dispositions are the habits and tendencies that decide whether a person actually puts reasoning skills to work: the inclination to look for alternatives, to ask for reasons, to hold a view only as firmly as the evidence allows, and to notice when a situation calls for careful thought at all.[^s1][^s2] The idea answers a puzzle teachers and managers see constantly. Plenty of people can spot a flaw in an argument on a test, yet fail to do it on the job when nobody tells them to look. Skill is necessary, but it is not the whole of good thinking.[^s1]

## Origin

In 1993 the education researchers David Perkins, Eileen Jay and Shari Tishman argued that most accounts of good thinking leaned too heavily on abilities, and proposed a dispositional theory instead.[^s1] They listed seven broad dispositions, such as being broad and adventurous, sustaining intellectual curiosity and being metacognitive, which together characterise good thinking.[^s1]

The philosopher Robert Ennis, whose definition of critical thinking as reasonable reflective thinking about what to believe or do is widely used, offered a leaner account in 1996. He grouped critical-thinking dispositions under three headings: caring to get it right (seeking and staying open to alternatives, believing only as far as the evidence warrants, being well informed), representing positions honestly and clearly, and caring about the dignity and worth of every person.[^s2] A few years earlier P. A. and N. C. Facione had built a disposition questionnaire, discussed below.[^s3]

## How it works

Perkins and colleagues described a disposition as a triad: an **inclination** to think a certain way, a **sensitivity** to occasions that call for it, and the **ability** to carry it out.[^s1] Consider an analyst handed a vendor report claiming a new tool cut processing time by 40%. Ability lets her check the comparison group. Sensitivity makes her notice that a single vendor-run pilot is exactly the kind of claim that deserves checking. Inclination makes her spend the twenty minutes doing it instead of forwarding the headline. Missing any one, and the 40% goes into the budget memo untested.[^s1]

Ennis disputed that triad. He argued a person can be inclined to clarify terms without being good at it or without always noticing when clarity is needed, so sensitivity and ability are not part of what a disposition is, even though they matter for whether it is useful and how it can be measured.[^s2] On his view a disposition is like the fragility of glass: a hidden tendency that shows itself only when conditions trigger it.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Name the trigger.** Decide in advance which situations deserve slow thinking, such as large spends, surprising numbers or claims from interested parties, to build sensitivity.[^s1]
2. **Make the habit cheap.** Short routines, like listing one alternative explanation before accepting a claim, turn inclination into practice.[^s2]
3. **Teach the habit, not just the skill.** Ennis argued dispositions apply across subject areas, even if people show them more in some contexts than others, so they are worth cultivating explicitly.[^s2]

## Evidence

Measurement is the central difficulty. The California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory, developed by the Faciones in 1992, uses 75 Likert-scale items across seven scales: truth-seeking, open-mindedness, analyticity, systematicity, self-confidence, inquisitiveness and maturity.[^s3] Ennis warned that self-report items of this kind are easy for test-wise respondents to fake when their aim is obvious, and hard to interpret when it is not; his own exploratory open-ended format reached high scorer agreement, but two items correlated only .27 with each other.[^s2]

Studies in the heuristics-and-biases tradition suggest dispositions do add something beyond intelligence. In a 2014 study of 216 undergraduates, Klaczynski found that a composite of thinking-disposition scales, including need for cognition, predicted normative answers on reasoning problems (r = .36) and stayed a significant predictor alongside general ability and numeracy; together the predictors explained about 36% of the variance.[^s4] The evidence status is mixed because such findings are correlational, rely on questionnaires and leave most variance unexplained, as Klaczynski himself notes.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Contrasts with** cognitive ability: dispositions and general ability were separate, independent predictors of normative reasoning.[^s4]
- **Demonstrated by** Klaczynski's 2014 study of numeracy, ability and reflectiveness.[^s4]
- **Studied in** cognitive psychology, especially research on individual differences in heuristics-and-biases tasks.[^s4]
- **Applied in** educational assessment through instruments such as the CCTDI.[^s3]
- **Explained by** the triadic theory of inclination, sensitivity and ability proposed by Perkins, Jay and Tishman.[^s1]
- **Narrower** dispositions in this graph include actively open-minded thinking and need for cognition; Ennis's list is the main philosophical alternative to the triadic account.[^s2]
