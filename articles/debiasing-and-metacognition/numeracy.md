# Numeracy

Numeracy is the ability to understand and work with numbers, and in decision research it means above all the ability to grasp probabilities, percentages, proportions and ratios well enough to use them in a judgment.[^s2] It matters to critical thinking because so many consequential claims (a treatment's benefit, a product's failure rate, a forecast's chance of rain) arrive as numbers, and because research by Ellen Peters, Paul Slovic and colleagues suggests that numeracy shapes not only whether people understand such figures but how much their judgments are pushed around by wording and feelings.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The term was coined in 1959 by Geoffrey Crowther of the UK Committee on Education.[^s2] Its modern use in judgment and decision making grew from medical risk communication in the 1990s. Short objective tests followed: a three-item scale by Schwartz and colleagues (1997), an 11-item extension by Lipkus, Samsa and Rimer (2001), and an expanded scale by Peters and colleagues (2007).[^s2] In 2006 Peters, Västfjäll, Slovic and co-authors published four studies in *Psychological Science* arguing that numeracy changes the processes behind decisions, not just comprehension.[^s1] In 2012 Cokely, Galesic and colleagues introduced the Berlin Numeracy Test as a short measure of risk literacy.[^s3]

## How it works

According to Peters's account, numeracy works through two routes. First, more numerate people are more likely to apply the right numerical rule, such as converting between frequencies and percentages, so they are less moved by the surface form of the information.[^s1][^s5] Second, numbers carry clearer and stronger feeling for them: a difference between, say, a 2% and a 9% risk registers as meaningfully good or bad, so numeric information, rather than mood or other irrelevant cues, supplies the affect that guides choice.[^s1][^s5] Numeracy therefore does not remove feeling from judgment; it changes which feelings count.[^s5]

*Workplace example.* Two analysts review a supplier quality report. One reads that 96% of parts pass inspection; the other reads that 4% fail. A less numerate reviewer may rate the supplier noticeably better in the first case, because the positive wording feels better, and may let a good mood after a pleasant site visit colour the assessment too. A more numerate reviewer converts both framings to the same 40 defects per 1,000 parts and compares that figure with the contract threshold. In Peters and colleagues' exam-score study, students rated performance more favourably when scores were framed as percent correct than as percent incorrect, and the framing effect was larger among the less numerate.[^s6]

## How to apply it

- **Restate every figure in at least two forms** (percent and natural frequency, gain and loss) before judging, so that the frame stops doing the work.[^s6][^s2]
- **Ask what the number means for the decision**, such as how far a risk sits from a threshold, so that affect attaches to the quantity rather than to the mood of the meeting.[^s5]
- **Know your own level.** The adaptive Berlin Numeracy Test takes about three minutes and predicted understanding of everyday risks better than older scales in 21 studies with 5,336 participants in 15 countries.[^s3]
- **Design for low numeracy** when communicating risk: order information by relevance, remove nonessential figures and make higher numbers mean better outcomes, all of which helped low-numerate readers in studies reviewed by Reyna and colleagues.[^s2]

## Evidence

Low numeracy is common. In the 2003 US National Assessment of Adult Literacy, 22% of adults scored below basic in quantitative skills, and in early tests by Schwartz and Lipkus most participants got no more than one item right, even in educated samples.[^s2] Reyna and colleagues' 2009 review linked low numeracy to distorted perceptions of screening risks and benefits and to greater susceptibility to mood, framing and ratio-bias effects.[^s2] Peters and colleagues reported that highly numerate participants were less prone to framing and less swayed by irrelevant emotional considerations, and the effects held after controlling for a measure of general intelligence.[^s1]

The limits are real. Reyna and colleagues note that the 2006 findings were mixed: numeracy reduced ratio bias and framing in two of four experiments, and in one case highly numerate people found a bet more attractive when it was objectively worse.[^s2] Most numeracy research is correlational, and the review found no evidence that numeracy screening or targeted numeracy training improves health outcomes.[^s2] For the affect heuristic specifically, Skagerlund and colleagues (2020) found in 41 students that Berlin Numeracy Test scores correlated with weaker risk-benefit coupling (r = 0.44), but the link disappeared after controlling for abstract reasoning ability, whereas cognitive reflection remained a predictor.[^s4] The evidence status here is therefore mixed: numeracy reliably predicts better use of numbers, but whether it is itself the cause, and whether training it debiases, is unsettled.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

- **Counteracts** the affect heuristic: Peters's review reports that more numerate people are less influenced by non-numeric affect such as mood, while their feelings track numeric risk more closely.[^s5] The 2020 Skagerlund study tempers this, as noted above.[^s4]
- **Counteracts** the framing effect: less numerate people showed larger attribute-framing effects in the exam-score study.[^s6]
- **Demonstrated by** the four Peters et al. (2006) studies, which first argued that numeracy alters decision processes.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Geoffrey Crowther, who coined the word in 1959.[^s2]
- **Studied in** judgment and decision making, where it is treated as an individual difference alongside intelligence.[^s1]
- **Related to** the Cognitive Reflection Test and the Berlin Numeracy Test; the latter still predicted risk understanding after controlling for fluid intelligence and the CRT.[^s3]
