# Source-monitoring framework

The source-monitoring framework (SMF) explains how people attribute a mental experience to its origin: whether a detail was perceived or imagined, read in a report or heard from a colleague, learned last week or long ago.[^s1] Its central claim is that memories do not carry a source label. People infer the source from the features a memory contains (perceptual detail, context, emotion, traces of their own thinking), so source errors are a normal by-product of how memory works rather than a sign of something broken.[^s1] For critical thinkers the framework matters because a vague sense that something is familiar is easy to mistake for evidence that it is true, famous or important.

## Origin

Marcia K. Johnson and colleagues developed the framework, building on Johnson and Raye's 1981 work on *reality monitoring* (telling perceived events from imagined ones); it was set out in full by Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay in *Psychological Bulletin* in 1993.[^s1] A 2009 review by Mitchell and Johnson in the same journal surveyed fifteen years of behavioural and brain-imaging work built on it.[^s1]

## How it works

Memories bind together features such as sights, places, times, meanings, feelings and records of the mental operations that produced them, and those bound details are what let a person judge where an experience came from.[^s1] The framework distinguishes two styles of judgment. Undifferentiated signals such as familiarity, fluency or recency support quick, *heuristic* attributions, while retrieving specific details or reasoning about them supports slower, *systematic* ones; the authors treat the two as ends of a continuum, not separate systems.[^s1] How much detail is demanded depends on goals and stakes: chatting with friends needs less than testifying in court.[^s1] Errors become likely when events resemble one another, when prior knowledge or inferences fill gaps, or when a person accepts familiarity without asking where it comes from.[^s1]

The classic demonstration is the *false fame* effect. Jacoby and colleagues had students read a list of made-up names, then judge which names in a later list were famous.[^s2] Tested right away, people remembered the names were from the list and rejected them; tested a day later, the names felt familiar but their source had faded, and they were more often judged famous.[^s2] When attention was divided at test, people lost the ability to use source recollection to oppose familiarity, and the protective effect disappeared.[^s2] The authors concluded that checking the source is a separate, effortful act that people often skip unless prompted.[^s2]

A workplace example: a hiring manager feels sure a candidate's former employer is a "well-known" firm, but on reflection realises the name is familiar only because it appeared in a cold-call email last month. Recalling that specific source exposes the familiarity as irrelevant to the firm's standing, the same kind of check that protected people from false fame when source recollection was easy.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Notice the cue.** When a judgment rests on "this rings a bell", flag it as a heuristic attribution.[^s1]
2. **Ask where the familiarity came from.** Try to recall a specific episode. Pachur and colleagues argue that recalling one particular, unrelated source signals that recognition is unreliable, whereas familiarity spread across many forgotten encounters is more likely to track real prominence.[^s3]
3. **Judge the source's relevance.** In recognition-heuristic studies, recognition-consistent choices dropped considerably when they could attribute recognition to the experiment itself, and only about 30% chose recognized cities known for unrelated reasons over fictional ones in Oppenheimer's 2003 study.[^s3]
4. **Slow down when stakes are high.** Evaluating whether recognition is useful seems to take time; in a low-validity environment, fast decisions followed recognition more often than slow ones.[^s3]
5. **Ask for the source, not just the fact.** In eyewitness research, tests that require people to say *where* they learned each detail reduce, and sometimes eliminate, the uptake of misleading post-event information.[^s4]

## Evidence

Source misattribution is one of the most reproduced phenomena in memory research; the 2009 review integrates a large body of behavioural and neuroimaging studies on source memory, and the framework's account of false memories rests on the same principles as true ones.[^s1] The debiasing record is more conditional. Source-identification tests reduce eyewitness suggestibility, and explicit warnings about misleading information can lower errors further, but warnings also have unintended effects on what people report.[^s4] A 2016 study of 216 participants found that an initial memory test before exposure to misleading details lowered later misinformation recall after a 48-hour delay (about 0.24–0.28 versus 0.43) and cut false source attributions, but it also made people more conservative about correct sources, and the authors note other studies found the opposite pattern.[^s5] Source monitoring therefore helps most when people are prompted, unhurried and have distinct memories to consult; it cannot restore details that were never encoded.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

- It is a narrower case of **metacognition**: it judges the origin of one's own memories and feelings of familiarity, and sits beside other metamemory models such as Nelson and Narens's.[^s1]
- It was **introduced by** Marcia K. Johnson and colleagues and is **studied in** cognitive psychology.[^s1]
- It **counteracts** misuse of the **recognition heuristic**: knowing that recognition stems from an irrelevant or experimental source sharply reduces reliance on it.[^s3]
- It **counteracts** the **false fame effect**, which the 1989 "becoming famous overnight" experiments **demonstrate**: recollecting that a name came from a study list opposes the familiarity that would otherwise make it seem famous.[^s2]
- It **counteracts** the **misinformation effect** in eyewitness memory when people are asked to identify the source of each detail.[^s4][^s5]
- It is related to processing fluency and the illusory truth effect, which also turn ease of processing into a judgment cue, and to source evaluation, its information-literacy counterpart for external documents.[^s1][^s3]
