# Social norms approach

The social norms approach is a technique for correcting misperceived norms: people's inaccurate beliefs about how common an attitude or behaviour is among their peers.[^s1] It rests on two observations. Perceptions of what peers do and approve of shape our own choices, and those perceptions are often wrong, typically overestimating risky or unhealthy behaviour and underestimating healthy behaviour.[^s1] The remedy is to measure the real norm and tell the group what it is.[^s2]

## Origin

The approach grew out of college alcohol research. H. Wesley Perkins and Alan Berkowitz's 1986 study on a single undergraduate campus found that more than three-quarters of students held moderate views about intoxication, yet almost two-thirds believed their peers accepted frequent drunkenness.[^s2] Perkins later described the core idea as simply communicating the truth about peer norms, and through the 1990s campuses ran campaigns built on it, with larger surveys (for example, 17,592 students at 140 institutions in a 1996 study with Henry Wechsler) showing the misperception was widespread.[^s2] Practitioners describe it as a blend of social marketing, sociology, behavioural psychology and evaluation research.[^s1]

## How it works

The approach targets several distinct errors in social perception. Under *pluralistic ignorance*, most members of a group misjudge the group norm; under the *false consensus effect*, an individual wrongly assumes most of the group shares their own beliefs and behaviour.[^s1] Borsari and Carey's 2003 meta-analysis names false consensus as one source of these self-other gaps, alongside the tendency to attribute peers' visible behaviour to their character.[^s3] Perkins adds that vivid, memorable incidents and media coverage inflate the perceived norm, and that heavy drinkers come to feel part of a comfortable but fictitious majority, which then becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy.[^s2]

Workplace example: a team lead believes "everyone here answers email after 10 p.m., so I have to as well." An anonymous team survey shows that only a small minority do and most would prefer not to. Sharing that result corrects the lead's projection and the group's shared overestimate at the same time, and removes a pressure nobody actually endorsed, the same logic campus campaigns applied to drinking.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Measure the actual norm.** Run a credible, anonymous survey of the specific reference group; campaigns must rest on current, accurate data about the audience.[^s1]
2. **Measure the perceived norm.** Ask the same people what they think peers do or approve of; the gap between the two is the target.[^s3]
3. **Communicate the accurate, positive norm.** Frame the message around what most people already do ("most of us…") rather than scare tactics or "should" messages from authorities.[^s1] Campuses used orientation sessions, newspaper ads, radio, posters and small-group workshops for high-risk groups.[^s2]
4. **Choose the reference group carefully.** Misperceptions were largest for distant, generic reference groups ("the typical student") and for injunctive norms (what peers approve of) rather than descriptive ones (what they do), so local, specific norms make better messages.[^s3]
5. **Expect a long horizon.** Campaigns are culture-change efforts that take more than a year, and they are undermined when the wider environment keeps promoting the behaviour.[^s1]

## Evidence

The premise is well supported: Borsari and Carey pooled 102 tests from 23 studies with 53,825 participants (literature to February 2002) and found students consistently overestimated peers' drinking and approval of drinking.[^s3] Early campaigns reported drops in high-risk drinking of up to 20% in short periods, though Perkins conceded these evaluations could be criticised methodologically.[^s2]

Whether correcting the misperception changes behaviour enough to matter is less clear. The 2015 Cochrane review of 70 trials with 44,958 students found statistically detectable but very small effects at four months or more, for example about 0.9 fewer drinks per week from a baseline of 13.7, with low- to moderate-quality evidence; its authors judged the benefits too small for policy or practice.[^s4] The node is therefore marked *mixed*: the technique reliably documents and can narrow perception gaps, but its downstream behavioural effects are modest and context-dependent.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** H. Wesley Perkins and Alan Berkowitz, whose 1986 campus study first identified the misperception the approach targets.[^s2]
- **Counteracts** the false consensus effect, which Borsari and Carey list as one source of biased norm perception that the approach corrects with actual data.[^s3]
- **Counteracts** pluralistic ignorance, the group-level misperception of a norm that campaigns are designed to dispel.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Borsari and Carey's 2003 meta-analysis documenting consistent self-other discrepancies in perceived norms.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** the 2015 Cochrane review, which found the behavioural effects of social norms interventions too small to be meaningful.[^s4]
- **Related to** the fundamental attribution error and the availability heuristic, two mechanisms (dispositional attributions and vivid incidents) that inflate perceived norms.[^s2][^s3]
