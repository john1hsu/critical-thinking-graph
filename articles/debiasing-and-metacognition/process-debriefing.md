# Process debriefing

Process debriefing is a way of correcting a false impression that goes one step beyond saying "that information was wrong". Alongside the retraction, the person is told how such impressions work: that people tend to build explanations around information they receive, and that those explanations can keep a belief alive after the information itself has been withdrawn. Armed with that account, the person can check their own lingering impression and discount it.[^s1] It is the remedy that the original belief perseverance research proposed for its own finding, and it remains a useful model for anyone who must retract a claim and wants the retraction to stick.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The technique comes from Lee Ross, Mark Lepper and Michael Hubbard's 1975 experiments in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. Participants received false success or failure feedback on a novel discrimination task and were then told the feedback had been predetermined and random. After this standard "outcome" debriefing, both participants and observers still held impressions that matched the false feedback. When the researchers instead gave a "process" debriefing that explicitly discussed the perseverance process, false self-perceptions were generally eliminated.[^s1] The authors linked the result to the ethics of deception research: if a bare retraction leaves false self-beliefs behind, researchers owe participants more than a bare retraction.[^s1]

## How it works

Process debriefing targets the mechanism Ross and colleagues proposed for belief perseverance: people generate causal explanations for the feedback they receive, and those explanations survive the retraction as apparently independent support.[^s1] Explaining that mechanism gives people a reason to distrust the explanations as well as the original data.

Consider a manager who tells a new analyst, after a trial run, that the analyst's forecast was the least accurate on the team. A day later it turns out the scores were swapped in the spreadsheet. An outcome-style correction would be: "Sorry, that ranking was a data error; ignore it." A process-style correction adds: "Once people hear a result like that, they usually start finding reasons for it, such as being new to the tools or rushing, and those reasons can stick even after the result is withdrawn. If any of that has crossed your mind, it was built on a number that never applied to you." The second version names the leftover reasoning, which is what the analyst would otherwise carry into the next forecast, mirroring the outcome-versus-process contrast in the original experiments.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. **Retract clearly.** State what was false and why; a vague retraction gives nothing to work with.[^s4]
2. **Name the persistence process.** Explain that impressions and self-generated reasons tend to outlast the evidence that prompted them.[^s1]
3. **Be specific about the false content.** In a 2022 study of 439 participants, an enhanced debriefing that explained how memory errors arise and explicitly identified the misinformation used eliminated the post-debriefing misinformation effect, outperforming a typical debriefing.[^s4]
4. **Allow time and conversation.** Across six studies, a prolonged, personal debriefing worked best of the procedures tested after false feedback about intelligence or attractiveness, whereas written and brief in-person debriefings did not remove the effects on well-being.[^s3]
5. **Check, and follow up.** Ask whether the person still holds the belief, and revisit it later; in a 2024 preregistered study, belief in an implanted false childhood event fell from 52% to under 7% after debriefing and stayed low three days on.[^s5]

Pitfalls: it takes effort from the listener, and people who cannot or will not do that reflective work may benefit more from a direct statement that the underlying test or measure was invalid.[^s2]

## Evidence

The evidence is supportive for beliefs but weaker for feelings. A 2007 replication by McFarland, Cheam and Buehler, as summarised in a Saarland University dissertation, again found the standard outcome debriefing ineffective and process debriefing more effective; a revised outcome debriefing that told participants the test itself was invalid did even better.[^s2] The limits come from work on emotional effects. Miketta and Friese's 2019 studies found that negative false feedback continued to lower well-being and raise hostile attributions after several kinds of thorough debriefing, with effects lasting two weeks or more in follow-ups; every version reduced the harm somewhat, but none removed it fully.[^s3] Those studies tested outcome-style and extended personal debriefings rather than the 1975 process script, so they bound the claim rather than refute it.[^s2][^s3] The fair reading is that explaining the persistence process reliably helps undo false self-perceptions, while mood and self-esteem effects can outlast any single debriefing.

## Connections

- **Counteracts** belief perseverance: it was introduced by Lee Ross and colleagues in the 1975 debriefing study that demonstrated the effect, as the remedy that eliminated it.[^s1]
- **Counteracts** the misinformation effect and is **supported by** Greenspan and Loftus's 2022 enhanced-debriefing study, which used the same idea of explaining how the error arises.[^s4]
- **Challenged by** Miketta and Friese's 2019 ego-threat studies, which show that thorough debriefings only partly reverse emotional harm.[^s3]
- A form of **debiasing**, related to consider-the-opposite, which also asks people to work against their own explanations, and to debunking, prebunking and the continued influence effect, where retractions of public misinformation face the same leftover-belief problem.[^s1][^s4]
- Understanding belief perseverance is a prerequisite, since the technique works by teaching that phenomenon.[^s1][^s2]
