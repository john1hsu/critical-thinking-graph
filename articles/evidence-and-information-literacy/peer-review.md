# Peer review

Peer review is the process in which an editor sends a submitted manuscript to independent specialists in the same field, whose reports inform the decision to accept, request revisions or reject. Most scholarly journals use it, and it is often called a cornerstone of scientific publishing, yet its main functions are poorly defined and it consumes a large share of publishing costs, an estimated £1.9 billion a year worldwide in one 2016 estimate.[^s2] For a critical thinker, the label "peer reviewed" is a useful signal about a source, but a much weaker guarantee than many readers assume.[^s1]

## Origin

Refereeing grew out of learned-society publishing rather than any single inventor. Historian Aileen Fyfe reports that the Royal Society took formal responsibility for the *Philosophical Transactions* in 1752 but only adopted written referee reports in the 1830s, with other British societies doing similar things around then.[^s5] Early referees mostly flagged logical slips, factual mistakes and missing credit to earlier work, and the system served partly to spread responsibility for editorial decisions; the Society even denied that publication certified truth.[^s5] The modern idea of peer review as "quality control" only took hold in the mid-twentieth century.[^s5] Systematic research on whether it works began later still, with the first International Congress on Peer Review in Biomedical Publication in 1989.[^s2]

## How it works

A typical journal workflow runs: the editor screens a submission, invites two or three reviewers, who judge its originality, methods, analysis and whether the conclusions follow from the data, and then decides using their reports. Variants differ in who knows whom: single-blind (reviewers know the authors), double-blind (neither side knows) and open review (identities revealed).[^s2]

Workplace example: a product team wants to adopt a new onboarding design because a vendor's white paper reports a 30 percent rise in retention. A colleague points out that a peer-reviewed study on a similar intervention found a small effect. Peer review gives the second source some independent scrutiny the white paper lacks, but it does not by itself show that the study's sample, measures or analysis fit the team's situation; the team still has to read the methods.[^s1][^s4]

## How to apply it

Treat peer review as one input to source evaluation, not a verdict:

1. **Check that it really happened.** Confirm the venue is a recognised journal that reviews submissions; preprints, conference abstracts and press releases usually have not been reviewed.[^s2]
2. **Ask what review can catch.** Reviewers see a write-up, not the raw data, and in a trial at the *BMJ* reviewers given papers seeded with nine major methodological errors found under three of them on average.[^s4] Read the methods yourself, or look for a statistical reviewer's input, which was the one intervention with evidence of improving final papers.[^s2]
3. **Look for agreement across studies.** A single reviewed paper can still be wrong; systematic reviews and replications carry more weight than any one publication.[^s1]
4. **Don't treat rejection or acceptance as proof.** Reviewer judgments can be inconsistent, so "rejected by a journal" or "published in a journal" is weak evidence about truth on its own.[^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence that editorial peer review improves research quality is thin. A 2007 Cochrane review of 28 studies found little empirical support for it as a quality-assurance mechanism; it found some evidence that review improves readability and reporting, and no clear benefit from blinding or reviewer training, while stressing that absence of evidence is not evidence of ineffectiveness.[^s1] A 2016 meta-analysis of 22 randomised trials found that reviewer training and checklists did not improve outcomes, blinding authors made no difference, open review modestly improved report quality, and adding a statistical reviewer improved final manuscripts in two trials from a single journal.[^s2]

Studies of reliability are more worrying. Peters and Ceci (1982) resubmitted twelve already-published psychology papers, with invented author names and institutions, to the journals that had printed them; only three were recognised, and eight of the remaining nine were rejected, often for "serious methodological flaws".[^s3] In the 2008 *BMJ* trial of 607 reviewers, short training packages produced only slight gains in error detection.[^s4] The overall picture is mixed: review seems to polish reporting and catch some problems, but it is neither a reliable error filter nor a consistent judge of quality.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Scholarly communication** (broader): peer review is one component of the system by which research is vetted and published, alongside editing and indexing.[^s5]
- **Challenged by the Jefferson 2007 Cochrane review**, which found little empirical evidence that it ensures quality.[^s1]
- **Challenged by Peters and Ceci's resubmission study**, which showed inconsistent judgments on previously accepted papers.[^s3]
- **Challenged by Schroter et al.'s 2008 trial**, in which reviewers missed most deliberately inserted major errors.[^s4]
- **Supported, in part, by Bruce et al.'s 2016 meta-analysis**, which found that specific variants (open review, statistical reviewers) can improve review or manuscript quality.[^s2]
- **Related** to source evaluation and the hierarchy of evidence, because readers use review status as a credibility cue, and to publication bias and the replication crisis, which show that reviewed literatures can still be systematically distorted.[^s1][^s2]
