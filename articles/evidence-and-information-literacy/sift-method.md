# SIFT

SIFT is a short routine for deciding whether an online source or claim deserves your trust, built from four moves: **S**top, **I**nvestigate the source, **F**ind better coverage, and **T**race claims, quotes and media back to their original context.[^s1] Rather than asking a reader to audit a page against a long list of features, it sends the reader off the page to see what others say about the source and the claim, and it is designed to take seconds, often well under a minute, for everyday checks.[^s1] In that sense it packages lateral reading into a sequence that can be taught and remembered.[^s4]

## Origin

The method was developed by Mike Caulfield, a digital-literacy educator, between 2016 and 2021.[^s1] It first appeared in his 2017 open textbook *Web Literacy for Student Fact-Checkers* as "four moves and a habit", drawing on Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew's Stanford observations of how professional fact checkers judged websites; a June 2019 blog post gave it the SIFT acronym.[^s1] Caulfield presented it as an alternative to checklist approaches such as the CRAAP test, which he argued suited library databases better than the open web.[^s1] In 2023 he and Wineburg expanded the approach into a book, *Verified*, published by the University of Chicago Press, which frames the skill partly as learning what to ignore when attention is scarce.[^s4]

## How it works

Each move answers a different question.[^s1][^s5]

- **Stop.** Before reading deeply, reacting or sharing, pause and ask whether you know this source and what you want from it. If you notice a strong emotional pull, treat that as a cue to slow down.[^s1][^s2]
- **Investigate the source.** Spend a moment learning who is behind the content, what their expertise and agenda are, and whom they are writing for.[^s5]
- **Find better coverage.** If the source itself matters less than the claim, look for other reporting or expert analysis on the same claim and see whether it agrees, disagrees or adds context.[^s1][^s5]
- **Trace to the original.** When a post summarises a study, quotes someone or reuses a photo, follow it back to the original and check whether it was represented accurately.[^s1][^s5]

A workplace example: a colleague circulates a screenshot of a chart, attributed to an industry survey, showing that remote teams are "40% less productive". Stopping, you notice that you were ready to forward it because it confirms what you already thought. Investigating, you find the account that posted it is a vendor selling office-occupancy software. Finding better coverage, you see that trade outlets reported the same survey with a narrower conclusion. Tracing, you locate the survey report and discover that the chart compared only two departments and measured hours logged, not output. The claim was not invented, but its framing was not supported, and four quick moves showed that.[^s1][^s5]

## How to apply it

1. **Use it as a triage, not an audit.** The goal is a fast, good-enough judgment about whether a source is worth your time, and deeper research only when the stakes justify it.[^s1]
2. **Pick the move that fits the problem.** A viral image calls mostly for tracing; an unfamiliar website calls for investigating the source; a surprising claim from a known outlet calls for finding other coverage.[^s1][^s5]
3. **Treat "better" coverage as "other" coverage when needed.** Some library guides note that alternative sources may represent different perspectives rather than simply better or worse ones.[^s5]
4. **Practise repeatedly.** Instructors who replaced a checklist with SIFT found that students used it well only when they met it early and practised it several times; mentioning it once was not enough.[^s3]

**Pitfalls:** each check is only as good as what search engines and reference sites return, which is why *Verified* spends chapters on the strengths and weaknesses of Google and Wikipedia.[^s4] Reviewers have also noted that the moves can sound like common sense, which may lead learners to underrate the practice needed to make them habitual.[^s4]

## Evidence

The strongest test so far is a quasi-experimental study in a first-year civics course at a large public university. In 2021, Brodsky and colleagues, with Caulfield as a co-author, compared 136 students taught the moves with 94 students in matched sections without the instruction. At posttest the taught group averaged 2.22 on a 0–4 fact-checking rubric versus 1.15 for controls, and about 61% of taught students reached a correct judgment by reading laterally on at least one problem, against almost none of the controls.[^s2] The authors caution that sections were not randomly assigned, delivery was not fully controlled, the study came from one institution, there was no retention test, and students' self-reports did not match their actual behaviour.[^s2]

Classroom reports point the same way but are weaker. A 2023 report on two asynchronous one-credit library courses observed students relying on surface cues such as a ".org" ending under a CRAAP-based design and using SIFT productively after a redesign, but it had no control group, very small numbers and a simultaneous change of search platform.[^s3] Because the evidence is promising but limited, from few research groups and short follow-up, this node's evidence status is mixed.

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Mike Caulfield, who developed the moves and the acronym.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with** the CRAAP test and other checklist methods that judge a source by inspecting its own features.[^s1][^s3]
- **Counteracts** misinformation and disinformation, as shown by improved judgments of fake news, clickbait health claims and misattributed photos in the civics-course study.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** the 2021 civics-course study of first-year students.[^s2]
- **Applied in** information-literacy instruction, including credit-bearing library courses.[^s3]
- **Related to** lateral reading, the fact-checker habit that SIFT turns into a routine,[^s4] and to click restraint, source credibility and prebunking as neighbouring tools for judging and resisting misleading content.[^s1]
