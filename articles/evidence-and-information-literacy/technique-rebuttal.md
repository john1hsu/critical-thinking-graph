# Technique rebuttal

A technique rebuttal answers a misleading argument by exposing how it misleads rather than by disputing what it claims. Instead of supplying the correct facts about vaccines or the climate, the respondent names the rhetorical move the speaker relies on, for example demanding an impossible standard of certainty or cherry-picking a handful of studies.[^s1][^s3] Its counterpart is the topic rebuttal, which corrects the substantive claims with the evidence on the subject.[^s1] Researchers in the misinformation literature also call it a technique-based or logic-based correction.[^s5][^s6]

## Origin

The topic/technique distinction was set out and tested by the psychologists Philipp Schmid and Cornelia Betsch at the University of Erfurt in a 2019 *Nature Human Behaviour* paper reporting six experiments and an internal meta-analysis.[^s1] Their technique rebuttals drew on catalogues of tactics that recur in science denial, such as impossible expectations and selective use of evidence.[^s2][^s3] The idea sits within a wider programme, associated with John Cook and colleagues, that treats misinformation as a set of reusable reasoning flaws; Cook's group later proposed a "fact-myth-fallacy" structure in which a correction states the fact, flags the myth and then explains the fallacy behind it.[^s5]

## How it works

In the Schmid and Betsch design, online participants heard or read a debate in which a denier argued against vaccination or climate action. An advocate then either stayed silent, gave a topic rebuttal, gave a technique rebuttal, or did both.[^s2] One example of a technique rebuttal: when the denier says vaccines are not 100% safe, the advocate points out that this is an impossible expectation, because no medical procedure meets that bar.[^s2]

A workplace version: in a supplier review, a speaker argues that a proposed cloud migration should be dropped because "nobody can guarantee zero downtime." A topic rebuttal would cite the provider's uptime record. A technique rebuttal says instead that the objection sets a standard no option, including the current system, can meet, so it cannot distinguish between the choices on the table. The second answer does not require the respondent to know the uptime figures, and it transfers to the next objection built the same way.[^s3]

That transferability is the technique's main attraction. Tactics repeat across topics, so a respondent who learns a short list can answer claims in fields where they lack deep expertise, whereas topic rebuttals require keeping up with a large and changing research literature.[^s3]

## How to apply it

1. **Identify the move, not just the claim.** Ask what makes the argument persuasive despite being weak: a fake expert, cherry-picked data, an impossible standard, a conspiracy explanation or a logical fallacy.[^s5]
2. **Name it plainly and show why it misleads.** Explain what a fair version of the argument would need, such as the full body of evidence rather than a selected few studies.[^s3][^s5]
3. **Pair it with the facts where you can.** Cook's group recommends combining a fact-based element with a technique-based one.[^s5]
4. **Under volume, rebut the tactic, not every item.** Facing a Gish gallop, a 2024 *Scientific American* opinion piece advises against trying to refute each claim in the time available; it recommends exposing the tactic to the audience and then returning to one's own case.[^s4]

**Pitfalls.** Naming a fallacy is not by itself a refutation; a University of Maryland course on argumentation warns that one must still show the conclusion is wrong, and that fallacy labels are approximate because many fallacies shade into each other.[^s8] The 2024 opinion piece frames the counter as openly calling out the speaker's dishonesty;[^s4] the Maryland course's advice to respond to what people actually argue is a reminder to keep the focus on the flaw in the argument rather than on the arguer.[^s8]

## Evidence

Across Schmid and Betsch's six experiments, exposure to a denier lowered attitudes and intentions toward vaccination; both topic and technique rebuttals reduced that damage to a similar degree, combining them added nothing measurable, and there was no evidence of a backfire effect, including among US conservatives.[^s1][^s2] The effects were weaker in the climate experiment, and outcomes were self-reported attitudes and intentions in online samples rather than behaviour.[^s2][^s3] A 2020 Instagram experiment by Vraga, Kim, Cook and Bode, as summarised by Cook, found logic-based corrections worked whether they came before or after the myth, while fact-based corrections were cancelled when the myth came second.[^s6] Results are not uniform, however: a 2019 study from the George Mason group found that logic-based and humour-based corrections reduced misperceptions about HPV vaccination but not about climate change or gun control.[^s7] Cook's group noted in 2024 that there is little research on how best to answer a message containing several fallacies at once, which is exactly the Gish-gallop situation.[^s5] The evidence status is therefore *mixed*: technique rebuttals help in several settings, but effects vary by topic and are modest.

## Connections

- **Narrower than debunking.** It is one way to correct misinformation after exposure, alongside fact-based correction.[^s5]
- **Counteracts misinformation and disinformation.** Schmid and Betsch found that technique rebuttals reduced the influence of denialist messages in public discussions.[^s1]
- **Counteracts the Gish gallop.** Exposing the flooding tactic, rather than chasing each claim, is the counter recommended for that format.[^s4]
- **Demonstrated by Schmid and Betsch (2019).** Their six experiments are the main test of the technique.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with prebunking.** A technique rebuttal is reactive, while prebunking teaches the same tactics in advance; Sander van der Linden, quoted in coverage of the 2019 study, argued that pre-emptive inoculation is preferable and rebuttal is a second line of defence.[^s3]
- **Related to Brandolini's law and informal fallacies.** Because refuting nonsense costs more than producing it, a response aimed at the reusable fallacy rather than each claim economises on effort.[^s3][^s4]
