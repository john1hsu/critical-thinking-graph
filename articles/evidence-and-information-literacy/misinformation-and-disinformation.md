# Misinformation and disinformation

Misinformation is false information that circulates without anyone intending harm; disinformation is false information deliberately created to hurt a person, group, organization or country.[^s1] Psychologists studying belief often use "misinformation" as an umbrella for any information that later turns out to be false, treating disinformation as the intentional subset and noting that the boundary is hard to draw in practice.[^s2] For a critical thinker the distinction matters because it changes the question you ask: with misinformation you ask whether the claim is true, while with disinformation you also ask who benefits from your believing it.[^s1]

## Origin

Both words are much older than social media, but the now-standard three-way typology comes from Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan's 2017 report for the Council of Europe, *Information Disorder*.[^s1] They placed mis- and disinformation alongside a third category, malinformation (genuine information, such as a leaked private document, used to inflict harm), under the umbrella of "information disorder".[^s1] The report also proposed analysing any case through three elements (the agent who creates and spreads it, the message itself, and the interpreter who receives it) and three phases (creation, production and distribution), on the grounds that the same message can shift category as it passes from a deliberate originator to sincere sharers.[^s1]

## How it works

A typical workplace case: a forwarded screenshot claims the company is cancelling a benefit next month. Someone in another office invented it to stir up anger, which makes it disinformation at the source; colleagues who share it because they are worried are spreading misinformation, since they believe it.[^s1] Several psychological drivers help such a claim stick. People often judge headlines by quick intuition rather than deliberation; repetition makes a claim feel familiar and therefore true, even when people know better; and they often forget where they first heard something, so a dubious origin is not discounted later.[^s2] Emotional content, trust in in-group messengers and the belief that others agree add social and affective pressure.[^s2]

Even after the benefit rumour is officially denied, some staff may keep acting as if it were partly true. This is the continued influence effect: misinformation keeps shaping reasoning after a correction has been understood and remembered, probably because the correction is poorly integrated with the original story or because the claim is later recalled without its "false" tag.[^s2] That is why a bare retraction tends to work worse than a correction that supplies a plausible alternative account of what is really happening.[^s2]

## How to spot and respond to it

- Check the agent before the message: who first published this, and what would they gain if you believed it?[^s1]
- Treat strong emotion, novelty and a sense that "everyone is saying it" as cues to slow down, not as evidence.[^s2][^s3]
- When correcting, lead with the accurate account, mention the false claim only once with a warning, explain why it is wrong, and use a messenger the audience trusts.[^s2]
- Expect corrections to fade over weeks; important ones may need repeating.[^s2]

## Evidence

The best-known large-scale study, Vosoughi, Roy and Aral (2018), analysed about 126,000 rumour cascades shared by some 3 million Twitter users between 2006 and 2017. Stories rated false by six independent fact-checking organisations spread further, faster and deeper than true ones, most strongly for political news; automated accounts amplified true and false stories at similar rates, so the difference was driven by human sharing, and false stories tended to be more novel.[^s3]

On correction, the 2022 review by Ecker and colleagues concluded that "backfire" effects, where a correction strengthens the false belief, are rare and often failed to replicate, so the fear of backfire should not generally deter debunking; it also cautioned that much of the evidence comes from small, mainly US samples.[^s2] Evidence status is set to *mixed* because the size of the problem is disputed. Altay, Berriche and Acerbi (2023) argue that alarmist accounts overstate how prevalent and influential online misinformation is: most online attention goes to entertainment rather than news, engagement is not the same as belief, false content mostly reaches people who already agree, and people are more often uninformed than misinformed.[^s4] They add that whether falsehood "travels faster than truth" depends on how misinformation is defined and sampled.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** information disorder, the umbrella that also covers malinformation.[^s1]
- **Formalized by** the 2017 Council of Europe *Information Disorder* report, which supplied the intent-based typology and the agent-message-interpreter framework.[^s1]
- **Explained by** the illusory truth effect, through which repeated exposure makes false claims feel true; related to the continued influence effect, which explains why corrections only partly work.[^s2]
- **Studied in** cognitive psychology, which investigates why people believe and resist correcting false claims.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** Vosoughi, Roy and Aral's 2018 study of true and false news diffusion on Twitter.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** Altay, Berriche and Acerbi's 2023 critique of inflated prevalence and impact claims.[^s4]
- **Counteracted by** prebunking, which warns people about manipulation techniques in advance and can generalise across topics, and by debunking, which corrects a claim after exposure; source credibility matters to both, because trusted messengers make corrections more persuasive.[^s2] Lateral reading and the SIFT method, covered in their own articles, target the same problem from the information-literacy side.
