# Source credibility

Source credibility is how far the origin of a claim deserves, or is thought to deserve, belief on the question at hand. Research in persuasion has treated it as a judgment about the communicator rather than the message, built mainly from perceived expertise and trustworthiness, and it treats credibility as a cue people use when deciding whether to accept what they read or hear.[^s3] In philosophy the same issue appears as the problem of testimony: most of what anyone knows comes from other people, so the question is what makes it reasonable to take their word for it.[^s4]

## Origin

The experimental study of credibility began in Carl Hovland's communication research programme at Yale. In a 1951 experiment, Hovland and Weiss gave students identical articles on four topics (for example, selling antihistamines without prescription and the future of cinemas), attributed either to sources students rated as trustworthy, such as a medical journal, or to sources they rated untrustworthy, such as a gossip columnist.[^s1] The epistemological question is far older: Adler traces it to the contrast between Hume, for whom trust in testimony rests on observed experience that reports tend to match the facts, and Reid, who held that people are by nature disposed both to tell the truth and to believe what they are told.[^s4]

## How it works

Hovland and Weiss found that the source changed how a message was received but not how much of it was learned. Readers judged low-credibility versions less fair, and opinion moved in the advocated direction far more after high-credibility attributions (about 23% net change versus about 7%), yet scores on factual quiz questions about the content were almost identical.[^s1] Four weeks later the gap had closed: agreement with high-credibility versions had fallen and agreement with low-credibility versions had risen, suggesting people remember the content longer than they remember where it came from.[^s1] That delayed rise is the sleeper effect. A 2004 meta-analysis of 72 data sets found it is small on average and appears reliably only when the discrediting information comes after a message that recipients were able and motivated to think about.[^s2]

A workplace example: a team lead circulates a report claiming a vendor's software causes outages. If the report comes from the internal reliability team, it moves opinion at once; if it comes from a rival vendor's marketing department, readers discount it. A month later, though, people may recall the claim about outages and forget that it came from a competitor, which is exactly when the discount silently disappears.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Philosophers disagree about how much checking is required before accepting testimony. Reductionists want positive reasons to think a speaker reliable, often from background knowledge such as reputation, professional sanctions and the prior plausibility of what is said; anti-reductionists, following Burge, hold that we may accept intelligible testimony unless something counts against it.[^s4] Fricker's position sits between them: a hearer should always do some assessment of a speaker's trustworthiness, because accepting without it is gullibility.[^s4] In practice that means asking two separate questions of any source: is it in a position to know (expertise, access), and does it have reasons to tell the truth or to shade it (trustworthiness, interests)? Because the sleeper effect shows that source tags fade faster than content, note the source when you record a claim, so that a discount you applied today is still attached to the claim when you reuse it later.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

Credibility effects are real but moderated. A 2010 meta-analysis of 54 data sets from 18 reports found that credible sources persuaded more than non-credible ones both immediately (d = 0.35) and at a delay (d = 0.21), with the advantage shrinking over time.[^s3] The effect was largest when audiences had no prior attitude and little knowledge of the topic, and much weaker when people already held views, which the authors interpret with dual-process models such as the elaboration likelihood model: credibility works as an easy shortcut for people who cannot or will not process the arguments themselves.[^s3] Two limits follow for critical thinkers. A credible source can still be wrong, and credibility shifts opinion without improving what people learn from the message, so credibility is a reason to raise or lower confidence, not a substitute for checking evidence when the stakes justify it.[^s1] Even in the testimony literature, the prior plausibility of what is said counts alongside the speaker's standing, so an implausible claim from a reputable source still deserves scrutiny.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by the Hovland and Weiss 1951 study.** The experiment held content constant and varied only the attributed source, isolating the effect of credibility on opinion and showing its decay over four weeks.[^s1]
- **Introduced by Carl Hovland.** Hovland's Yale group framed credibility as a variable in communication effectiveness and co-authored the founding experiment.[^s1]
- **Supported by the 2010 meta-analysis.** Kumkale, Albarracín and Seignourel pooled 54 data sets and confirmed a persuasive advantage for credible sources, strongest for audiences without prior attitudes.[^s3]
- **Explained by the elaboration likelihood model.** The same meta-analysis reads credibility as a peripheral cue used most when ability or motivation to process is low.[^s3]
- **Studied in epistemology.** The epistemology of testimony asks when relying on a speaker is justified, which is the normative counterpart of credibility research.[^s4]
- **Related.** The sleeper effect is the delayed side of credibility: discounting a source suppresses persuasion at first, but the discount can fade faster than the message.[^s2] Lateral reading and SIFT are practical ways to check a source, and ad hominem and tu quoque are what goes wrong when an attack on a source replaces engagement with an argument that does not rest on its say-so.
