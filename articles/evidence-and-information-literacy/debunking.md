# Debunking

Debunking is the after-the-fact correction of misinformation: once a false or misleading claim is circulating, you confront it with a refutation that explains why it is wrong and what is true instead.[^s1] It is the counterpart of prebunking, which tries to build resistance before people meet the claim.[^s1] Research since the 1990s shows that corrections reliably reduce belief in a false claim but rarely erase its influence completely, so the craft of debunking is mostly about making corrections stick.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Debunking as a research topic grew out of memory studies of the continued influence effect, in which people keep using a piece of information in their reasoning even after they have accepted and can recall a retraction of it.[^s1] Ecker and colleagues explain the effect as the old claim and the correction both staying in memory and competing for retrieval, rather than the correction overwriting the original.[^s1] A 2017 meta-analysis by Chan, Jones, Hall Jamieson and Albarracín pooled 20 experiments with 6,878 participants published between 1994 and 2015 and established the basic picture: corrections help, but misinformation persists.[^s2]

Practical guidance was consolidated by Stephan Lewandowsky, John Cook and Ullrich Ecker in *The Debunking Handbook 2020*, which replaced Cook and Lewandowsky's 2011 handbook. Its 22 authors used a preregistered, IPCC-style process in which contributors rated candidate phenomena and recommendations, and only items with majority support were included.[^s4]

## How it works

A bare "that's false" leaves a hole in the person's mental model of why something happened, and the familiar false explanation tends to flow back into that hole.[^s1] Effective debunks therefore do three things: they replace the myth with a causal alternative, explain why the myth seemed plausible and why it is wrong, and place the correction right next to the myth so the inconsistency is noticed.[^s1][^s4]

Ecker and colleagues recommend a sequence often summarised as fact, myth, fallacy, fact: lead with the accurate account, mention the myth once with a warning that it is false, explain the reasoning error or technique that makes it misleading, and end by restating the fact.[^s1][^s4]

Workplace example: a rumour spreads that a product launch slipped because the new engineering lead "rewrote everything". A manager's correction might read: "The launch moved because a supplier's chip shipment arrived six weeks late (fact). You may have heard the delay was caused by a code rewrite (warning plus myth). That story took hold because the code changes happened in the same month, but timing alone doesn't show cause; the build was ready before the parts were (fallacy explained). The new date is set by the supplier's delivery (fact)." The alternative cause gives people something to put in place of the rumour rather than an empty space, which is the gap-filling role reviews assign to the alternative explanation.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. **Pick your battles.** Debunk when a falsehood is spreading widely or could cause harm; a claim few people will ever see is rarely worth the effort of a correction.[^s1][^s4]
2. **Lead with the fact** in plain, memorable language, ideally the causal alternative.[^s1]
3. **Name the myth once**, flagged as false and placed directly before the correction; do not keep repeating it.[^s4]
4. **Explain the fallacy.** Say why the claim is misleading, for example a confusion of timing with cause, cherry-picking or a fake expert.[^s1][^s4]
5. **Give detail and invite reasoning.** Corrections with new evidence beat simple labels, and audiences who work out for themselves why the claim is wrong hold on to the correction better.[^s2]
6. **Use trusted messengers and repeat the correction.** Trustworthy sources help, and debunking effects can fade within weeks, so plan follow-ups.[^s1]

**Pitfalls:** stopping at "false" without an alternative; letting the myth dominate a headline; and, in long debunks, spending effort on detail the audience will not read.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence

The 2017 meta-analysis found that detailed counter-messages produced larger debunking effects than labels or brief explanations, but also that misinformation persisted after correction, and that persistence was lower when audiences generated counterarguments themselves.[^s2] The 2022 review by Ecker and colleagues concluded that corrections generally reduce reliance on misinformation without eliminating it, that effects can wane over weeks, and that belief change does not always carry through to behaviour such as sharing or voting.[^s1]

For years practitioners worried about "backfire effects", where a correction makes the myth stronger by repeating it (familiarity), overloading the reader (overkill) or threatening their worldview. The evidence has not held up well. Swire-Thompson, DeGutis and Lazer's 2020 review found that worldview backfire appeared mostly in subgroups and often failed to replicate, including in one study of 52 issues and over 10,000 participants; that the familiarity backfire rested on weak evidence; and that many apparent backfires came from unreliable single-item measures.[^s3] Both the 2020 handbook consensus and the 2022 review say fear of backfire should not stop people correcting misinformation, though worldview-threatening corrections tend to be less effective.[^s1][^s4] Because corrections work reliably but only partially and fade over time, this node's evidence status is mixed.

## Connections

- **Counteracts** misinformation and disinformation, and specifically the continued influence effect that keeps a retracted claim active in reasoning.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Chan and colleagues' 2017 meta-analysis of debunking experiments.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with** prebunking: debunking corrects after exposure, prebunking inoculates before it, and reviews recommend using both.[^s1]
- **Related to** technique rebuttal, a debunking style centred on explaining the misleading technique, and to belief perseverance, the illusory truth effect and processing fluency, which explain why repeated myths feel true, and to motivated reasoning, which makes worldview-threatening corrections less effective.[^s1][^s3]
