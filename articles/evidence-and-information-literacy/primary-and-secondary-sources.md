# Primary and secondary sources

A primary source gives firsthand evidence about its subject: it was produced by a participant, witness or instrument at or near the time and place being studied. Letters, diaries, speeches, interviews, photographs, datasets and original research reports are typical examples.[^s4][^s3] A secondary source comes later and works from primary material, describing, interpreting or analysing it; biographies, monographs, reviews and most textbooks fall here.[^s4] The distinction matters for critical thinking because every step away from the original evidence is a chance for selection, simplification or error to creep in.

## Origin

The idea is much older than the modern labels. Modern historians usually trace its methodological form to Leopold von Ranke's 1824 essay on the critique of recent historians, which asked of every writer whether they were a firsthand witness, then tested credibility against internal evidence and other accounts, and set aside writers who merely repeated others.[^s1] Ranke spoke of original versus derived accounts and modelled the procedure on the philologist's reconstruction of ancient texts, building on Enlightenment doubts about the reliability of historical narratives.[^s1]

Charles-Victor Langlois and Charles Seignobos codified the approach for students in their 1898 *Introduction to the Study of History*. They described documents as the traces left by past thought and action and insisted there is no substitute for them.[^s2] Their chapters on source criticism show how to work out which documents copy which, and warn that a derivative text is worth only what its sources are worth.[^s2] University library guides now teach the same distinction to students in every discipline.[^s3][^s4]

## How it works

The label is a relationship, not a property of the document. A source is primary or secondary *relative to a research question*. A historian's book about a wartime president is a secondary source on the president but a primary source for a study of that historian's writing style; a 19th-century newspaper is primary for the events it reported but would usually be secondary today.[^s3] Fields also differ: in literature the poem is the primary source and criticism of it is secondary, while in the sciences the primary source is the paper that reports the original experiment.[^s3]

A workplace example: a manager reads in an industry newsletter that "studies show remote teams are 30% less productive." The newsletter is a secondary source. Going upstream means finding the study it relies on, checking who was measured, what "productivity" meant and whether the figure appears there at all.[^s6] Often the original turns out to be narrower or more hedged than the summary.

Two points from classic source criticism guard against misusing the distinction. First, primary does not mean reliable: an eyewitness may err or present events as they wished them to be seen, and a document of certain origin may still contain false statements, so each claim must be weighed on its own.[^s2] Second, several secondary sources that agree do not add up to independent confirmation if they all copy the same original; agreement counts only when the accounts are independent.[^s2]

## How to apply it

1. **Name the question** first, then decide which sources are primary for that question.[^s3]
2. **Go upstream:** when a claim cites research or an event, trace it to the original publication or first report before relying on it.[^s6]
3. **Check independence:** when several sources agree, ask whether they trace back to the same origin.[^s2]
4. **Assess the primary source itself:** who produced it, whether they were in a position to observe, and what motive or error might shape it.[^s1][^s2]
5. **Use secondary sources for context**, synthesis and expert interpretation, which primary material rarely provides by itself.[^s4]

**Pitfalls:** treating "primary" as a badge of truth; assuming a peer-reviewed review is primary evidence; and counting echoes of one report as many confirmations.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

The cost of relying on secondhand citations has been measured. A 2009 *BMJ* analysis of 242 papers and 675 citations about one biomedical claim found that the literature created unfounded authority through citation bias against refuting papers, amplification by papers that had no data of their own, and invention, where a hypothesis was cited into fact.[^s5] Readers who had checked the primary data rather than the chain of citations would have seen a weaker case.[^s5]

The distinction has limits. The boundary is blurred: the same item can be primary for one question and secondary for another, and many chronicles that look original are compilations of earlier works.[^s3][^s2] Going to the original also takes expertise and time, which is why secondary syntheses written by specialists remain indispensable.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Narrower than** source evaluation: deciding how close a source sits to the evidence is one part of judging it.[^s4]
- **Part of** source criticism, the historian's method of tracing filiation between documents and testing witnesses.[^s2]
- **Studied in** historiography, where Ranke's critique of derived accounts set the modern pattern.[^s1]
- **Influenced by** Leopold von Ranke, whose 1824 critical method separated firsthand from borrowed testimony.[^s1]
- **Applied in** information literacy instruction, which teaches students to classify sources relative to their question.[^s3]
- **Related to** the SIFT method, which grew out of Caulfield's four moves, including "go upstream" to the original source.[^s6]
- **Related to** lateral reading and source credibility, which judge who produced a source rather than how close it is to the evidence.[^s6]
