# Evaluating expert testimony

Evaluating expert testimony is the practical skill of deciding how much weight a non-expert should give an expert's claim, and in particular which of two disagreeing experts to believe, without first becoming an expert oneself. Its best-known formulation is Alvin Goldman's list of five kinds of evidence a novice can use: the experts' arguments, agreement from other experts, appraisals by meta-experts such as credentials, evidence of interests and biases, and past track records.[^s1] Where the argument from expert opinion asks whether an appeal to one authority is well made, this technique handles the harder case where authorities conflict and the listener cannot check the substance.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

John Hardwig argued in 1985 that laypeople can be rational in believing a claim because they have good reason to think an expert has good reasons for it, even with no evidence of their own; since no one can check more than a sliver of what they believe, rationality sometimes requires deference.[^s2] That raised an obvious follow-up: deference to whom? Goldman's paper "Experts: Which Ones Should You Trust?" (*Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, 2001) posed the novice/expert problem directly and answered Hardwig's worry that such trust is groundless by setting out the five sources of evidence above.[^s1][^s3] Elizabeth Anderson (2011) later argued that ordinary citizens with a basic education and web access can make reliable second-order judgments about where a trustworthy scientific consensus lies, using the anthropogenic global-warming case to show the criteria in use.[^s4]

## How it works

Goldman separates *esoteric* statements, which sit inside a field and which a novice cannot assess, from *exoteric* ones the novice can check. The technique works by moving the question from the esoteric claim to exoteric evidence about the people making it.[^s1] Suppose a small firm's two consultants disagree about whether its warehouse floor can bear a new racking system. The owner cannot do the load calculations, but can notice that one engineer answers each of the other's objections while the reverse does not happen; that this engineer's view matches two independent assessments; that both are licensed; that the other works for the racking vendor; and that the first has a record of past assessments that held up. Goldman calls inferring relative expertise from the debate itself *indirect* argumentative justification: an inference to the best explanation about who knows more, not a check of the physics.[^s1]

Each source has a known trap. Headcounts only help when the agreeing experts reached their views independently; followers of a guru, or people repeating a rumour, add numbers but not evidence.[^s1] Interests matter because they can skew conclusions: Goldman cites a 1999 *JAMA* study in which 38% of nonprofit-sponsored oncology drug studies reached unfavourable conclusions, against 5% of drug-company-sponsored ones.[^s1] Track records are usable because some once-esoteric questions later become checkable by anyone, as a predicted eclipse is on the day it happens.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. State the disputed claim and identify who the competing experts are and what field it belongs to.[^s1]
2. Compare how each side handles the other's arguments: who rebuts, who is rebutted, who answers questions directly.[^s1]
3. Check for consensus among *independent* experts, not just the number of names on a list.[^s1][^s4]
4. Check credentials and appraisals from bodies qualified to judge the field.[^s1]
5. Look for financial, career or ideological stakes that could bend each side's conclusions.[^s1]
6. Look for a track record on questions that have since been settled.[^s1]

A common pitfall is mistaking a list of credentialed signatories for an expert consensus. Cook, Lewandowsky and Ecker (2017) tested a "fake experts" message, a petition claiming over 31,000 science-degree signatories against human-caused warming. In a sample of 392 U.S. adults, a short prior explanation of the fake-experts tactic neutralised the message's effect on perceived consensus. It was one example with about 100 people per group and no long-term follow-up.[^s5]

## Evidence and limitations

The technique is a normative proposal from social epistemology, not a tested training programme. Neil Levy (2024) argues that each criterion is harder to use than it looks. Rhetorical skill can mimic dialectical superiority. Judging whether scientists are independent takes deep domain knowledge. Appeals to meta-experts push the problem up a level. Incentives distort both sides, track records are themselves disputed, and credentials appear on both sides of lasting disputes.[^s3] He concludes that the criteria can extend trust from sources already trusted but cannot ground it, that novices applying them deeply are trespassing on expert territory, and that improving the information environment may do more than individual vetting.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Alvin Goldman** in *Experts: Which Ones Should You Trust?* (2001), which set out the five sources of evidence.[^s1]
- **Counteracts ad verecundiam.** The criteria test whether a putative authority is relevant, independent, unbiased and reliable, the very conditions whose failure turns an appeal to authority into a fallacy.[^s1]
- **Influenced by John Hardwig**, whose account of epistemic dependence Goldman was answering.[^s2][^s3]
- **Challenged by Levy (2024)**, who argues the novice/expert problem cannot be solved at the level of the individual novice.[^s3]
- **Related to source credibility, conflict of interest and prebunking**, which supply parts of the same toolkit: judging a source, spotting stakes, and recognising the fake-experts tactic in advance.[^s1][^s5]
