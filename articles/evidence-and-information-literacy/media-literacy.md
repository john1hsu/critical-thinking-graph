# Media literacy

Media literacy is the capacity to treat any media message (a news story, an advert, a viral video, a sponsored post, a corporate slide deck) as something constructed by someone for a purpose, and to question it accordingly. The most widely cited definition, from a 1992 US leadership conference, describes it as the ability to access, analyse, evaluate and communicate messages in a wide variety of forms.[^s1][^s2] The National Association for Media Literacy Education (NAMLE) has since extended that list to accessing, analysing, evaluating, creating and acting, using all forms of communication.[^s5] Where information literacy centres on finding and using information to meet a need, media literacy centres on how messages are made, by whom and to what effect, and it treats making media as part of understanding it.[^s2][^s5]

## Origin

Teaching people to read films, newspapers and advertising critically has a long history, but the term's modern definition was set by the National Leadership Conference on Media Literacy, convened by the Aspen Institute at the Wye Center in Maryland in December 1992. About two dozen leaders of the movement attended, and Patricia Aufderheide wrote up the conference report, published in 1993.[^s1] The report framed media literacy as a citizen's ability to access, analyse and produce information for specific outcomes, and set up task forces on curriculum and teacher training.[^s1]

In 1998 Renée Hobbs mapped the field's internal disagreements in the *Journal of Communication*. She identified seven debates: whether media literacy should protect young people from harmful media or build their judgement, whether producing media is essential, whether to use popular culture as classroom material, whether the field should pursue explicit political goals, whether it belongs only in schools, whether it should be a separate subject or woven into others, and whether media companies should fund it.[^s2] Her conclusion was that a pedagogy of inquiry, built on asking critical questions about messages, is the common ground that holds the movement together.[^s2]

## How it works

The core move is to stop asking only "is this true?" and also ask "who made this, why, how, and for whom?" NAMLE's principles describe this as active inquiry into the messages people consume, create and share, combined with healthy scepticism, not cynicism, about media institutions as commercial and cultural forces.[^s5]

Consider an operations manager who sees a polished post in a professional network feed: a bar chart showing that teams using a particular scheduling app finish projects 40% faster, with a link to a "free industry report". A media-literate reading asks who produced the chart (the app vendor's marketing team), what its purpose is (lead generation for a sale), which techniques are used (a truncated axis, a cherry-picked comparison group, a testimonial from a satisfied customer) and who the target audience is (managers under deadline pressure, primed to want a fix). None of this proves the claim false, but it changes the message from a neutral finding into advertising whose evidence must be checked elsewhere, for example by reading laterally about the report's origin.[^s5][^s2] Creating a message, such as drafting the team's own internal summary of the tool, makes those construction choices visible from the inside, which is why the field counts production as part of the skill.[^s2]

## Evidence

Media literacy is studied mainly through intervention research. A 2012 meta-analysis by Se Hoon Jeong, Hyunyi Cho and Yoori Hwang pooled 51 interventions and found an average positive effect of about d = 0.37 across outcomes including media knowledge, critical thinking, perceived realism of media portrayals, attitudes, self-efficacy and behaviour. Programmes with more sessions worked better, programmes packing in more components worked less well, and effects did not differ by age group, setting, topic or country.[^s3]

A 2024 meta-analysis by Guanxiong Huang, Wufan Jia and Wenting Yu focused on misinformation. Across 49 experiments with about 81,000 participants, interventions improved overall resilience to misinformation (d = 0.60), with smaller effects on reducing belief in false content (d = 0.27) and larger effects on telling true from false (d = 0.76) and on reducing sharing (d = 1.04). Effects were stronger with multiple sessions, among college students than among crowdsourced online adults, and in cultures high in uncertainty avoidance.[^s4]

These results support teachability but warrant caution, which is why this node is marked mixed. Effects vary widely with dose and audience, many outcomes are measured soon after the intervention, and the field itself still disputes whether its aim is protection, empowerment or civic action, so programmes labelled "media literacy" are not interchangeable.[^s3][^s4][^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced in** the 1993 Aspen Institute report of the National Leadership Conference on Media Literacy, which supplied the field's standard definition.[^s1]
- **Studied in** communication studies, where Hobbs's account of the movement's debates appeared.[^s2]
- **Applied in** media literacy education, whose core principles NAMLE sets out for teachers.[^s5]
- **Demonstrated by** the 2012 meta-analysis of media literacy interventions and the 2024 meta-analysis of their effect on misinformation resilience.[^s3][^s4]
- **Related to** information literacy, source evaluation, lateral reading and prebunking, which share the goal of judging messages before accepting or sharing them, and to misinformation and disinformation, the problem the 2024 meta-analysis measured.[^s4][^s5]
