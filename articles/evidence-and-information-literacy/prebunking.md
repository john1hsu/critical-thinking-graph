# Prebunking

Prebunking is the pre-emptive cousin of fact-checking. Instead of correcting a false claim after people have absorbed it, you warn them in advance that someone may try to mislead them and show them a harmless, weakened version of the trick, so that they recognise and resist the real thing later.[^s1] The approach is the practical application of inoculation theory, which treats resistance to persuasion like immunity to a virus: brief exposure to a weakened attack, together with its refutation, builds defences against stronger attacks.[^s2]

## Origin

The underlying theory comes from social psychologist William J. McGuire, whose 1961 papers and 1964 chapter set out the vaccine analogy.[^s2] McGuire's early experiments tried to protect "cultural truisms", beliefs almost nobody questions, such as the value of brushing your teeth, which people had never had to defend.[^s2] Two ingredients became the core of the method: **threat**, the recognition that one's view is vulnerable and worth defending, often produced by a forewarning; and **refutational preemption**, raising likely counterarguments and answering them in advance.[^s2] A 2010 meta-analysis by Banas and Rains, as summarised by Compton and colleagues, found that inoculation protects even against attacking arguments that were not the ones refuted, an effect researchers call umbrella protection.[^s2]

In the 2010s, researchers including John Cook, Stephan Lewandowsky, Ullrich Ecker, Sander van der Linden and Jon Roozenbeek shifted the target from specific claims to the reasoning techniques that misinformation reuses. Cook and colleagues showed that explaining the "fake experts" and "false balance" tactics, without rebutting the particular claims to come, neutralised their effect.[^s3] "Prebunking" became the common name for this technique-based inoculation in public campaigns, including short videos run as YouTube adverts.[^s1]

## How it works

A typical prebunk has three parts: a forewarning that someone may try to manipulate you, a pre-emptive explanation of the technique, and a "microdose", a short, fictional and often humorous example of it.[^s1]

Consider an internal-communications team expecting misleading rumours during an office relocation. Rather than chasing each rumour, they send a two-minute video before the move is announced. It warns staff that changes like this attract confident, emotional posts; explains the false-dilemma tactic of presenting only two options when more exist; and shows a deliberately silly example, such as a post claiming the company must either move everyone to the far suburb or shut the department down. When a real post later says "either we accept the new site or we all lose our jobs", employees are more likely to notice the missing options and check the official plan before sharing it. This mirrors the design of the 2022 videos, which used non-political, fictional microdoses so that the lesson was about the technique rather than any contested topic.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. **Anticipate the attack.** Identify the claims or, better, the recurring techniques your audience is likely to meet: emotional language, scapegoating, false dichotomies, ad hominem attacks, fake experts.[^s1][^s3]
2. **Forewarn.** Say plainly that people may try to mislead the audience on this topic; the sense of threat motivates people to defend their view.[^s2]
3. **Explain and refute the technique.** Show why it is misleading and what a fair version of the argument would include.[^s3]
4. **Give a weakened example.** Use a short, neutral or fictional case so the lesson does not depend on, or trigger, partisan identity.[^s1]
5. **Deliver it before exposure and top it up.** Run the prebunk ahead of the expected misinformation and repeat or quiz it later, because effects fade.[^s4][^s5]

**When to use it:** before a predictable wave of misleading claims, such as an election, a product recall, a public-health campaign or an organisational change, and when the same techniques recur across many specific claims.[^s1][^s3]

**Pitfalls:** an intervention that simply makes people say "fake" more often can lower trust in true information too; outcome measures should test both true and false items.[^s6] Effects are modest at scale and short-lived without reinforcement.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence

Six preregistered experiments in 2022 with 6,464 US participants found that 1.5-minute technique videos improved recognition of manipulation, confidence and the ability to tell trustworthy from untrustworthy posts, meeting 16 of 20 preregistered outcomes.[^s1] In the accompanying YouTube field study, roughly 967,000 people saw a video as an advert; among about 22,600 who answered a test question, the treated group identified the technique about 5% more often, a small effect that narrowly missed its preregistered threshold.[^s1] Cook and colleagues' 2017 experiments (N = 714 and 392) found technique-based inoculation cancelled the effect of climate misinformation and reduced its polarising effect across political views.[^s3]

Durability is limited. A 2021 set of three longitudinal experiments found a gamified inoculation stayed effective for about three months when people were regularly tested, but faded within about two months without testing.[^s4] A 2025 paper reporting five longitudinal experiments with 11,759 participants found text and video inoculations lasted about a month, games decayed faster, and memory-focused boosters extended the protection.[^s5]

The main critique concerns what prebunking actually improves. A 2023 reanalysis of 13 experiments on the inoculation games *Bad News* and *Go Viral!*, using receiver operating characteristic analysis, concluded that the games mostly made people more sceptical of all news rather than better at telling true from false.[^s6] That critique targets the game format and the way earlier studies scored outcomes; the video studies reported improved discernment.[^s1][^s6] Because effects are real but small, decaying and partly disputed, this node's evidence status is mixed.

## Connections

- **Explained by** inoculation theory, the attitude-resistance model of threat plus refutational preemption.[^s2]
- **Influenced by** William J. McGuire, who founded that theory in the early 1960s.[^s2]
- **Counteracts** misinformation and disinformation, and in particular the ad hominem and false-dilemma techniques taught in the 2022 videos.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** the 2022 YouTube inoculation study and Cook and colleagues' 2017 fake-experts experiments.[^s1][^s3]
- **Challenged by** the 2023 signal-detection reanalysis arguing that game-based inoculation shifts response bias rather than improving discernment.[^s6]
- **Related to** debunking, its after-the-fact counterpart, and to lateral reading and source credibility, which address who is behind a claim rather than how it argues.[^s1]
