# Predatory publishing

Predatory publishing is the business of running journals (and often conferences) that look like legitimate scholarly outlets but put the publisher's income ahead of scholarship. A 2019 consensus statement defined predatory journals and publishers by four features: false or misleading information, departure from best editorial and publication practice, a lack of transparency, and aggressive, indiscriminate solicitation of authors.[^s1] For a critical thinker the phenomenon matters because it breaks the usual shortcut of trusting a paper because it appears in "a journal": the outward signs of peer review can be bought without the review itself.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The label was coined by librarian Jeffrey Beall, who described such outlets as publishing counterfeit journals to exploit the author-pays open-access model, and whose list of suspect publishers became the main reference point for early research.[^s2] Because the term grew from one person's judgments, scholars long disagreed about what it covered. A 2018 scoping review led by Kelly Cobey found 334 articles discussing predatory journals, but nearly four in five were commentaries or editorials and only 38 reported empirical data; across those 38 the authors extracted 109 distinct proposed characteristics and concluded that no agreed definition existed.[^s2] That gap prompted a summit in Ottawa in April 2019, where 43 participants from 10 countries, including publishers, funders, librarians and researchers, reached the consensus definition above through several rounds of discussion and surveys.[^s1] The group deliberately left journal quality, intent to deceive and peer-review quality out of the definition, because those are hard to audit from outside.[^s1]

## How it works

A typical predatory outlet sends flattering, unsolicited emails inviting a submission, promises fast acceptance, lists an impressive editorial board and claims indexing or impact factors it does not have, then charges a publication fee that was not clearly disclosed up front.[^s1][^s2] In the U.S. case against OMICS Group, a federal court found that the publisher had falsely claimed rigorous peer review and boards of prominent academics while hiding steep fees, and in 2019 it entered a judgment of more than $50.1 million.[^s4]

Workplace example: a marketing manager is handed a supplier's brochure that cites a "peer-reviewed study" showing its software doubles productivity. A quick check shows the journal is unknown to colleagues, names no clear peer-review process, and lists a publisher with no verifiable address. The appearance of publication adds nothing; the claim should be weighed as if it came straight from the vendor's own marketing.[^s1][^s5]

## How to spot it

The Think.Check.Submit campaign, promoted by university libraries, reduces screening to a few questions: do you or your colleagues know the journal, does it clearly describe its type of peer review, can you easily identify and contact the publisher, and does the publisher belong to a recognised industry initiative?[^s5] The empirical literature adds warning signs that recur across studies: low transparency about operations, aggressive email solicitation, missing or unverifiable contact details, false claims about open access, and fees that are hidden or unusually low.[^s2] No single sign is decisive, since many also appear at small legitimate journals, so check several and look the publisher up independently rather than trusting its own website.[^s2]

## Evidence

The clearest experimental evidence comes from a 2017 sting reported in *Nature*. Sorokowski and colleagues created a fictitious, unqualified scientist and applied on her behalf to be an editor at 360 journals, 120 each drawn from Beall's list, the Directory of Open Access Journals and the Journal Citation Reports. Forty of the Beall's-list journals (33%) appointed her, against 8 (7%) of the open-access directory journals and none of the impact-factor journals; some acceptances came with offers to share revenue.[^s3] The result supports the view that these outlets do not vet the people they present as gatekeepers.[^s3]

Scale estimates depend heavily on the definition used. The 2019 consensus authors cite an Italian study in which roughly 5% of 46,000 researchers had published in predatory outlets, note that public research funds pay many of the fees, and warn that prevalence figures can shift several-fold with the criteria chosen.[^s1] The 2018 scoping review found the empirical base thin and dominated by opinion pieces, and found that most of the more than 90 published checklists for spotting such journals were not evidence based.[^s1][^s2] The phenomenon itself is well documented; its exact size and boundaries remain debated.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Scholarly communication** (broader): predatory publishing is a failure mode of the system through which research is reviewed and published.[^s1]
- **Introduced by Jeffrey Beall**, who coined the term and compiled the first widely used list of suspect publishers.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by the Sorokowski et al. 2017 fake-editor sting**, which showed far higher acceptance of an unqualified editor at listed predatory journals than at indexed ones.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with peer review**: predatory outlets advertise peer review and editorial boards they do not actually provide, as the FTC found in the OMICS case.[^s4]
- **Related to the Think.Check.Submit checklist**, a short tool for vetting a journal before trusting or submitting to it.[^s5]
- **Related** to source evaluation, source credibility and lateral reading, because spotting a predatory outlet means checking the publisher from outside its own claims; to the hierarchy of evidence, since a predatory "publication" should not inherit the weight of a reviewed one; and to the appeal to authority, because the trappings of a journal borrow credibility the content has not earned.[^s1][^s5]
