# Suppressed evidence

Suppressed evidence, better known as cherry picking, is the fallacy of building a case from the evidence that helps it while leaving out information the arguer knows or suspects is relevant and significant, usually because that information would weaken the conclusion.[^s1] Every stated premise can be true and relevant, yet the argument still misleads, because its strength depends on what was left out.[^s2] Dowden's Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry reserves "suppressed evidence" for deliberate omission and lists "incomplete evidence" and "cherry-picking the evidence" as names for the same error when the omission is inadvertent.[^s1]

## Origin

The fallacy has no single inventor; it is a standard entry in modern catalogues of informal fallacies and critical-reasoning textbooks.[^s1][^s2] Southworth and Swoyer present it as a generic, catch-all label: when no more specific fallacy fits, the general duty still applies to weigh the relevant evidence one knows about, or that is likely to bear on the issue.[^s2] Dowden groups it with slanting, the wider practice of misrepresenting the evidence on an issue by suppressing part of it, misconstruing it or lying.[^s1] In research, the same error has been measured directly as selective outcome reporting, in which trial reports present the results that reached statistical significance and drop the rest.[^s3][^s5]

## How it works

The argument map above shows the pattern in a purchasing decision. A colleague argues for switching suppliers on the strength of early deliveries and a lower price, both true, but does not mention that two of five recent shipments failed inspection.[^s1] The stated premises make the case look strong; the missing premise would change the decision. Dowden's own example is similar: praise for a computer purchase looks convincing until one learns the seller was a relative who charged well above market price and that an independent review ranked the machine near the bottom.[^s1]

Southworth and Swoyer note why the move is so common: leaving out unfavourable evidence makes one's own side look strong and the other side look weak, which is what advocates are paid or motivated to do.[^s2] Lawyers, advertisers and campaigners routinely present one side, and partisans in policy debates cite only the statistics that favour them.[^s2] Winning an argument is not the same as reasoning well, so people who must decide for themselves, such as jurors or voters, need to weigh the evidence on all sides.[^s2]

## How to spot it

Ask what an informed critic would add. Signs include statistics with no comparison group or time range, a handful of vivid successes with no count of failures, and sources that all come from one side of a dispute.[^s1][^s2] Check how the evidence was selected: Dowden notes that when a generalization rests on a biased sample, and the relevant statistical evidence was hidden or overlooked, the error can be classed as suppressed evidence rather than simple hasty generalization.[^s1] Southworth and Swoyer advise against relying on a view's critics to state it fairly, especially on emotionally charged topics.[^s2]

Not every omission is fallacious. Leaving out details that do not bear on the conclusion is not the fallacy, and no one can review every piece of evidence; the standard is not to ignore evidence one knows about or that is likely relevant.[^s1][^s2] Southworth and Swoyer also caution against reaching for this label whenever information is missing: if a more specific fallacy fits, name that one, and in either case explain why the argument is weak.[^s2]

## How to counter it

Ask for the full record: all the cases, the denominators and the time span, not just the examples offered.[^s1] Look up what the other side says from its own sources rather than from its critics.[^s2] In research settings, compare published results with the study's protocol or registration. Chan and colleagues did exactly this for 102 trials and found outcomes that had been planned but never reported.[^s3] When you are the one arguing, apply the same duty to yourself: consider the relevant evidence you know about, including what cuts against your conclusion.[^s2]

## Evidence

The fallacy itself is a norm of fair argument, so its status is normative.[^s1] Its research counterpart is well documented. Chan and colleagues (2004) compared 102 trial protocols with the 122 articles that reported them: on average, half of efficacy outcomes and nearly two-thirds of harm outcomes per trial were incompletely reported. Statistically significant efficacy outcomes had 2.4 times the odds of being fully reported, 62 percent of trials changed, added or dropped a primary outcome, and 86 percent of responding authors at first denied that unreported outcomes existed.[^s3] A 2013 update of a systematic review by Dwan and colleagues, covering 20 cohort studies, found that 40 to 62 percent of studies had at least one primary outcome changed, introduced or omitted, and that significant outcomes had higher odds of being fully reported (odds ratios 2.2 to 4.7).[^s4] These figures come from clinical trials and may not carry over to other fields or to everyday argument.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Instance of slanting.** Dowden treats slanting as unfair handling of the evidence as a whole, with suppression as one of its means.[^s1]
- **Confused with hasty generalization.** A biased sample can be a hasty generalization or, when relevant data were hidden or overlooked, suppressed evidence.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with the Texas sharpshooter fallacy.** The sharpshooter selectively treats coincidences as meaningful after the fact, as when one hit among many predictions is counted and the misses ignored; suppressed evidence omits known counter-evidence from an argument.[^s1]
- **Related to confirmation bias.** Dowden describes confirmation bias as seeking support for a favoured hypothesis while ignoring or underweighting contrary evidence; inadvertent cherry picking is its argumentative output.[^s1]
- **Related to the straw man.** Both misrepresent the state of a debate, which is why critics of a view are poor guides to it.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by Chan et al. (2004).** Comparing protocols with publications revealed systematic omission of non-significant outcomes.[^s3][^s5]
- **Supported by Dwan et al. (2013).** A systematic review confirms that outcome reporting bias recurs across cohorts of trials.[^s4]
