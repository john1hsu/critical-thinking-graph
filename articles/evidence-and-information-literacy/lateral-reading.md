# Lateral reading

Lateral reading is a way of judging an unfamiliar website by leaving it. Instead of studying the page's design, citations, domain or "About" section, the reader opens new tabs and searches what other, independent sources say about the organisation or author behind it, then returns to the content with that context.[^s1] Its opposite, vertical reading, means staying on the page and judging it by what it shows about itself.[^s1] The phrase captures a counter-intuitive idea: on the open web, you often learn more about a source by reading less of it.[^s2]

## Origin

The term comes from Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew of the Stanford History Education Group. In a 2017 working paper, later published in *Teachers College Record*, they watched 45 people evaluate live websites while thinking aloud: 10 PhD historians, 10 professional fact checkers and 25 Stanford first-year students.[^s1][^s2] In one task, participants compared a long-established paediatric professional body with a small, similarly named splinter group with an ideological agenda. The fact checkers left the splinter group's landing page after about half a minute on average and all of them correctly identified both organisations, while historians left after about a minute and a half and most students rated the splinter group as the more reliable of the two.[^s1] Two of the ten historians who did read laterally performed like the fact checkers, which suggests the difference was strategy rather than domain knowledge.[^s1]

Mike Caulfield's 2017 open textbook *Web Literacy for Student Fact-Checkers* turned the observation into a teachable routine. "Read laterally" became one of four moves, alongside checking for previous fact-checking work, going upstream to the original source, and circling back when a search goes nowhere; Caulfield credits the phrase to Wineburg and later recast the moves as the SIFT acronym.[^s5]

## How it works

Suppose a procurement analyst is asked to summarise research on a new workplace-safety product, and a polished site at a .org address offers a "policy institute" report showing dramatic benefits. Read vertically, everything looks credible: a professional logo, footnotes, a mission statement about worker welfare. Read laterally, the analyst opens a new tab, searches the institute's name together with words such as "funding" or "who runs", and within a minute finds reporting that the institute was set up by a public relations firm working for the product's manufacturer. The report is not automatically false, but it is now advocacy to be weighed against independent evidence, not neutral research.[^s1]

This mirrors Wineburg and McGrew's minimum-wage task, in which the fact checkers traced a slick advocacy site to a public relations firm in under a minute on average, while historians took several minutes and four in ten never found the sponsor.[^s1] The fact checkers also practised what the authors called click restraint: scanning the list of search results, URLs and snippets before choosing what to open, rather than clicking the first hit.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. **Pause before reading closely.** When a source is unfamiliar and the stakes matter, do not start by weighing the page's arguments or appearance.[^s1]
2. **Open new tabs and search about the source,** not just the claim: the organisation's name, its funders, the author's background, plus terms like "funding" or "criticism".[^s1][^s5]
3. **Exercise click restraint.** Read the results page as a whole and favour independent descriptions of the source over the source's own pages.[^s1]
4. **Go upstream** if the page is reporting someone else's work, and check the original.[^s5]
5. **Return and judge** the content in light of what you learned about who produced it and why.[^s3]

**When to use it:** any time you meet an unfamiliar website, report or social-media post that you might rely on or share, especially on contested topics or where money is involved.[^s1][^s3]

**Pitfalls:** domain endings, logos, references and "About" pages are easy to fake, so checklists built only on a page's own features can create false security.[^s1][^s3] Lateral reading also depends on the quality of what search turns up, and the original study observed only a few tasks with small groups in a lab setting.[^s1]

## Evidence

Several intervention studies by the same research group report that lateral reading can be taught. In a 2021 pretest–posttest study, 87 undergraduates in an asynchronous online nutrition course completed four roughly hour-long modules; mean scores rose from about 4 to about 7 out of 13, and the number of students who read laterally on a climate-website task rose from 3 to 67.[^s3] A district-wide field study published in 2022 compared 271 high-school students in classrooms that received six lessons with 228 students in regular government classes and found significantly greater growth in judging the credibility of digital content in the taught classrooms.[^s4][^s6]

The limits are acknowledged by the authors themselves: the durability of the gains, whether students use the strategy unprompted in daily life, and how motivated reasoning on controversial topics affects it were not established, and much of the evidence comes from one research group.[^s3] For that reason this node's evidence status is mixed rather than robust.

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew, from their comparison of fact checkers, historians and students.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with** vertical reading, the habit of judging a site by its own features.[^s1]
- **Part of** the SIFT method, Caulfield's four-move routine for checking online information.[^s5]
- **Counteracts** misinformation and disinformation by exposing who is behind a source before its content is accepted.[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** the 2021 online-course study of college students and the 2022 high-school field study.[^s3][^s4]
- **Related to** click restraint and to source credibility, the property lateral reading is designed to assess.[^s1]
