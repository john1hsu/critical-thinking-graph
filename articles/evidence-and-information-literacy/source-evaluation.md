# Source evaluation

Source evaluation is the umbrella practice of deciding how far to rely on where a claim came from: a web page, a report, a dataset, a social-media post or a person offering expert testimony. The Association of College and Research Libraries frames it as context-dependent: a source's authority reflects its creator's expertise and credibility, but which kind of authority you need depends on what you will use the information for.[^s3] Evaluating the source is distinct from evaluating the argument itself, yet for most people most of the time it is the only practical check, because they cannot verify the underlying evidence on their own.[^s6]

## Origin

The problem is old in epistemology: when a non-expert must choose between experts who disagree, what can they go on? Alvin Goldman's 2001 paper set out five kinds of evidence a novice can use: the arguments each expert offers, agreement from other experts, assessments by meta-experts such as credentials, signs of interests or biases, and past track record.[^s6]

In education, source evaluation became a named skill with library instruction. A widely used checklist, the CRAAP test (currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, purpose), was published by librarian Sarah Blakeslee in *LOEX Quarterly* in 2004 and is still adapted in university library guides.[^s1][^s2] A 2007 review by communication scholar Miriam Metzger surveyed such checklist recommendations alongside studies of how internet users actually judge credibility, and called for better models and credibility education.[^s7] The ACRL's Framework for Information Literacy, filed in 2015 and adopted in 2016, includes the frame "authority is constructed and contextual", which treats evaluation as context-sensitive judgement; the current version, with revisions approved in 2026, also addresses search algorithms and generative AI.[^s3]

## How it works

Suppose a product manager is asked to decide whether to adopt a new project-management tool, and a colleague forwards a polished article reporting that teams using it ship 40% faster. Source evaluation asks several questions before the figure enters the decision. Who wrote and paid for this, and do they gain if you believe it (Goldman's interests and biases)?[^s6] What expertise or track record do they have?[^s6] Can the 40% be traced to a study and corroborated elsewhere, and is the information current and relevant to a team like yours (the accuracy, currency and relevance questions of library checklists)?[^s2] If the article turns out to be sponsored content from the vendor, its figure may still be true, but it should not carry weight until an independent source supports it.

That example is not hypothetical in form. In assessments run by the Stanford History Education Group, more than 80% of 203 middle-school students took a native advertisement labelled "sponsored content" for a news story, and college students were swayed by high production values and polished "About" pages.[^s5]

## How to apply it

1. **Fix the purpose first.** Decide what the claim is for: a casual conversation, a business decision and a published report need different levels of authority.[^s3]
2. **Identify the source, not just the content.** Find who created and sponsored it; in the Stanford assessments students often judged content while ignoring authorship and sponsorship.[^s5]
3. **Check expertise and interests.** Ask what qualifies the source on this question and whether it has a stake in your belief; these are two of Goldman's five lines of evidence.[^s6]
4. **Corroborate.** Look for independent confirmation and for whether other experts agree; agreement counts for more when the agreeing sources are not simply copying each other.[^s6]
5. **Check fit and currency.** Confirm the material is recent enough and actually addresses your question.[^s2]
6. **Watch your own pull.** The ACRL framework asks learners to read skeptically while noticing how their own biases, including confirmation bias, shape what they treat as authoritative.[^s3]

**Pitfalls.** Checklists that inspect only the page itself reward surface cues: domain endings, a professional design or a confident "About" page are easy to fake, and these were exactly the cues that misled students in the Stanford work.[^s5] For unfamiliar websites, the related techniques of lateral reading and SIFT address this by leaving the page to see what others say about its source.

## Evidence

Evidence that people need the skill is strong; evidence on how best to teach it is still developing. McGrew and colleagues (2018) gave short civic-online-reasoning tasks, from paper exercises to live web searches, to middle-school, high-school and college students in 12 US states and found that students at every level struggled to evaluate online claims, sources and evidence.[^s4] The wider 2016 Stanford project behind that paper gathered 7,804 student responses from 12 states.[^s5] Metzger's 2007 review set credibility-assessment recommendations against empirical studies of how users actually judge online information, critiqued the existing models, and recommended changes to credibility education.[^s7] Because the deficit findings are robust but intervention results depend on the method taught, the evidence status here is mixed.

## Connections

- **Broader:** source evaluation is a core component of information literacy, which the ACRL framework organises around it, and an application of critical thinking to testimony.[^s3][^s6]
- **Applied in** information-literacy instruction, where the ACRL framework sets it out as knowledge practices and dispositions.[^s3]
- **Studied in** epistemology, as the problem of how novices can rationally rely on experts.[^s6]
- **Demonstrated by** the McGrew et al. (2018) civic-online-reasoning assessments, which showed how poorly untrained students evaluate online sources.[^s4]
- **Counteracts** misinformation and disinformation: the Stanford researchers presented these skills as a defence against sponsored content, fake accounts and misleading sites.[^s5]
- Related to source credibility, conflict of interest, the hierarchy of evidence and the argument from expert opinion, which supply specific questions within the broader practice.[^s6]
