# Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework

*Information Disorder: Toward an interdisciplinary framework for research and policymaking* is a 2017 report written by Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan for the Council of Europe, with support from Harvard's Shorenstein Center and the verification nonprofit First Draft.[^s1] Its main contribution is vocabulary. Instead of "fake news", it offers a framework for describing polluted information by asking whether content is false, whether it is meant to harm, who is behind it, and how it changes as it travels.[^s1] For critical thinkers the report matters because it turns a loaded slogan into questions that can be checked one by one.

## Origin

The report appeared in October 2017, amid concern about fabricated stories and organised influence campaigns online. At the time, researchers and policymakers used "fake news" to mean anything from satire to state propaganda.[^s1] Wardle and Derakhshan judged the term inadequate for the problem. They also noted that politicians had started using it against news outlets whose coverage they disliked, so the label itself had become a weapon. They proposed "information disorder" and "information pollution" as umbrella terms instead.[^s1] The report presents its opinions as the authors' own rather than official Council of Europe policy. It closes with 34 recommendations for technology companies, governments, media organisations, civil society, education ministries and funders, including a standardised news-literacy curriculum.[^s1]

## How it works

The framework has three parts.[^s1]

- **Three types**, sorted by falseness and intent to harm. *Misinformation* is false content shared without intent to harm. *Disinformation* is false content knowingly created or shared to cause harm. *Malinformation* is genuine content used to harm, often by moving private information into public view.[^s1]
- **Three elements**, examined separately: the *agent* who creates or spreads the content and their motives, the *message* with its format and accuracy, and the *interpreter* who makes sense of it and may act on it.[^s1]
- **Three phases** in a message's life: *creation*, *production* and *distribution*. Different agents with different motives can handle each phase.[^s1]

A workplace example shows why the phases matter. Someone invents a memo claiming that the company will close a regional office and posts it to stir up anger. At the source, this is disinformation. Worried staff who forward it in good faith are spreading misinformation. If a real but confidential reorganisation plan were leaked to embarrass a manager, that would be malinformation, even though every word was true.[^s1] The report adds that interpreters become agents when they reshare, and that trust between peers makes this spread fast.[^s1] Drawing on James Carey's "ritual" view of communication, the authors argue that people often share news to signal belonging rather than to inform. On this view, fact-checking alone cannot fix the problem.[^s1]

## Influence

The definitions spread quickly. A Social Science Research Council review reports that the intent-based definition gained ground among scholars, activists and nonprofits.[^s2] Hayward notes that bodies including the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), the European Commission and UNESCO adopted the vocabulary.[^s3] Wardle and Derakhshan also wrote the module on information disorder for UNESCO's handbook for journalism education and training.[^s4]

## Limitations

The framework's central test, intent, is hard to apply. The SSRC review notes that motives are difficult to measure. Irony and trolling let creators both claim and deny intent. Journalists face professional and legal limits on accusing people of bad intent, while disinformers face no such limits. The same narrative can also move between categories as it is reshared.[^s2] In a 2024 *Social Epistemology* paper, Tim Hayward argues that falsity is the wrong criterion, because selectively presented true facts can still produce false beliefs. He also argues that "malinformation" slides between meaning harmful content and content shared with malign intent. Adding intent variables to the scheme, he counts at least ten kinds of information disorder.[^s3] In practice, then, the three labels work best as prompts for questions, not as verdicts.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduces information disorder** as the umbrella term for polluted information, offered in place of "fake news".[^s1]
- **Introduces the mis/dis/malinformation typology** that the graph's misinformation and disinformation article relies on. The words themselves are older, so this attribution has medium confidence.[^s1]
- **Authored by Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan.**[^s1]
- **Influenced by James W. Carey**, whose ritual view of communication the report adapts to explain echo chambers.[^s1]
- **Applied in journalism education** through the UNESCO handbook module written by the same authors.[^s4]
- **Challenged by Hayward (2024)**, who disputes both the falsity criterion and the coherence of "malinformation".[^s3]
