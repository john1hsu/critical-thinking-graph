# Statistical literacy

Statistical literacy is the capacity of ordinary adults to make sense of the numbers aimed at them. In Iddo Gal's widely used account it has two linked parts: interpreting and critically evaluating statistical information and data-based arguments met in everyday settings, and being able to discuss or communicate one's reactions to them, such as what a figure means, what follows from it and where the conclusion is doubtful.[^s1] Gal stresses that most adults meet statistics in *reading contexts* (news, advertising, medical leaflets, workplace reports) as data consumers who did not produce the numbers, which differs from the *enquiry contexts* of students and analysts who collect and analyse data themselves.[^s1]

## Origin

The phrase is older than the modern research field. A 1943 statement of goals for school statistics already named statistical literacy as an aim, and Helen Walker's 1951 paper in *The American Statistician* was the first to put it in a title, likening it to verbal literacy.[^s3] It became a rallying term after Katherine Wallman's 1993 presidential address to the American Statistical Association, which tied public mistrust of statistics to an inability to understand and critically assess the statistical results found in daily life.[^s3][^s1] In the 1990s and early 2000s Jane Watson, Milo Schield and Gal developed it within statistics education; Schield emphasised statistics as evidence in arguments, especially questions of confounding and causation.[^s3] Gal's 2002 model in the *International Statistical Review* became the standard reference.[^s1]

## How it works

Gal's model joins five knowledge elements (general literacy for reading prose, tables and graphs; statistical knowledge; practical mathematics such as percentages and averages; knowledge of the real-world context; and critical skills) with two dispositions: a habitual critical stance toward quantitative claims, and the belief that one is able, and entitled, to question them.[^s1] He turns the critical element into a list of "worry questions" a reader can ask: where the data came from and what kind of study produced them, how the sample was drawn, how reliable the measures are, whether averages and graphs fairly represent the data, what a probability claim is based on, what information is missing, and whether other explanations fit.[^s1]

A workplace example shows the elements at work. A vendor's slide claims its training course "halves staff errors". A statistically literate manager asks: halves from what baseline, so that 4 errors per 1,000 tasks falling to 2 is a small absolute change; which teams were measured, and did the vendor pick its best clients; was there a comparison group; and did anything else change in the same quarter. None of this needs calculation beyond percentages, but each question targets a known way numbers mislead.[^s1][^s2]

## How to apply it

Gigerenzer and colleagues document how often the absence of these skills, which they call collective statistical illiteracy, affects patients, journalists and physicians alike.[^s2] Their health examples translate into general habits:

- **Ask for absolute risks, not only relative ones.** A 1995 UK warning that some contraceptive pills doubled clot risk described a rise from about 1 to 2 in 7,000 women; the authors estimate the resulting scare was followed by roughly 13,000 extra abortions in England and Wales.[^s2]
- **Prefer mortality to survival rates when judging screening.** Five-year survival can rise because of earlier diagnosis and overdiagnosis even when deaths per head do not change.[^s2]
- **Restate conditional probabilities as natural frequencies.** In a training study reported in the review, only 21% of 160 gynaecologists picked the best answer for the chance of cancer after a positive mammogram; after learning to use natural frequencies, 87% did.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

The construct is useful but loosely bounded. A 2023 review by the UK Office for Statistics Regulation found no agreed definition: education-focused versions stress statistical knowledge, while adult-focused versions add critical evaluation, decision use and an understanding of where official statistics come from, and the evidence base for measurement tools is limited.[^s4] Evidence that people misread risk statistics, and that clearer formats help, is strong in health settings, but much of it comes from specific formats and samples rather than from tests of general statistical literacy.[^s2] The OSR also cautions that education-only approaches may put too much responsibility on data consumers, since not everyone can invest in complex skills, and points to evidence on how producers can tailor communication to audiences of differing literacy.[^s5] Gigerenzer's group makes the same point, tracing poor understanding partly to non-transparent framing by those who present the figures, not only to the audience.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Formalized by** Iddo Gal, whose 2002 knowledge-plus-dispositions model and worry questions gave the concept its standard structure.[^s1]
- **Studied in** statistics education, where Walker, Wallman, Watson, Schield and Gal developed it as a goal of schooling and adult learning.[^s3]
- **Applied in** risk communication, where transparent formats such as absolute risks and natural frequencies are proposed to make health statistics understandable.[^s2]
- **Applied in** official statistics, where regulators treat public statistical literacy as a condition for statistics serving the public good.[^s4]
- **Requires** numeracy, which corresponds to the practical mathematical element in Gal's model; statistical literacy is broader because it adds context knowledge, critical questioning and dispositions.[^s1]
- **Related to** information literacy and media literacy as a sibling competence for evaluating sources, and to base-rate neglect and the framing effect, the errors its questions help to catch.[^s2]
