# Conflict of interest

A conflict of interest exists when circumstances create a substantial risk that someone's professional judgment about a primary interest will be unduly swayed by a secondary one. The primary interest is whatever the role is for, such as a patient's welfare or the quality of a study; the secondary interest is usually financial gain, which rules focus on because it is more objective and easier to measure than ambitions or loyalties.[^s1] For a critical thinker the concept matters because so much of what we believe rests on the judgment of advisers, researchers and reviewers whose interests we cannot see directly.

## Origin

The working definition used in medicine and research ethics comes from the political philosopher Dennis F. Thompson of Harvard, who set it out in a 1993 *New England Journal of Medicine* article and restated it in 2009.[^s1] Thompson stresses that a conflict is a condition, not a conduct: it exists whether or not anyone was actually influenced, and if bias does occur, the problem becomes a separate and more serious one, such as misconduct.[^s1] He criticises the phrase "even the appearance of a conflict" because it implies that appearances are a lesser matter and invites vague, subjective accusations; charges should rest on circumstances that can be objectively described.[^s1]

## How it works

Rules on conflicts are preventive rather than accusatory. Thompson gives two reasons for judging the situation instead of each person's motives: investigating how much a payment shaped a given decision would be intrusive and impractical, and neither patients nor the public, nor often the decision makers themselves, can reliably tell what moved a judgment.[^s1] The severity of a conflict depends on how likely undue influence is (larger and longer-lasting financial ties, closer relationships and wider discretion raise the risk; team oversight and external audit lower it) and on how serious the harm would be if it occurred.[^s1]

Experiments show why this caution is warranted. In Cain, Loewenstein and Moore's coin-jar study, 147 Carnegie Mellon students acted as advisers or estimators valuing jars of coins. Advisers paid for high estimates suggested about $20.16 on average, against $16.48 from advisers paid for accuracy; when the conflict was disclosed, suggestions rose further to about $24.16.[^s3] Estimators discounted disclosed advice somewhat, but not enough, and ended up less accurate than when nothing was disclosed.[^s3]

A workplace example: a consultant recommends a software platform to a client and is paid a referral fee by its vendor. The fee does not prove the advice is wrong, but it creates exactly the risk the concept describes, so the client should weigh the recommendation as less independent and look for confirmation from someone without that tie.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Ask three questions of any source: what is its role meant to serve, what else does it stand to gain from your conclusion, and how large, durable and unsupervised that gain is.[^s1] Treat disclosure as information, not a cure. Thompson calls it necessary but usually insufficient, to be combined with management (divestment, blind trusts, handing key tasks to an unconflicted colleague, independent oversight) or, in severe cases, prohibition.[^s1] Cain and colleagues found that advisees under-discount disclosed advice partly because they anchor on the number they were given, and that a contrasting opinion from an unconflicted adviser made them markedly less willing to follow conflicted advice.[^s4] The practical remedy is therefore a second, independent opinion rather than a mental adjustment. Finally, keep the concept apart from a personal attack: a conflict is a reason to check the evidence, not proof that the claim is false.

## Evidence and limitations

The best-established finding concerns research funding. A 2017 Cochrane methodology review by Lundh and colleagues found that industry-sponsored drug and device studies more often reported favourable efficacy results (risk ratio 1.27 across 25 papers) and favourable conclusions (risk ratio 1.34 across 29 papers) than non-industry studies.[^s2] The bias was not explained by standard risk-of-bias ratings: sponsored studies did not consistently score worse on items such as randomisation and allocation concealment, and the excess of favourable results persisted among low risk-of-bias papers.[^s2]

Evidence on disclosure is more mixed. Cain, Loewenstein and Moore's 2011 studies found that disclosure led some advisers to exaggerate more and others to restrain themselves, and that disclosure made inflated advice seem more ethical to advisers, a form of moral licensing.[^s4] Sah's 2019 direct replication of their house-price study (290 participants) reproduced the finding that disclosure increased advisers' bias, but found little evidence of harm to advisees' estimates, although a pooled analysis of original and replication still showed advisees earning less under disclosure.[^s5] Sah also notes that in medical settings disclosure has sometimes reduced bias, so the direction of the effect appears to depend on context.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Dennis F. Thompson.** His primary-versus-secondary-interest definition is the one most institutional policies adopt.[^s1]
- **Supported by the Lundh 2017 Cochrane review.** Pooled evidence that funding source predicts favourable results and conclusions is the strongest empirical case that conflicts matter.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by the Cain, Loewenstein and Moore coin-jar experiment.** Conflicted advisers inflated their advice, and disclosure made it worse.[^s3]
- **Supported by Sah's 2019 replication.** The adviser side of the disclosure effect replicated with a similar effect size.[^s5]
- **Applied in evidence-based medicine.** Sponsorship is treated as a distinct source of bias when appraising trials and reviews.[^s2]
- **Studied in judgment and decision making.** Behavioural research on advice-taking, anchoring and moral licensing explains why disclosure alone fails.[^s4]
