# Information literacy

Information literacy is the capacity to recognise when you need information and then to locate, evaluate and use it well. The American Library Association's 1989 committee report described an information-literate person as someone who knows when information is needed and can find, assess and use it effectively, and who has "learned how to learn".[^s1] A 2018 definition from the UK library body CILIP widens this to finding, evaluating, using and creating information in every form, from print to data, images and speech, and puts critical thinking and balanced judgement at its centre, along with awareness of the ethical and political issues around information.[^s3] In a critical-thinking graph it is the umbrella under which practical skills such as source evaluation, lateral reading and SIFT sit.

## Origin

Paul Zurkowski, then president of the US Information Industry Association, is generally credited with coining the term in a 1974 report to the National Commission on Libraries and Information Science. He framed being information literate as knowing how to find out what is known or knowable on a subject, urged that universal information literacy be achieved within a decade, and tied it to economic competitiveness; his report said little about teaching or about libraries as teachers.[^s2]

Librarians took the idea up over the next fifteen years. The ALA's Presidential Committee on Information Literacy issued its final report on 10 January 1989, arguing that schools and colleges should restructure learning around real information resources, that doing so would strengthen students' critical thinking, and that teacher education and state assessments should build these skills in.[^s1] Later definitions, such as CILIP's 2018 statement, apply the idea across five contexts: everyday life, citizenship, education, the workplace and health.[^s3]

## How it works

Information literacy is less a single technique than a cycle of linked judgements. Take an operations analyst asked whether the company should switch to a four-day week. An information-literate approach starts by recognising what is actually unknown (effects on output? on retention? in which sectors?), then searching beyond the first headline, judging each source's authority and purpose, distinguishing a pilot study's findings from a vendor's marketing, and finally using and citing the evidence honestly in a recommendation. CILIP's examples are similarly mundane: checking product reviews, comparing insurance offers, spotting online scams and managing a digital footprint.[^s3] The ALA report framed the same cycle as a problem-solving approach inside each discipline rather than a separate library skill.[^s1]

The concept overlaps critical thinking but adds a focus on the information environment itself: how knowledge is organised and where to look for it.[^s1] CILIP links it directly to recognising bias and misinformation, and argues it lets people question assumptions and authority where appropriate and take a full part in democratic life.[^s3]

## Evidence

Two findings stand out. First, people tend to overrate their own information skills. In Gross and Latham's 2007 study of 51 incoming university students, every student overestimated their score on a standard information literacy test before taking it (guessing about 75% correct against an actual 65%), 45% tested as non-proficient, and the non-proficient group overestimated most; the gap narrowed but did not close after testing. The sample was small, which limits generalisation.[^s5]

Second, instruction helps, but the evidence on how much is thinner than often assumed. Koufogiannakis and Wiebe's 2006 systematic review screened 4,356 citations, kept 122 studies, and meta-analysed 16: traditional and self-directed instruction both beat no instruction, and computer-assisted teaching performed about as well as classroom teaching. Most outcomes, however, measured only lower-order learning such as remembering and understanding.[^s4] A 2023 scoping review of 49 studies of single-session first-year library instruction was more sceptical: skill gains were modest, confidence rose more than ability, effects on grades were small or mixed, and many studies lacked controls or reported results selectively.[^s6] Because the need is well documented but the effect of common teaching formats is disputed, the evidence status here is mixed.

## Limitations

Definitions vary in breadth, from Zurkowski's job-skill framing to CILIP's civic and ethical one, so studies that "measure information literacy" may not measure the same thing.[^s2][^s3] Test-based measures also tend to capture knowledge of search tools rather than the higher-order evaluation that matters most for reasoning.[^s4] And confidence gains after instruction can mask little real change, echoing the overestimation problem.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a domain-specific application of critical thinking, which the 1989 ALA report and CILIP's 2018 definition both place at its core.[^s1][^s3]
- **Introduced by** Paul Zurkowski, whose 1974 report to the US National Commission on Libraries and Information Science is the usual first use of the term.[^s2]
- **Studied in** library and information science, whose researchers produced the systematic review and evidence summaries on how it is taught and measured.[^s4][^s5]
- **Applied in** information-literacy instruction, the library teaching whose effects the 2006 meta-analysis assessed.[^s4]
- **Demonstrated by** Gross and Latham (2007), which showed how far low-skilled students overestimate their information skills, a pattern close to the overconfidence effect and relevant to metacognition.[^s5]
- **Challenged by** the 2023 scoping review of one-shot instruction, which questions whether the most common way of teaching it achieves much.[^s6]
- Related to source evaluation, lateral reading, SIFT and primary versus secondary sources, the practical components most often taught under its name, and to misinformation and disinformation, the problem CILIP highlights.[^s3]
