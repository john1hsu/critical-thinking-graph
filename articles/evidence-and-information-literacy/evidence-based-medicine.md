# Evidence-based medicine

Evidence-based medicine (EBM) is the practice of deciding how to care for an individual patient by making conscientious, explicit and judicious use of the best current evidence, as Sackett and colleagues put it in 1996.[^s1] Their account stresses that research evidence is only one ingredient: it must be joined with the clinician's individual expertise, and the decision is about a particular person, not a population average.[^s1] The later Sicily statement generalised the idea to all health professions as evidence-based practice and placed the final choice with the person receiving care, informed by the clinician's explicit and tacit knowledge and bounded by available resources.[^s4] For critical thinkers, EBM is the best-developed real-world model of how to weigh evidence of differing quality under time pressure and with something at stake.

## Origin

Sackett's group noted that EBM's intellectual roots reach back at least to mid-nineteenth-century Paris.[^s1] The modern movement grew at McMaster University in Hamilton, Ontario, where David Sackett's Department of Clinical Epidemiology and Biostatistics ran critical-appraisal courses from 1978 and published a "Readers' Guides" series in the *Canadian Medical Association Journal* in 1981.[^s2] Gordon Guyatt coined the phrase "evidence-based medicine" in a 1991 *ACP Journal Club* editorial, and on 4 November 1992 the Evidence-Based Medicine Working Group announced it in *JAMA* as a new paradigm for medical practice; the related Users' Guides series, directed by Guyatt, eventually ran to 32 articles over about 11 years.[^s2] By the mid-1990s the movement had spread to training programmes, specialist centres and review bodies such as the Cochrane Collaboration, prompting both enthusiasm and suspicion.[^s1]

## How it works

The Sicily statement breaks evidence-based practice into five steps: turn the uncertainty into an answerable question; search systematically for the best available evidence; appraise it for validity, clinical relevance and applicability; apply the result in practice; and evaluate how well you did.[^s4] Appraisal leans on an evidence hierarchy: although versions differ, all rank randomised controlled trials and systematic reviews of them above cohort studies, case series and, at the bottom, expert opinion and consensus.[^s5]

A workplace example: an occupational-health nurse is asked whether standing desks will reduce back pain among office staff. Rather than following a colleague's enthusiasm or a vendor brochure, she frames a question (in adult desk workers, do sit-stand desks reduce back-pain days compared with ordinary desks?), looks for a systematic review of trials, checks whether the trials were randomised, sizeable and independently funded, and asks whether participants resemble her staff. She then discusses the realistic size of any benefit with the people concerned, notes their preferences and the budget, and agrees to review outcomes after six months. Each step maps onto one of the five steps above.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Use EBM's logic whenever a decision depends on what works: ask a focused question, prefer study designs that rule out more alternative explanations, and then ask whether the evidence transfers to the case in front of you.[^s5] Greenhalgh and colleagues add that good practice presents benefits and harms in plain, individual terms such as the number needed to treat, uses judgment rather than mechanical rule-following, and reaches decisions through conversation about what matters to the person.[^s3] Common pitfalls are treating a guideline as a command, mistaking a statistically significant but tiny average effect for a meaningful one, and forgetting that a person with several conditions may fit none of the trial populations.[^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

EBM has been criticised from the start: in 1996 its founders were already answering charges that it served cost-cutters and suppressed clinical freedom.[^s1] In 2014 Greenhalgh, Howick and Maskrey argued that the movement faced a crisis: industry was shaping which questions got studied and which trials got published, guidelines had multiplied beyond what clinicians could read, large trials chased marginal gains, and single-disease rules fitted patients with multimorbidity poorly.[^s3] Philosophers have questioned the primacy of randomised trials. Reiss and Ankeny's encyclopedia entry reviews arguments that randomisation does not guarantee balanced groups in small trials, that many well-established treatments were never trialled, and that a trial's result in its sample need not hold for a different target population, so clinical judgment cannot be engineered away.[^s5] Russo and Williamson argue that causal claims require evidence of a mechanism as well as statistical association, a view others reject or accept only in qualified form.[^s5]

## Connections

EBM was named by Gordon Guyatt and developed in David Sackett's clinical-epidemiology department, so it links to both and to epidemiology.[^s2] It is the parent of the hierarchy of evidence and leans on randomised controlled trials and systematic reviews, which is why the RCT is listed as a prerequisite.[^s5] It contrasts with simply deferring to expert opinion, which sits at the bottom of its hierarchies.[^s5] Conflicts of interest amplify its weaknesses because industry influence distorts the evidence base it relies on.[^s3] It is challenged by the Russo–Williamson thesis on mechanisms and is a central topic in the philosophy of medicine.[^s5] The Cochrane Collaboration, a systematic-review body whose growth the founders cited as part of the movement's spread, connects it to the related nodes on Archie Cochrane and Iain Chalmers.[^s1]
