# Bounded rationality

Bounded rationality is the view that people and organisations decide under real limits: they lack complete information, cannot foresee every consequence, and have only so much time and computing power. Instead of judging them against an agent who optimises over every option, it asks which search procedures, stopping rules and simplifications let a limited mind do well enough.[^s1][^s2] It is the umbrella under which most modern work on heuristics sits, and it changes the critical thinker's question from "was this the best possible choice?" to "was this a sensible process given what could be known and computed?"[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Herbert Simon set out the programme in a 1955 article in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, arguing that the "economic man" of classical theory, with full knowledge, stable preferences and unlimited calculating skill, should give way to a model of choice compatible with the information and computational capacities organisms actually have.[^s2] He used the phrase "bounded rationality" as a label for this critique in 1957.[^s1] Simon later contrasted *substantive* rationality, which is judged by whether the outcome is best by some criterion, with *procedural* rationality, which is judged by whether the deliberative process was sound and proportionate to the stakes.[^s5][^s1]

## How it works

Simon's 1955 model replaces global optimisation with a handful of simplifications. Outcomes are sorted into "satisfactory" and "unsatisfactory" rather than ranked on a full scale; alternatives are examined one at a time, and the first satisfactory one is taken; and the aspiration level rises when good options are easy to find and falls when they are scarce.[^s2] This is *satisficing*. His own example is a house seller: the optimal asking price would require knowing the distribution of future offers, but a realistic seller starts high, lowers the price as offers arrive, and accepts the first offer that meets the current target.[^s2]

A workplace version: a manager must hire a contractor this week. A full optimiser would solicit every bid in the region and score each on a weighted model. A boundedly rational manager sets minimum requirements (licensed, available within ten days, under budget), reviews bids in the order they come in, relaxes the deadline requirement if nobody qualifies, and signs the first contractor who clears the bar. Whether that was good reasoning depends on whether the thresholds and the amount of search fit the size of the job, not on whether a cheaper contractor existed somewhere.[^s1][^s5]

## Interpretations

The concept has been developed in three distinct directions, and confusing them is a common source of talking past one another.[^s3]

- **Optimisation under constraints.** Many economists add search or computation costs to a standard model and let the agent optimise anyway. Todd and Gigerenzer argue this keeps the very ideal Simon rejected and demands even more computation.[^s3][^s1]
- **Heuristics and biases.** Daniel Kahneman described his work with Amos Tversky as an attempt to draw a "map of bounded rationality": a catalogue of systematic departures from rational-agent norms, explained by fast intuitive processes, accessibility and attribute substitution.[^s4]
- **Ecological rationality.** Gigerenzer and colleagues stress Simon's image of rational behaviour as shaped by two blades of a pair of scissors, the mind's limits and the environment's structure. On this view a simple heuristic is judged by how well it fits the environment it is used in, not by a logical or probabilistic norm.[^s3]

## Evidence and limitations

That people search incompletely and use thresholds and shortcuts is well documented, but what follows from it is disputed, so the evidence is best described as mixed.[^s1][^s3] Kahneman's 2003 Nobel lecture summarises decades of experiments showing framing effects, reference dependence and neglect of base rates and scope, which he treats as costs of bounded cognition.[^s4] Todd and Gigerenzer review studies in which one-reason heuristics used about a third of the cues of regression models yet came close to, or in cross-validation beat, them across 20 real-world datasets, and in which recognition alone predicted city sizes surprisingly well; they concede that some claims about the benefits of cognitive limits remain debated.[^s3] The SEP entry adds that modelling bounded rationality as constrained optimisation fits economists' "as if" methods but says little about actual procedures, and that simple decision rules can misfire, as when ultimatum-game players reject low but positive offers.[^s1]

## Connections

Bounded rationality was introduced by Herbert Simon in his 1955 paper *A behavioral model of rational choice*, as a direct alternative to the full-information optimiser assumed by expected utility theory.[^s1][^s2] Satisficing is its central decision rule.[^s2] It is the parent idea of the heuristics-and-biases programme, which Kahneman explicitly framed as mapping bounded rationality, and of the related prospect theory, dual-process theory and attribute substitution; that framing made it a core topic of behavioral economics.[^s4] Gerd Gigerenzer's fast-and-frugal heuristics and ecological rationality are the rival development, studied within judgment and decision-making research.[^s3]
