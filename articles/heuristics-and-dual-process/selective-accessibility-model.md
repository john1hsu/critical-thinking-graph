# Selective accessibility model

The selective accessibility model (SAM) is a social-cognition explanation of the anchoring effect. It holds that anchoring is not a failure to adjust far enough from a starting number but a by-product of how people answer the comparison question that usually comes first. Asked whether a value is higher or lower than an anchor, people tentatively test whether the target could actually be close to the anchor; that test brings anchor-consistent knowledge to mind, and the same knowledge is then the easiest to use when they produce their own estimate.[^s1][^s2] In the model's own terms, anchoring is a special case of semantic priming.[^s1]

## Origin

Fritz Strack and Thomas Mussweiler developed the account at German universities in the late 1990s. Their 1997 paper in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* framed anchoring as priming and reported three studies on when activated information is and is not used.[^s1] A 1999 paper in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* gave the account its name, linking it explicitly to hypothesis-consistent testing.[^s2] A 2000 paper with Tim Pfeiffer derived a remedy from it, arguing that deliberately considering the opposite compensates for selective accessibility.[^s6]

## How it works

The model describes two steps. In the comparison step, a person tests the single hypothesis that the target equals the anchor and, as in positive hypothesis testing generally, searches for evidence that fits it.[^s1] In the estimation step, that evidence is already active, so it dominates the absolute judgment, but only to the extent that it is applicable and representative of the new question.[^s1]

A workplace example shows the pattern. A director asks a team, "Will the system migration take more or less than 18 months?" To answer, engineers picture what would make it a long project: legacy integrations, data cleanup, vendor delays. When the director then asks for their best estimate, those reasons are the first ones that come back, and the estimates drift toward 18 months even if the number was a guess. On the SAM view the number did its work by steering which knowledge was retrieved, not by serving as a starting point that people adjusted away from.[^s1][^s3]

Strack and Mussweiler supported this with three findings. Anchoring was much stronger when the estimate concerned the same dimension as the comparison (for example, the height rather than the width of a landmark), which a purely numeric account struggles to explain. When the comparison concerned a different object, estimates could move away from the anchor instead of toward it. And with plausible anchors the comparison took longer but the later estimate was faster, as if the needed information had already been gathered.[^s1]

## How to apply it

For a practitioner the model's main lesson is diagnostic: an anchor is dangerous because it shapes what you remember, so the fix is to change what you remember. Mussweiler, Strack and Pfeiffer argued that generating reasons why the anchor is inappropriate offsets the one-sided retrieval it triggers.[^s6] In practice that means writing your own estimate before hearing any number, or listing reasons the true value could lie far from a proposed figure before settling on an estimate.[^s6][^s1] Bahník and Strack also derived a sharper prediction: if the knowledge an anchor activates is the same knowledge the estimate would use anyway, the anchor should add nothing. Their temperature and car-price studies largely matched that pattern.[^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence for the model is real but uneven, so this graph marks it as contested. Its original studies were small, with around 30 participants each in the first two.[^s1] The 2016 overlap studies used samples of about 600 online participants each and found the predicted asymmetries, though one low-anchor effect was not significant and the authors called for independent replication.[^s3] Against this, Harris and colleagues in 2019 tried to reproduce a signature test of the model, the claim that an anchor speeds recognition of anchor-consistent words in a lexical decision task. Across five experiments with 128 participants per set, anchors moved estimates as usual but never produced the predicted word-recognition pattern, and a Bayesian meta-analysis favoured the null by about 21 to 1.[^s4] Those authors still regarded selective accessibility as a good account of anchoring, but argued that this line of priming evidence should no longer count strongly in its favour.[^s4] A 2016 study of visual quantity and age estimation found large anchoring effects that were unaffected by cognitive load or source credibility and argued that the model cannot fully explain anchoring in tasks with little semantic content, where numeric priming fits better.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Fritz Strack and Thomas Mussweiler in their 1997 paper.[^s1]
- **Explained by** semantic priming: the model treats anchoring as priming of anchor-consistent knowledge.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** the 1997 dimension, object and plausibility studies.[^s1]
- **Supported by** the 2016 information-overlap studies, which confirmed a novel prediction of the model.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** the 2019 multi-experiment failure to replicate its lexical-decision evidence.[^s4]
- **Contrasts with** numeric priming, which locates anchoring in activation of the number itself and may better explain anchoring with semantically thin targets.[^s5]
- It also contrasts with the anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic, which treats the anchor as a starting point for insufficient adjustment; the 2016 overlap results are hard for that account to produce.[^s3]
