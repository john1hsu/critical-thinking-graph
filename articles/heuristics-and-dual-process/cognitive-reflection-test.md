# Cognitive reflection test

The cognitive reflection test (CRT) is a three-item quiz of arithmetic brainteasers, each built so that a wrong answer springs to mind at once while the right answer needs a moment of checking. Its author presented it as a quick measure of one cognitive trait: the disposition to reflect on a problem instead of reporting the first answer that occurs to you.[^s1] It has since become one of the most widely used individual-difference measures in judgment and decision-making research, valued because it is short, free-response and behavioural rather than a self-report questionnaire.[^s2]

## Origin

Shane Frederick, a decision researcher, introduced the test in a 2005 article in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*. He reported that CRT scores predicted the kinds of choices that test expected utility theory and prospect theory, such as patience about delayed rewards and willingness to take risks for gains, and that the test related to other measures of cognitive ability and cognitive style.[^s1] He also reported sizeable sex differences in scores and linked them to differences in time and risk preference.[^s1] The test is framed in dual-process terms: the items invite a fast "System 1" answer that a slower "System 2" check must suppress.[^s5]

## How it works

The best-known item is the bat-and-ball problem: a bat and a ball cost $1.10 together, and the bat costs $1.00 more than the ball, so what does the ball cost? Most people first think "10 cents", but then the bat would cost $1.10 and the total $1.20; the answer is 5 cents. The other two original items, about machines making widgets and a lily-pad patch that doubles daily, share the same structure of a tempting "lure" answer.[^s5] The score is simply the number of correct answers, so it ranges from 0 to 3. In large naive samples, scores bunch at the bottom: in one set of studies over 40% of first-time takers got none right and only about 13% got all three.[^s3]

A workplace illustration: a team lead asked how long it takes 100 analysts to review 100 files, if 5 analysts review 5 files in 5 minutes, may blurt "100 minutes". A colleague who pauses to note that each analyst reviews one file in 5 minutes reaches the correct "5 minutes". The CRT treats that pause, rather than raw arithmetic skill, as the thing being measured.[^s1]

## How to apply it

- **As a research covariate.** Researchers add the CRT to studies of biases, risk and time preference, or belief, to test whether more reflective people respond differently.[^s2]
- **Record prior exposure.** About a third of online participants in one six-study series had seen the items before, and they scored markedly higher; the authors recommend asking about prior exposure and using it as a covariate.[^s3]
- **Use fresh or expanded items.** Because the original three items are widely publicised, many studies add newer items or alternative versions such as a multiple-choice form.[^s5][^s4]
- **Do not over-read one person's score.** With three items, the test has limited reliability and strong floor effects, so it is better for group comparisons than for judging an individual.[^s3]
- **For teaching,** the bat-and-ball problem is a quick classroom demonstration that a confident answer can be wrong and that a habit of checking pays off.[^s1]

## Evidence

The CRT's predictive power is well documented. Toplak, West and Stanovich (2011) found it predicted performance across a wide battery of heuristics-and-biases tasks better than measures of intelligence, thinking dispositions and executive functioning, and still explained extra variance after those were controlled; they argued it captures a tendency toward "miserly" processing that intelligence tests miss.[^s2] Białek and Pennycook (2018), pooling about 2,500 participants, found that prior exposure raised scores (d ≈ 0.57) yet did not weaken the test's correlations with 17 outcome variables.[^s3]

What the test measures is disputed. Attali and Bar-Hillel (2020) compared CRT items with ordinary maths items, with and without tempting lures, in samples of about 250 and 1,286 people. A single maths-ability factor fitted as well as a separate "reflection" factor, the two factors correlated at about .90 or more, and well-chosen plain maths items predicted reasoning and choice tasks about as well as CRT items. They concluded the CRT items are first of all maths problems, though they did not settle whether ability, intelligence or reflective style drives the prediction.[^s4] The status is therefore mixed: a robust predictor whose interpretation as a pure measure of reflection is contested.

## Connections

The CRT was introduced by Shane Frederick in his 2005 article *Cognitive Reflection and Decision Making*.[^s1] It is explained by dual-process theory, which frames each item as a conflict between an intuitive and a deliberate answer, and it is the standard behavioural measure behind discussions of deliberate reflection and attribute substitution.[^s5] Its predictive validity is supported by the Toplak, West and Stanovich (2011) study, and it is a staple instrument of judgment and decision-making research.[^s2] It is challenged by Attali and Bar-Hillel's (2020) analysis, and that analysis is also why the CRT is often confused with numeracy: high scores may reflect mathematical skill as much as a reflective disposition.[^s4] Prior-exposure research shows the measure survives repeated use but should be administered with care.[^s3]
