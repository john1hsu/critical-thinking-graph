# Satisficing

Satisficing is a way of choosing in which a person sets a standard for what would count as good enough, looks at options one after another, and takes the first one that meets the standard. It does not rank every option on a single scale.[^s1][^s2] Hertwig and Kozyreva describe it as heuristic search governed by a stop rule, with aspirations that can be adjusted. In their account, the aspiration level is the target a satisfactory option must reach or exceed.[^s2] Satisficing is the best-known procedure within bounded rationality. It replaces the question "which option is best?" with "which option is good enough, given what it costs to keep looking?"[^s2]

## Origin

Herbert Simon introduced the term in "Rational Choice and the Structure of the Environment", published in *Psychological Review* in 1956. He argued there that organisms adapt well enough to "satisfice" and do not, in general, optimize.[^s1] His model is a simple creature foraging for food on a surface where food heaps are scattered sparsely. It can see only a short distance, moves at limited speed, and stores a limited amount of energy. Simon showed that a creature with a fixed aspiration level and a short planning horizon could survive with near certainty if it searched with purpose, using no utility function at all. The same creature moving at random had a survival chance of under 1%.[^s1] He concluded that economic and statistical theories of maximizing are a poor starting point for explaining how real organisms choose. He suggested that how good a simple strategy is depends on the structure of the environment as much as on the chooser's mental capacity.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

Satisficing has three parts: an aspiration level, a sequential search, and a stop rule. Options often arrive one at a time and may disappear if not taken. The search therefore ends at the first option that clears the aspiration level, not after every option has been compared.[^s2] Aspirations are not fixed. A chooser who finds satisfactory options easily can raise the bar, and one who finds none can lower it.[^s2]

A workplace example: an operations manager must replace a failing courier within two weeks. She writes down three must-haves: on-time delivery above 97%, coverage of all current postcodes, and a price within budget. She then contacts carriers in order of referral and signs with the first one that meets all three. If the first five fall short on price, she revisits the budget rather than searching forever. Comparing every carrier in the region might find a slightly better deal, but the delay and effort could cost more than the difference.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

Choice experiments support satisficing as a description of how people search. In a 2011 *American Economic Review* study, Caplin, Dean and Martin gave subjects choices they could not fully examine. Most subjects searched in sequence and stopped once they found an option above a reservation level, which fits Simon's model. Those reservation levels and search orders also shifted in systematic ways when the decision environment changed.[^s3]

The evidence on satisficing as a personality style is weaker. In 2002, Schwartz and colleagues introduced a Maximization Scale. Across seven samples, people who scored high as "maximizers" reported less happiness, optimism and life satisfaction and more regret and depression than "satisficers". They were also less satisfied with their purchases.[^s4] Later measurement work questioned this picture. Diab, Gillespie and Highhouse (2008, 191 students) found that the original scale mixed in content other than seeking the best option. A narrower 9-item scale was related to regret but not to life satisfaction.[^s5] Turner and colleagues (2012) found low reliability in the original scale. Their satisficing subscale behaved as a separate construct, not as the opposite end of maximizing. They also called their well-being correlations tentative because they came from single student samples.[^s6] The overall status is **mixed**. Satisficing is well supported as a search procedure. The claim that maximizers are less happy depends on how maximizing is measured.[^s5][^s6]

## How to spot it

Look for a decision that ended once an option passed a threshold rather than after a comparison: "the first candidate who ticked every box", or "the first quote under budget". Ask what the aspiration level was, whether anyone wrote it down, and whether it moved during the search.[^s1][^s2]

## How to counter it

Satisficing is often the sensible choice, so the aim is to use it deliberately rather than to remove it. Because the result depends on the aspiration level and on how it is adjusted, write the criteria down before searching. Also decide in advance what would justify raising or lowering the bar, so that "good enough" is not set by whichever option happens to come first.[^s1][^s2] When the stakes are high and options can be compared side by side, such as in competing explanations, vendor contracts or diagnoses, compare several options at once before stopping, since incomplete search can miss the best option.[^s3] When stakes are low and searching is costly, accept the first adequate option and move on. Measures of maximizing link exhaustive comparison to regret.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Herbert Simon** in his 1956 *Psychological Review* paper, where the word and the foraging model first appear.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with maximizing**, the search for the best possible option. Schwartz and colleagues turned this contrast into an individual-difference measure.[^s4]
- **Supported by** Caplin, Dean and Martin's 2011 choice experiment, in which most subjects stopped searching at a reservation level.[^s3]
- **Studied in** judgment and decision making as the core procedure of bounded rationality, alongside later simple-heuristics research.[^s2]
- **Narrower than bounded rationality** and **related to** fast-and-frugal heuristics and ecological rationality. Hertwig and Kozyreva trace these back to Simon's view that rational behaviour depends on both the task environment and the chooser's abilities.[^s2]
