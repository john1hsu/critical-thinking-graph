# Cognitive psychology

Cognitive psychology is the part of psychology that studies how people take in, store, transform and use information: perceiving, attending, remembering, solving problems and reasoning.[^s1] Britannica describes it as the study of human cognition with particular attention to how thinking shapes learning and behavior, and notes that some scholars treat it as a branch of the wider cognitive science.[^s5] For critical thinking it matters because nearly everything known empirically about how people actually reason (as opposed to how logic says they should) comes from its laboratories.[^s4]

## Origin

Through the 1950s, American experimental psychology was dominated by behaviorism, which restricted the discipline to observable stimuli and responses and set mental representations aside.[^s3] George Miller, one of the participants, describes the change that followed as a counter-revolution against that definition and dates the birth of cognitive science to an MIT symposium on 11 September 1956.[^s2] Around that time Miller's work on the limited capacity of short-term memory, Noam Chomsky's critique of behaviorist accounts of language, and early artificial intelligence made talk of internal mental processes scientifically respectable again.[^s3] Miller credits Chomsky's syntactic theory, more than Shannon's information theory, with showing psychologists how to model such processes.[^s2]

The field took its name from Ulric Neisser's 1967 book *Cognitive Psychology*, which pulled perception, pattern recognition, attention, memory and problem solving into a single information-processing account; Alan Baddeley later said the book named the field, and Neisser became known as its father.[^s1] Neisser himself grew dissatisfied with narrow laboratory tasks and, in *Cognition and Reality* (1976), argued for a more ecological approach influenced by the Gibsons' theory of direct perception.[^s1]

## How it works

The working assumption, shared with cognitive science, is that thinking is best understood as mental representations (concepts, rules, images, analogies) plus procedures that operate on them.[^s3] The main method is the controlled experiment with human participants, interpreted through a theory and ideally a computational model of the process being tested.[^s3]

A workplace illustration: a team suspects its reviewers approve pull requests that "look right" without checking the logic. A cognitive psychologist would turn this hunch into an experiment, for instance giving reviewers matched code changes that are either valid or subtly broken and either conventionally styled or not, then measuring accuracy and response time. If tidy-but-broken changes are approved more often, the result points to a representation (surface familiarity) standing in for analysis, and the model suggests an intervention to test next, such as a checklist that forces a step-by-step trace. This is the same logic behind classic reasoning studies, where people asked to judge whether conclusions follow necessarily make systematic logical errors and are strongly swayed by the content and context of the problem.[^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

The deduction paradigm, in which participants evaluate syllogisms and conditionals against the rules of logic, was one of the field's main research programmes for about four decades.[^s4] Evans's 2002 review argued that it grew from a logicist outlook that few reasoning researchers still hold: logic is no longer widely treated as either the right standard for everyday reasoning or a description of how people reason, and the paradigm is now used mainly to study pragmatic and probabilistic processes.[^s4] He also warned that standard instructions can make pragmatic influences look like biases, a caution worth keeping in mind when reading claims that people are "irrational".[^s4]

The broader computational framework faces its own critiques. Thagard lists challenges that it neglects emotion, consciousness, the body, the physical and social environment, and dynamical rather than computational processes; he regards several as calls for expansion rather than refutations.[^s3] Miller, looking back, preferred to speak of the cognitive sciences in the plural because the hoped-for unified science had not fully arrived.[^s2]

## Connections

Cognitive psychology is a subfield of psychology and is **part of** cognitive science, one of its core disciplines alongside linguistics, artificial intelligence, neuroscience, anthropology and philosophy.[^s3] It **contrasts with** behaviorism, the stimulus-response programme it displaced, and was **influenced by** Noam Chomsky's linguistics.[^s2] It was **introduced by** Ulric Neisser in the sense that his 1967 textbook gave the field its name.[^s1] Its research on deduction is the home of Peter Wason's tasks and of the study of errors in deductive reasoning, and its later turn toward pragmatic and probabilistic processes connects it to dual-process theory, the heuristics and biases program and judgment and decision making.[^s4] It is **challenged by** embodied and situated approaches to cognition, which echo Neisser's own ecological turn.[^s3][^s1]
