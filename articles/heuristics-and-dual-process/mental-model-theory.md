# Mental model theory

Mental model theory is an account of how ordinary people reason. It holds that, given some premises, a reasoner imagines the distinct possibilities those premises allow, represents each as a mental model, draws a conclusion that holds in the models, and, when reasoning carefully, checks it by looking for a counterexample: a possibility consistent with the premises in which the conclusion fails.[^s1] It stands against the older view that the mind contains a tacit "mental logic" of formal inference rules.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The research group that maintains the theory traces the idea of reasoning with models to Charles Sanders Peirce in the 1890s and to Kenneth Craik, who in 1943 proposed that the mind builds small-scale models of reality to anticipate events (although Craik still thought reasoning ran on verbal rules).[^s5] The modern theory was set out by the psychologist Philip Johnson-Laird in his 1983 book *Mental Models*, written when most cognitive scientists assumed people reasoned with symbolic logic.[^s5][^s1] It has since been extended from deduction to quantifiers, spatial and temporal relations, causation, induction and explanation, and implemented in computer programs.[^s1][^s5]

## How it works

The theory rests on a few principles.[^s1][^s5]

- **Each model is a possibility.** A model stands for what is common to one set of possibilities; "A or B, but not both" yields two models, an inclusive "A or B" yields three.[^s1][^s5]
- **Models are iconic where they can be.** Their structure mirrors the structure of what they represent, so relations nobody stated, such as the order of objects in a layout, can be read off directly.[^s1]
- **More models, more difficulty.** Inferences that call for several models take longer and produce more errors; working memory struggles beyond about three, so people tend to settle on the first or simplest model.[^s1]
- **The principle of truth.** To save working memory, models represent what is true in each possibility and leave out what is false. This is the theory's most distinctive prediction, because it implies systematic, compelling errors.[^s1]
- **Fast and slow.** An intuitive process builds an initial model; a slower, deliberate process searches for alternative models and counterexamples.[^s5]

**Workplace example.** A manager is told that exactly one of these is true: (1) the vendor's contract will be renewed; (2) the project gets new budget or new staff, but not both. She then learns the contract will be renewed. Can the project still get both new budget and new staff? Most people say no: their models of statement 2 show only budget-without-staff and staff-without-budget. But if statement 1 is the true one, statement 2 is false, and one way for it to be false is that the project gets *both*. So the correct answer is yes. Johnson-Laird reports this "illusory inference" with an equivalent bread-soup-salad problem.[^s1]

## How to apply it

As a tool for one's own reasoning, the theory recommends the habits that its errors point to.[^s1][^s5]

1. **List the possibilities explicitly**, including what each premise rules *out*, before drawing a conclusion. Writing out the cases where a statement is false is the antidote to the principle of truth.[^s1]
2. **Hunt for a counterexample.** Ask whether any possibility fits all the premises yet contradicts the conclusion; if one exists, the inference is invalid.[^s1]
3. **Prefer exclusive, simple framings and iconic diagrams** when presenting a problem to others, since fewer models and diagrams whose layout matches the situation improve accuracy.[^s1]
4. **Be wary of confidence on compound "or else" and "only one of these is true" premises**, where the illusions are strongest.[^s2]

## Evidence

The illusions are the theory's showcase. In Johnson-Laird and Savary's 1999 studies, every one of 16 Princeton students drew the invalid conclusion on two card-hand illusions while doing well on matched control problems, and in a later experiment illusory problems drew fallacious answers 63-85% of the time against 98% accuracy on the control.[^s2] Participants were confident in their wrong answers as well as their right ones.[^s2] Other studies summarised in a 2010 review found that implausible conclusions prompted more search for counterexamples, that iconic diagrams helped, and that exclusive disjunctions were easier than inclusive ones, as the model count predicts.[^s1]

The theory is not settled. A 2012 meta-analysis by Khemlani and Johnson-Laird compared 12 theories of syllogistic reasoning on six data sets: the mental-model theory predicted 95% of the conclusions people actually drew, more than any rival, but had a lower overall accuracy (78%) than the verbal-models (84%) and conversion (83%) accounts, and the authors concluded that none of the 12 theories was adequate.[^s3] Johnson-Laird himself concedes that the theory's account of conditionals has not convinced critics.[^s1] Advocates of mental logic, notably Lance Rips, argue the model theory is too inexplicit to say exactly where its predictions come from, and reviewers of that debate note that neither approach readily explains content effects in reasoning.[^s4] Probabilistic and domain-specific accounts offer further rivals.[^s4][^s5] Hence `evidenceStatus: contested`: the illusions themselves are robust laboratory findings, but whether models, rules or probabilities best explain reasoning is an open dispute.

## Connections

- **Broader:** it is one of the main theories within the psychology of reasoning, and **studied in** cognitive psychology.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Philip Johnson-Laird and **introduced in** his 1983 book *Mental Models*; **influenced by** Peirce's idea of reasoning with diagram-like representations.[^s5]
- **Demonstrated by** Johnson-Laird and Savary's 1999 illusory-inference experiments, which tested the principle of truth's most counterintuitive prediction.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** Rips's *The Psychology of Proof*, the leading formal-rule (mental logic) alternative.[^s4]
- **Related** to dual-process theory, since the model theory builds in a fast intuitive system and a slower counterexample search;[^s5] to the method of counterexample, which is the normative version of the search the theory describes;[^s1] to the syllogism, its main testing ground;[^s3] and to formal fallacies such as affirming a disjunct, which turns on the difference the theory draws between an inclusive "or" (three models) and an exclusive one (two).[^s1]
