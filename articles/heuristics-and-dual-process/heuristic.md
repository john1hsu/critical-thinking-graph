# Heuristic

A heuristic is a simple strategy for making a judgment or choice that ignores part of the information available, whether it is applied deliberately or runs automatically.[^s3] The everyday labels "rule of thumb" and "mental shortcut" capture the idea, but researchers have warned that they say little about what a given heuristic actually does; Shah and Oppenheimer (2008) proposed that a process should count as a heuristic only when it demonstrably reduces the effort a task requires.[^s2] Every named strategy in this domain, from availability to recognition to satisficing, is a heuristic in this general sense.

## Origin

The word comes from a Greek root meaning "serving to find out or discover".[^s4] The mathematician George Pólya used it for methods that help one find a solution, as opposed to analysis that checks one, and Herbert Simon, who had studied with Pólya, built on the idea when he proposed satisficing in the 1950s.[^s4] Newell and Simon (1972) then brought the term into psychology for simple processes that stand in for complex algorithms, rooted in Simon's view that minds work within limited time, knowledge and computing power.[^s2] In 1974 Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman argued in *Science* that people reduce hard probability judgments to a few heuristics, namely representativeness, availability, and adjustment from an anchor, which are generally useful but sometimes produce severe and systematic errors.[^s1] From the 1990s Gerd Gigerenzer's group developed precise process models such as the recognition heuristic and take-the-best, shifting the emphasis from error to adaptive performance.[^s2][^s4]

## How it works

Shah and Oppenheimer describe five ways a heuristic can lighten the load compared with weighing and adding up every cue: look at fewer cues, use cues that are easier to retrieve or store, simplify how cues are weighted (for example, weight them all equally), integrate less information, and consider fewer alternatives.[^s2] These moves can work alone or in combination, and people need not be aware of using them.[^s2]

Take a workplace case. A team lead asked to estimate a new project's duration might simply recall the last similar project and nudge that figure up or down. That saves effort, but Tversky and Kahneman showed that adjustment from a starting value is usually too small: in one study, people who first saw a random number of 10 gave a median estimate of 25% for the share of African countries in the United Nations, while those who saw 65 gave 45%.[^s1] The same lead choosing between two suppliers might instead pick the one whose name she recognizes, or check reliability first and stop as soon as one supplier wins on it; these are simple rules that ignore most of the spreadsheet.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limitations

Two research traditions read the same concept differently. The heuristics-and-biases view compares judgments with logic and probability theory and treats heuristics as the source of predictable errors, much as judging distance by clarity makes objects in haze look farther away.[^s1] The fast-and-frugal view asks when ignoring information pays off; a 2011 review in the *Annual Review of Psychology* concluded that individuals and organizations in business, medicine and law often rely on heuristics adaptively, and that ignoring some information can produce more accurate judgments than combining everything, particularly when predictability is low and samples are small.[^s3] Gigerenzer and Brighton explain this "less-is-more" effect through the bias-variance trade-off: a biased but simple rule can avoid the overfitting that makes flexible models predict poorly from limited data, as in cross-validated comparisons where take-the-best and equal-weight tallying outpredicted multiple regression.[^s4]

The limits are real on both sides. A heuristic that serves well in one environment can mislead in another, so its value is an empirical question rather than a given.[^s3] The concept itself has also grown loose: Shah and Oppenheimer note redundant constructs under different names and heuristics proposed without evidence that they reduce effort.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Treat "which heuristic am I using, and does this environment reward it?" as a standing check. Where stakes are high and good data exist, compare the quick answer with a fuller analysis; where the future is uncertain and data are thin, a simple, transparent rule may be the better tool.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

A heuristic is a form of reasoning that trades completeness for speed. The concept is explained by bounded rationality, the view that real minds work within limited resources, and it owes its psychological framing to Herbert Simon.[^s2] Pólya's use of the term for methods of discovery shaped the tradition Simon inherited.[^s4] Heuristics are a core subject of judgment and decision-making research, where the heuristics-and-biases program links them to cognitive biases and the fast-and-frugal program and ecological rationality ask when they succeed; take-the-best is one of its best-known models.[^s3][^s4] They contrast with algorithms, the exhaustive procedures they stand in for.[^s2] Attribute substitution and dual-process theory are later attempts to explain what heuristics share.[^s2]
