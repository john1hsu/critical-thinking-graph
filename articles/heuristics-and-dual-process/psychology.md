# Psychology

Psychology is the scientific discipline that studies psychological and biological processes and behavior in humans and other animals; Britannica notes that it combines a large profession of practitioners with a smaller, growing science of mind, brain and social behavior.[^s5] For critical thinking, psychology is the empirical counterpart to logic. Logic and the theory of rational choice describe how inferences and decisions should go, while psychology measures how people actually perceive, remember, judge and reason, and where they predictably depart from those standards.[^s2] Almost every cognitive bias, heuristic and debiasing technique in this graph is a psychological finding, so knowing how the discipline produces its claims is part of evaluating them.

## Origin

Questions about mind and knowledge belonged to philosophy for most of history, from Plato and Aristotle to Descartes, whose mind-body dualism still frames debates in the field.[^s5] Psychology became a separate experimental science in nineteenth-century Germany. Wilhelm Wundt, often called the father of experimental psychology, published his *Principles of Physiological Psychology* in 1873–74, and in 1879 the University of Leipzig recognized his equipment room as what is described as the world's first laboratory devoted to psychology.[^s1] Wundt answered Kant's doubt that inner experience could be studied exactly by arguing that some aspects of it can be measured experimentally, as Weber and Fechner had already shown; he still saw psychology as a hybrid that mediated between the natural sciences and philosophy.[^s1] His students measured things like reaction times to controlled stimuli.[^s4]

In the United States, William James argued that psychology should study how mental activity helps organisms adapt, an approach known as functionalism; his *Principles of Psychology* (1890) defined the field as the science of mental life.[^s4][^s5] Because trained introspection proved subjective and observers rarely agreed, behaviorism (John B. Watson, B. F. Skinner) redefined psychology as the study of observable behavior and dominated experimental work for decades.[^s4] From the 1950s, linguistics, computer science and neuroscience made mental processes respectable again, and the resulting cognitive revolution, named by Ulric Neisser's 1967 textbook, produced the cognitive psychology in which most reasoning research now sits.[^s4]

## How it works

Modern psychology spans subfields such as cognitive, social, developmental, clinical, personality and health psychology, together with cognitive neuroscience.[^s5] What unites them is a method: state a claim about mind or behavior, operationalize it as something measurable, and test it, usually by experiment or careful observation.[^s4]

A workplace example shows the habit of mind. A manager believes that staff who receive a sales target before estimating next quarter's revenue give inflated forecasts. Treated psychologically, the hunch becomes a testable question: randomly assign forecasters to see a high target, a low target or none, then compare their estimates. If the groups differ, the target is acting as an anchor; if they do not, the belief was folk psychology. The critical thinker's job when reading such a result is to ask how the variables were measured, whether the sample resembles the people the claim is about, and whether the effect has been replicated.[^s3]

## Psychology and logic

A long-running question is whether logic is itself a branch of psychology. The view that logical laws are psychological laws, called psychologism, was fought over in German-speaking philosophy from about 1890 to 1914.[^s2] Gottlob Frege argued that psychological logic confuses being true with being taken to be true, and Edmund Husserl argued that if logic were psychological its laws would be vague and only probable, which they are not.[^s2] Wundt's own *Logik* drew charges of psychologism.[^s1] The debate has since been largely absorbed into wider arguments about philosophical naturalism; W. V. O. Quine, for instance, called in the 1960s for epistemology to return to psychology.[^s2] For practical reasoning the settlement is a division of labor: logic supplies the standard, psychology explains the gap between the standard and actual performance.

## Evidence and limitations

Psychology's self-criticism is itself a lesson in evaluating evidence. The Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 studies published in 2008 in three leading journals: 97% of the originals had reported significant results, but only 36% of the replications did, and mean effect sizes fell by about half.[^s3] Replication rates were about 25% for social psychology and 50% for cognitive psychology by the significance criterion.[^s3] The authors cautioned that a failed replication does not prove an original was a false positive, that a successful one does not confirm the theory behind it, and that publication and reporting biases probably inflated many original effects.[^s3] The practical upshot is to treat a single striking study as a hypothesis, and to prefer meta-analyses and large multi-lab replications.

## Connections

Psychology was **introduced by** Wilhelm Wundt in the sense that his Leipzig laboratory made it an experimental discipline,[^s1] and it was **influenced by** philosophy, from which it inherited its first questions.[^s5] It **contrasts with** logic: the psychologism debate fixed the distinction between how people think and what follows from what.[^s2] Behaviorism, functionalism and structuralism are its main historical schools, each defined by a different view of what psychology may legitimately measure.[^s4] Its reliability is **challenged by** the 2015 Reproducibility Project, the centrepiece of the replication crisis.[^s3] Its subfields cognitive psychology and social psychology, and the psychology of reasoning and judgment and decision making, are where most of this graph's biases and heuristics were discovered.[^s5]
