# Dual-process theory

Dual-process theory is the idea that people reach judgments in two broadly different ways. **Type 1** processes are fast and autonomous: they run whenever triggered, make few demands on working memory and deliver a default answer. **Type 2** processes are slower and reflective. They load heavily on working memory and support hypothetical thinking, the ability to hold an imagined possibility apart from what one currently believes.[^s1] The popular labels "System 1" and "System 2" come from this tradition. Evans and Stanovich, two of its leading theorists, warn that "dual systems" is a misleading phrase, because the fast side is really a collection of many subsystems.[^s1]

## Origin

Two-process accounts grew up in parallel in research on reasoning, decision making and social cognition. By 2009 Keren and Schul could describe them as having become popular over the previous two decades.[^s2] The many versions used many labels: intuitive versus analytic, associative versus rule-based, heuristic versus systematic. In their 2013 review, Evans and Stanovich concluded that most of those paired adjectives are typical correlates, not defining features. They kept only autonomy for Type 1 and working-memory-based decoupling for Type 2 as defining.[^s1] Frederick's 2005 **Cognitive Reflection Test** gave the theory its best-known measure. The test's three short items each invite a quick wrong answer, so it gauges the ability or disposition to check the first answer that comes to mind.[^s5]

## How it works

In the standard **default-interventionist** picture, Type 1 processing proposes an answer and Type 2 steps in only when difficulty, novelty and motivation call working memory into play.[^s1] The bat-and-ball item shows the pattern. A bat and ball cost $1.10 together, and the bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. Most people's first answer for the ball is 10 cents; the correct answer is 5 cents. The classic reading is that getting it right takes slow deliberation that overrides the intuitive answer.[^s4]

*Workplace example.* A finance analyst reviewing a vendor quote sees that a support plan "adds only 10% per year" and approves it at a glance. That quick judgment is the Type 1 default. A colleague pauses, writes out the five-year total and notices that the uplift compounds on the previous year's price. That deliberate check is the Type 2 intervention. On the theory's account, the error was not ignorance of compounding. It was a fast default that no one paused to test.[^s1][^s4]

## How to apply it

1. Treat a confident first answer to a numerical or logical question as a hypothesis, especially when it arrived instantly. CRT-style items show how often the first answer is wrong.[^s5]
2. Create the conditions that trigger Type 2 checking: time, a clear signal that the task is tricky, and a reason to care about getting it right.[^s1]
3. Do not assume that slow means correct. Evans and Stanovich stress that Type 1 answers are not always non-normative and Type 2 answers are not always right.[^s1]

## Evidence

Supporters point to experimental dissociations. Time pressure and working-memory load increase belief-biased reasoning, which is the pattern expected if the logical response depends on Type 2 resources. They also cite neuroimaging and individual differences: higher cognitive ability goes with more normative answers on hard heuristics-and-biases tasks.[^s1] Frederick reported that CRT scores predict time and risk preferences that matter to theories of choice.[^s5]

The framework itself remains disputed. Keren and Schul (2009) judged two-system models to be conceptually unclear and weakly tested, and concluded that they offered little scientific advance.[^s2] Kruglanski and Gigerenzer (2011) argued that intuitive and deliberate judgments follow the same rule-based principles. On their account, speed, effort and resource demands vary continuously and do not line up into two clusters.[^s3] De Neys (2023) reviewed two-response and conflict-detection studies. People who end with the correct answer often gave it already under time pressure, and people who give the biased answer still show sensitivity to the conflict. He proposed a revised model in which competing intuitions and uncertainty monitoring decide when deliberation starts.[^s4] Because the architecture is still in dispute, this article rates the evidence as contested.

## Connections

- **Studied in** cognitive psychology, where Evans and Stanovich defend it against five standard criticisms.[^s1]
- **Challenged by** Keren and Schul's 2009 critique of two-system theories, by Gerd Gigerenzer's unimodel argument with Kruglanski, and by De Neys's 2023 revision of the exclusive, serial view.[^s2][^s3][^s4]
- **Related to** the heuristics-and-biases program and attribute substitution, which use the fast-versus-slow contrast to explain biases. It is also related to belief bias, its key experimental dissociation, and to the Cognitive Reflection Test.[^s1][^s5] Fast-and-frugal heuristics belong to the rival single-principle view.[^s3] Deliberate reflection and debiasing are practical attempts to trigger Type 2 checking.[^s1]
