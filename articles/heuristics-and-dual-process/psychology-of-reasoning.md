# Psychology of reasoning

The psychology of reasoning is the experimental study of how people actually reason: how they draw conclusions from premises, check whether a rule holds, judge whether an argument is any good, and reason from uncertain information. Its signature move is to give people a problem with a known "correct" answer under some normative theory, usually classical logic and later probability theory, and then study where and why their answers depart from it.[^s4][^s1] It sits inside cognitive psychology and overlaps with judgment and decision-making research, and it supplies much of the evidence that critical-thinking teaching relies on when it warns about predictable reasoning errors.[^s2]

## Origin

Psychologists ran reasoning experiments from the early days of experimental psychology in the nineteenth century, but for much of the twentieth century the dominant assumption was that people carry a tacit "mental logic" of formal inference rules, so that errors were mostly slips of interpretation.[^s3] The field took its modern shape in the 1960s and 1970s. Peter Wason's selection task, published in 1966, showed that most people fail an abstract test of a conditional rule yet do much better when the same logic is wrapped in familiar content, which was hard to square with a content-free mental logic.[^s3] By the early 1990s the task had become, in one oft-repeated description, the most intensively researched single problem in the field's history, and from the 1970s the heuristics-and-biases findings of Kahneman and Tversky widened attention from deduction to probability judgment.[^s2]

Jonathan Evans later called this long phase the "deduction paradigm": give untrained participants premises, instruct them to assume the premises are true, and score their conclusions against logic. By his 2002 assessment the paradigm had run for about forty years and had documented both widespread logical errors and strong effects of problem content and context.[^s4]

## How it works

Researchers work with a small set of standard tasks: categorical syllogisms, conditional inferences (modus ponens, modus tollens and their invalid look-alikes), the selection task, and probability problems such as the conjunction and base-rate problems.[^s2][^s3] Theories are then judged by whether they predict the pattern of answers, not just the error rate.

A workplace illustration: a compliance analyst is told, "If an expense claim is over the limit, it must have a manager's signature," and shown four claims (over limit, under limit, signed, unsigned). Which must she inspect? Logically, the over-limit claim and the unsigned claim. Abstract versions of this problem trip most people up, yet framed as checking for rule-breakers the success rate rises sharply, which is exactly the sort of content effect the field tries to explain.[^s2][^s3]

Three families of explanation have dominated:

- **Mental logic** accounts propose formal inference rules applied to the logical form of sentences.[^s3]
- **Mental model theory**, developed by Philip Johnson-Laird, holds that people imagine the possibilities consistent with the premises and read conclusions off those models; it predicts that problems needing more models are harder and that representing only what is true produces systematic "illusory" inferences.[^s3]
- **Dual-process** accounts split fast, intuitive processing from slower, rule-based processing, and are used to explain why people reason well on some tasks and poorly on others.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

Since roughly the 1990s a "new paradigm" has argued that logic is the wrong yardstick for most everyday reasoning. Mike Oaksford and Nick Chater reanalysed the selection task, conditionals and syllogisms as probabilistic inference and argued that many apparent errors are sensible under uncertainty; their 2020 review adds that some arguments classed as fallacies can be rationally persuasive.[^s5][^s1] Evans similarly concluded that few researchers now treat logic as the right norm, and warned that experimental instructions can turn ordinary pragmatic interpretation into something scored as a "bias".[^s4] Johnson-Laird, by contrast, argues that a purely probabilistic account cannot explain deductive competence in logic, mathematics or puzzles.[^s3]

This dispute matters for practitioners. Results from the field are best read as evidence about how people reason under particular instructions and materials, and claims that people are "irrational" depend on which normative standard is chosen. Samuels and Stich defend a middle position: people reason poorly on many laboratory tasks but well on others.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a branch of cognitive psychology, using its experimental and modelling methods.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Peter Wason**, whose selection task became the field's most studied problem.[^s2][^s3]
- **Contrasts with logic:** logic defines which inferences are valid; this field describes which inferences people make, and has steadily questioned logic as its benchmark.[^s4]
- **Influenced by mental model theory**, one of its leading explanatory frameworks.[^s3]
- **Influenced by Bayesian reasoning**, the basis of the probabilistic "new paradigm".[^s1][^s5]
- **Influenced by dual-process theory**, used to explain variation in performance across tasks and people.[^s2]
- **Related** to belief bias and the heuristics-and-biases program, two of its best-known bodies of findings, and to reasoning as the activity it studies.[^s2][^s4]
