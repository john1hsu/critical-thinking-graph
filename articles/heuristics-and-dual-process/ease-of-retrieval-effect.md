# Ease-of-retrieval effect

The ease-of-retrieval effect is the claim that when people judge how often something happens, how likely it is or what they themselves are like, they use not only *what* comes to mind but *how easily* it comes. In the classic demonstration, people who listed 12 examples of their own assertive behaviour, a hard task, rated themselves as less assertive than people who listed only 6.[^s1] Recalling more evidence produced a weaker conclusion, because the struggle to find it was read as a sign that such evidence is scarce.[^s1]

## Origin

Norbert Schwarz, Herbert Bless, Fritz Strack and colleagues introduced the idea in a 1991 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper subtitled "another look at the availability heuristic".[^s1] Tversky and Kahneman's availability heuristic had left open whether judgments rest on the amount of material recalled or on the felt ease of recalling it, since the two usually go together. Schwarz's team pulled them apart by asking for few or many examples.[^s1] They also showed that recalling 12 *unassertive* behaviours left people feeling more assertive than recalling 12 assertive ones, and that the effect vanished when a misattribution manipulation gave people another explanation for the difficulty.[^s1] Schwarz later folded the finding into a broader account in which metacognitive feelings, such as ease of recall or fluency of processing, serve as information alongside declarative content.[^s5]

## How it works

The proposed mechanism is an inference from experience. If examples come easily, people take that as evidence there are many of them and rely on the content; if examples come slowly, they infer the opposite.[^s5] What they conclude depends on a naive theory of memory, so the same feeling can be read differently when it is attributed to something else, such as background music or one's own lack of expertise.[^s1][^s5]

A workplace example: a team lead is asked to write ten reasons why a long-standing supplier is reliable before a contract renewal. After the first three, the reasons come slowly. If the hard list leaves her less confident in the supplier than a colleague who wrote three easy reasons, she has used the difficulty of the task, not the supplier's record, as evidence.[^s1][^s5] The same logic can make debiasing backfire. Schwarz and colleagues reviewed studies in which listing a few reasons why an outcome could have turned out otherwise reduced hindsight bias, but listing many reasons increased it, because generating many alternatives felt hard.[^s5]

## How to apply the finding

Treat the feeling of ease as a weak cue, not as data. When a judgment matters, count or look up cases instead of relying on how readily they come to mind, and ask whether a task felt hard because evidence is scarce or simply because it asked for many items.[^s5] When asking people to consider the opposite, ask for a few alternatives that come easily rather than a long list.[^s5] Reviewers of surveys and focus groups should also notice that the number of examples requested can itself shape the answers.[^s2]

## Evidence

A 2018 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis by Weingarten and Hutchinson pooled 582 effect sizes from 263 studies. For the standard few-versus-many paradigm it found a medium correlation of about .25, which shrank by roughly a quarter to a third after correcting for publication bias but stayed above zero.[^s2] Subjective ease carried only about a third to a half of the effect, so the authors concluded that ease is a real but partial mediator.[^s2] Misattribution weakened or reversed the effect, and strong prior attitudes and deeper processing motivation reduced it.[^s2]

Direct replications of the flagship study have been disappointing. In a 2019 national probability sample, recalling 12 examples felt harder than recalling 6, but the predicted reversal in self-rated assertiveness did not appear; it was the only one of seven canonical effects in that project that failed.[^s3] In 2021, Groncki, Beaudry and Sauer found that ease manipulations changed perceived difficulty but did not affect eyewitness confidence, and their replication of the 1991 assertiveness study also failed.[^s4] Schimmack's reanalysis of the meta-analytic literature argues that small studies and selective reporting inflate the pooled estimate and that some supportive work comes from a researcher with retracted papers; this is one critic's reanalysis, not a settled verdict.[^s6] The fairest summary is that people's ease-of-recall feelings do shift in the expected direction, while how reliably that feeling changes judgments remains disputed.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Norbert Schwarz** and colleagues in their 1991 assertiveness studies, which also **demonstrate** it.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Weingarten and Hutchinson's 2018 meta-analysis, which found a medium effect only partly mediated by ease.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** the 2019 national-sample replications and by Groncki and colleagues' 2021 studies, neither of which reproduced the judgment reversal.[^s3][^s4]
- **Explained by** feelings-as-information theory, in which metacognitive experiences inform judgment through people's naive theories of mental processes.[^s5]
- **Studied in** social psychology, where most of the experiments and the meta-analysis originate.[^s2]
