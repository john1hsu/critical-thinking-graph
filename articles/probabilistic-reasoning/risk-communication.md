# Risk communication

Risk communication is both a practice and a research field: it asks how to tell people about hazards, benefits and uncertainties so that they can make sound choices, and it tests which messages actually work. The FDA's 2011 evidence-based guide defines its aim as giving people the information they need for good decisions, and separates it from public relations by its commitment to accuracy over spin and its duty to cover benefits as well as risks.[^s2] For critical thinkers it is where probabilistic reasoning meets the public: the same numbers can inform or mislead depending on how they are framed.[^s3]

## Origin

The field grew out of 1970s and 1980s disputes over technological hazards such as nuclear power and chemicals, when risk analysts found that publishing technical estimates did not settle public controversy.[^s1] Writing in *Risk Analysis* in 1995, decision scientist Baruch Fischhoff summarised two decades of practice as a sequence of stages, each built on a belief that proved insufficient: that getting the numbers right is enough, that releasing them will persuade, that explaining them will end disputes, that comparing them with already-accepted risks will settle matters, that showing a good risk-benefit trade-off will win acceptance, that treating the public courteously will suffice, and finally that making the public a partner will solve the problem.[^s1] His point was that later stages add to rather than replace earlier skills, and that the realistic goal is fewer but better-informed conflicts rather than none.[^s1]

From the 1990s the field turned more quantitative and medical, as psychologists such as Gerd Gigerenzer studied how doctors and patients misread health statistics and argued that much of the confusion comes from non-transparent formats rather than from an inability to reason.[^s3] Statistician David Spiegelhalter's 2017 review in the *Annual Review of Statistics and Its Application* surveyed practice across health, weather forecasting, natural hazards, climate change, security and catastrophic risk.[^s5]

## How it works

Risk communication starts from a stated goal. In the FDA guide, Noel Brewer separates three aims (sharing information, changing beliefs, changing behaviour) and warns that messages alone are often weak tools for changing behaviour.[^s2] Fischhoff's chapter of the guide gives three tests for whether a message is adequate: it contains the facts users need for their decision, they can find those facts, and they can understand them.[^s2] Julie Downs's chapter adds that communicators are rarely members of their audience, so content should be shaped by audience research and then evaluated, ideally in randomized trials, rather than trusted on intuition.[^s2]

Number format matters most. Gigerenzer and colleagues recommend absolute risks over relative risks, natural frequencies over conditional probabilities, and mortality rates over survival rates when judging screening.[^s3] Their best-known cautionary case is the 1995 UK warning that newer contraceptive pills doubled the risk of blood clots. The "100% increase" was, in absolute terms, about one extra case per 7,000 women, yet the scare was followed by an estimated 13,000 additional abortions in England and Wales the following year.[^s3]

A workplace example: a safety manager reports that a new procedure "cuts injuries by 50%". Staff hear a large effect. Told instead that injuries fell from 4 to 2 per 1,000 workers a year, they can weigh the benefit against the procedure's cost and inconvenience. Both statements are true; only the second lets the audience judge the size of the change.[^s3]

## How to apply it

1. Decide whether you are informing, changing beliefs or changing behaviour, and measure success against that aim.[^s2]
2. Find out what decision the audience faces and give them the facts that matter for it, not everything the analysts know.[^s2]
3. Give absolute risks with a stated reference class and time frame ("2 in 1,000 over 10 years"), and if you give a relative risk, pair it with the absolute one.[^s3][^s4]
4. Use natural frequencies for test results and other conditional information, and use the same denominator throughout.[^s3]
5. Say how uncertain the estimate is and why, and match words, numbers and graphics to the audience.[^s5]
6. Test the message with members of the audience before release, and check afterwards whether it worked.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

The core format effects are well supported. A 2011 Cochrane systematic review (Akl and colleagues; 35 studies, 83 comparisons) found that natural frequencies were understood better than probabilities, and that relative risk reductions were perceived as larger and were more persuasive than the same effect given as an absolute risk reduction or number needed to treat.[^s4] Gigerenzer's group showed that brief training in natural frequencies raised the share of gynaecologists who correctly interpreted a positive mammogram to 87%.[^s3]

The limits are just as important. The Cochrane authors rated the evidence moderate, noted that no included study measured actual behaviour and none involved policy makers, and said it is uncertain whether any format leads people to choices consistent with their own values.[^s4] Brewer cautions that even accurate, well-tested messages often change behaviour less than policy changes do.[^s2] Spiegelhalter's review concludes that there is no single right format: communicators should take expert advice, be clear about objectives and work closely with their audiences.[^s5] Fischhoff's history is also a reminder that public disagreement about risk is often about values and trust, not misunderstanding, so better numbers alone will not end it.[^s1]

## Connections

Risk communication's agenda was shaped by **Baruch Fischhoff**, whose 1995 stage model became a standard account of how the practice matured, and who co-edited the FDA guide.[^s1][^s2] **Gerd Gigerenzer**'s work on transparent formats, including **natural frequencies**, pushed the field to treat statistical confusion as a design problem.[^s3] The field draws on **judgment and decision making** research on how people perceive risk and choose under uncertainty, and the FDA guide explicitly sets it apart from **public relations**: the aim is informed choice, not a favourable impression.[^s2] Logically equivalent relative and absolute formats yield different perceived benefit and persuasiveness, a pattern akin to the **framing effect**.[^s4] Base-rate-free statements invite **base-rate neglect**, which is why the field favours stating reference classes.[^s3]
