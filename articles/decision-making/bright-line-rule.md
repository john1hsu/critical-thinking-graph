# Bright-line rule

A bright-line rule settles a question with a sharp criterion fixed before anyone acts, such as an age, a dollar amount or a deadline, instead of an open standard like "reasonable" that gets its content only when someone later judges a case.[^s3] In critical thinking it matters mainly as an answer to the slippery slope argument: if a clear, defensible line can be drawn between the step being proposed and the feared outcome, the slide has somewhere to stop.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea comes from law, where scholars have long debated whether a legal command should be a rule set in advance or a standard applied after the fact.[^s3] Louis Kaplow's 1992 economic analysis framed the choice as one of timing: rules cost more to write but are cheaper for people to apply to their own plans, while standards are cheap to write but costly to interpret and adjudicate.[^s3] James G. Wilson argued in 1995 that the choice is not binary, because most doctrine sits somewhere on a continuum between bright lines and open balancing tests, with hybrids such as escape hatches and narrow exceptions.[^s4] In argumentation theory, Douglas Walton's 2015 analysis of the basic slippery slope scheme placed the bright line at the centre of the reply to such arguments.[^s1]

## How it works

Many slippery slopes run through a gray zone: a sequence of small steps where no single step looks decisive, so there is no obvious place to say "this far and no further".[^s1] Walton treats the question of whether a bright line can be placed in that gray zone as one of the most important critical questions to ask of any slippery slope.[^s1] Experimental work by Corner, Hahn and Oaksford (2006) shows why the gray zone is dangerous: with 60 Cardiff undergraduates, shifting a single borderline example changed both whether people extended a category to a new case and how convincing they found a slippery slope argument about it.[^s5] A sharp criterion stops that drift, because the next case is compared with the rule rather than with the last borderline decision.[^s3]

Workplace example: a manager hears that letting one team work from home on Fridays will snowball until nobody comes in. Rather than arguing about each request as it arrives, the firm writes a policy: at most two remote days a week, applied to every team. The fear rested on each concession becoming the benchmark for the next; a fixed, published cap removes that benchmark.[^s1] A critic of the slope argument does not even have to name the exact number up front, only show that a workable line can be supplied.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. Locate the gray zone: which steps in the feared chain are judged case by case?[^s1]
2. Propose a criterion that is easy to check and announced in advance, such as a number, a date or a named category.[^s3]
3. Check the price: a sharp rule will sometimes catch cases its purpose would spare and miss cases it should cover, the over- and under-inclusiveness that standards avoid.[^s3]
4. Ask whether the line is defensible on its own terms, not merely possible; consider a hybrid with a narrow escape hatch if the costs of rigidity are high.[^s4][^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

The rationale is normative, but its premise, that vague category boundaries drift under the pull of nearby examples, has experimental support.[^s5] Its limits are real. Eugene Volokh's 2003 study of legal slopes argues that the mere fact that a line can logically be drawn does not settle the matter; what counts is whether later decision-makers will actually hold it.[^s2] He notes that lines seen as arbitrary carry less weight with future courts than the principle behind them, and that even explicit limits on a ruling have later been read more broadly.[^s2] Wilson adds that exceptions tend to be stretched by those who benefit from them, so a bright line may erode in practice.[^s4] A bright line is therefore a strong reply to an unsupported slope, not a guarantee against a real one.[^s2]

## Connections

A bright-line rule counteracts the **slippery slope** fallacy by answering its key critical question about where the slide can stop.[^s1] It **contrasts with** the open **balancing test**, the other end of the doctrinal continuum Wilson describes.[^s4] The rules-versus-standards trade-off behind it is **studied in jurisprudence**, notably in Kaplow's economic analysis,[^s3] and it is **applied in legal reasoning**, where Volokh examines how judicial lines hold or give way.[^s2] It is related to **vagueness** and the **sorites paradox**, the source of the gray zone, and to **burden of proof**, since the slope's proponent must show why the line would fail.[^s1]
