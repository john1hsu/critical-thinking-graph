# Intelligence analysis

Intelligence analysis is the work of reading fragmentary, ambiguous and sometimes deliberately misleading information and turning it into judgments a decision maker can use: what is happening, why, and what is likely to happen next.[^s1] Dhami, Mandel, Mellers and Tetlock describe its core functions as forecasting significant events, characterising the uncertainty around those forecasts, and communicating probabilistic judgments to the people who must act on them.[^s4] Although it grew up inside government, the same problem faces a competitor-intelligence team, a corporate security unit or a risk desk, which is why the field has become a laboratory for applied critical thinking.

## Origin

As a self-aware discipline, intelligence analysis dates from the Second World War and the early Cold War. Sherman Kent, a Yale historian who served in the Office of Strategic Services and later chaired the CIA's Board of National Estimates from 1950 to 1967, is credited with making the analyst a respected role grounded in reason and the methods of scholarship.[^s1] In an essay for the CIA's in-house journal *Studies in Intelligence*, Kent argued that intelligence had become a profession with its own methods and vocabulary but lacked a literature: a cumulative body of writing in which practitioners would define terms, debate first principles and preserve hard-won knowledge rather than keeping it in their heads.[^s2] He gave the word "capability" as an example of a key term used in three different senses.[^s2]

A second turn came from psychology. In *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis* (1999), the CIA veteran Richards Heuer argued that the mind's built-in limits, not a lack of information, cause many analytic failures; that mind-sets are unavoidable simplifications that shape perception and resist change; and that merely knowing about cognitive biases does little to correct them.[^s1] His remedy was structure: tools such as analysis of competing hypotheses, which force analysts to test several explanations against the evidence instead of accepting the first plausible story.[^s1] Jack Davis's introduction to the book contrasts this with Kent, who fought bureaucratic and ideological bias but wrote little about overcoming the limits of cognition.[^s1]

## How it works

Consider an analyst at a manufacturing firm asked whether a key overseas supplier will miss deliveries next quarter. Reports are mixed: shipping data look normal, a local news item mentions labour unrest, and the supplier's account manager insists all is well. A disciplined analysis lists the competing explanations (routine noise, a real disruption, the supplier masking problems), asks which evidence actually discriminates between them, states the key assumptions, and ends with a probability ("about 30% that deliveries slip by more than two weeks") rather than a vague phrase. Dhami and colleagues argue that verbal expressions such as "possible" or "likely" communicate uncertainty poorly and recommend sharper verbal standards or numerical probabilities where feasible.[^s4]

## Evidence

The field's own performance has been measured. Mandel and Barnes scored 1,514 strategic forecasts made between 2005 and 2011 by analysts in a Canadian government assessment unit; discrimination and calibration were both good, and the main error was underconfidence, especially on harder and more important questions.[^s3] Statistically recalibrating the forecasts to be more extreme reduced that error, though the authors warn that any such correction must be validated on an organisation's own data.[^s3] Dhami and colleagues therefore recommend that intelligence organisations track forecasting accuracy routinely and draw on decision research for training, team design and aggregation.[^s4]

The evidence for popular structured techniques is thinner. In a 2018 experiment with 50 UK intelligence analysts, those trained in and required to use analysis of competing hypotheses were no more accurate than controls, and their probability judgments were somewhat less coherent; mathematical recalibration and averaging across analysts improved accuracy far more.[^s5] The authors caution that their task was simpler than real intelligence problems and the sample small, so the result is a warning rather than a verdict.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Influenced by Sherman Kent.** Kent shaped the professional ideal of the analyst and called for a cumulative literature of the craft.[^s1][^s2]
- **Influenced by Richards Heuer and cognitive psychology.** Heuer brought research on perception, memory and bias into analytic training and grounded structured techniques in it.[^s1]
- **Studied in judgment and decision making.** Decision scientists treat analytic judgments as forecasts that can be scored, calibrated and improved.[^s4][^s3]
- **Related techniques.** Analysis of competing hypotheses, key assumptions checks and devil's advocacy are structured tools developed for, or adopted by, analysts; their effectiveness is still being tested.[^s1][^s5]
