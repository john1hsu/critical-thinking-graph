# Judgment and decision making

Judgment and decision making (JDM), long called behavioral decision theory, is the field that asks how people actually estimate, predict and choose. It also asks how their judgments and choices compare with what formal theories say they should do. Mellers, Schwartz and Cooke's 1998 *Annual Review* chapter describes much of the field as the study of behavioral departures from rational choice theory. That theory assumes there is one correct answer, that beliefs and preferences hang together, and that subject and experimenter agree on what counts as correct.[^s1] The field's characteristic move is to pair a normative model with careful experiments. The gap between the two then serves as data about the mind and as a target for better decisions.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Most histories start with Ward Edwards. His 1954 *Psychological Bulletin* review, "The theory of decision making", introduced psychologists to the economic theory of risky and riskless choice, utility and games. Paul Slovic later called that literature large and heavily mathematical.[^s2] Edwards's 1961 *Annual Review of Psychology* chapter was the first under the heading "behavioral decision theory". According to Slovic, it set off a rapid growth of theories and controlled experiments.[^s2] INFORMS credits Edwards with establishing behavioral decision research. In 1962 he founded the Bayesian Research Conference, and in 1968 he set out a Bayesian paradigm for studying judgment that psychologists including Daniel Kahneman took up.[^s6] His students Slovic and Sarah Lichtenstein carried the program forward. Their 1977 review with Baruch Fischhoff surveyed more than 1,000 publications.[^s2]

The field also took institutional form. According to James Shanteau's history, about 31 researchers met in St. Louis in November 1980, right after the Psychonomic Society meeting. The meeting was first billed as a Midwestern judgment-decision research meeting, and Lichtenstein suggested the short name "JDM". The meetings became annual and grew into the Society for Judgment and Decision Making, which still meets alongside Psychonomics.[^s5]

## How it works

JDM research usually works with three kinds of model. Normative models set the standard, such as Bayesian probability or expected utility. Descriptive models say what people actually do. Prescriptive models are practical designs, from everyday rules of thumb to formal decision analysis, that help real people with real limits come closer to the standard.[^s4] Jonathan Baron argues that a systematic departure from the normative model counts as a bias. He adds that calling it an error is of little use unless a prescriptive remedy exists.[^s4]

Einhorn and Hogarth's 1981 review separated *judgment* (evaluating or predicting) from *choice* (committing to an option). They argued that judgment usually feeds choice but is neither necessary nor sufficient for it, since people sometimes choose against their own better judgment.[^s3] By the late 1990s the field's topics included risk perception and attitudes, emotion and regret, framing and other task effects, rule-based choice, overconfidence and calibration, and models of risky choice such as cumulative prospect theory.[^s1]

*Workplace example.* An HR team sees that few employees join the pension plan. A JDM-style analysis starts with the normative benchmark: most employees would be better off enrolled. It then documents behavior: enrollment depends heavily on the default option. Finally it tests a prescriptive fix, such as switching the default. Mellers and colleagues describe a well-known case of this pattern from car insurance. Only about 20% of New Jersey drivers bought the costlier right to sue when the cheaper option was the default, against about 75% in Pennsylvania, where the costlier option was the default.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

The field's methods are mostly laboratory gambles, verbal scenarios and calibration tests, supplemented by field data such as the insurance comparison.[^s1] Its central framing has been contested from the start. Einhorn and Hogarth warned that optimal models depend on assumptions about the environment and the time horizon. They added that rival normative systems exist, so an apparent bias may reflect the wrong benchmark rather than a flawed mind.[^s3] Mellers and colleagues list later versions of the same worry. Gigerenzer argued that good judgment is domain-specific and ecologically adaptive. Hammond stressed real-world accuracy alongside internal coherence. Other critics argued that the Asian disease framing problem is underspecified.[^s1]

## Connections

JDM was **introduced by** Ward Edwards, whose 1954 review is widely treated as its founding text (**introduced in** *The theory of decision making*).[^s6][^s2] It is **influenced by** expected utility and rational choice theory, which supply many of its normative benchmarks.[^s1] It is **challenged by** Gerd Gigerenzer's ecological view of good judgment.[^s1] The heuristics and biases program, prospect theory and behavioral economics grew within or alongside it. Decision analysis is its prescriptive arm, and bounded rationality is a rival account of what rationality should mean.[^s1][^s2][^s3]
