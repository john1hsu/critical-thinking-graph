# Group polarization

Group polarization is what happens when people who already lean the same way talk an issue over: the group's view after discussion tends to be more extreme, in the direction of that initial lean, than the average of what its members thought beforehand.[^s1] Cass Sunstein summarised it as the tendency of deliberation to move a group and its members toward a more extreme point in the direction their pre-deliberation views already pointed.[^s4] It is a reason why a meeting of like-minded people can come out more confident and more one-sided than any of them walked in, without anyone having been pressured.[^s3]

## Origin

The finding began as the "risky shift." In a 1961 master's thesis, James Stoner had participants advise fictional characters facing dilemmas such as whether to take a riskier job; after discussing the dilemmas in groups, they recommended bolder options than they had chosen alone.[^s1][^s4] Wallach, Kogan and Bem (1964) followed with a diffusion-of-responsibility account, but later studies found shifts toward caution on some items as well.[^s1][^s3] In 1969 Moscovici and Zavalloni showed discussion could intensify attitudes, not just risk choices, and in 1972 Myers and Arenson argued that the general pattern was polarization toward whichever side a group started on.[^s2][^s1] Reviews by Myers and Lamm (1976) and Isenberg (1986) fixed "group polarization" as the standard name.[^s1]

## How it works

The best single predictor of the direction of the shift is the group's pre-discussion median: if members lean toward risk, the group grows riskier; if they lean toward caution, it grows more cautious.[^s3] On a 0-to-8 scale, Hastie and Sunstein report, a group whose median starts at six typically ends near seven, and one starting at three ends near two.[^s3]

Three mechanisms are usually offered. **Persuasive arguments:** a group that leans one way hears mostly arguments for that side, including some that individual members had not thought of, so the pool of reasons is skewed.[^s2][^s3] **Social comparison:** people want to be seen favourably and slightly ahead of the group on its valued side, so once they learn where others stand they move further out; merely learning others' positions, with no discussion, can produce a shift.[^s2][^s1] **Corroboration and repetition:** hearing agreement raises confidence, and confident people take more extreme positions; Brauer, Judd and Gliner (1995) found that simply restating one's own view more often in discussion made attitudes more extreme, especially when members picked up and repeated one another's arguments.[^s2][^s3] Shared group identity strengthens the effect, while arguments from an out-group carry little weight.[^s3][^s4]

*Workplace example.* Five sales managers, each mildly optimistic about entering a new regional market, meet to set a first-year target. Each mentions a different favourable signal (a competitor's exit, a promising pilot client, a strong hiring pool), and nobody raises the risks because nobody privately weighted them much. Each manager hears four new reasons for optimism and nothing against it, and by the end the group signs off on a target well above any figure a single manager would have proposed. No one was bullied; the argument pool was one-sided, and agreement felt like confirmation.[^s3]

## Evidence

Group polarization is among the best-documented effects in group research. By 1995 more than 200 studies in laboratory and field settings had reported it, provided members began with the same inclination.[^s2] Hastie and Sunstein describe hundreds of studies across more than a dozen countries.[^s3] It has been found for attitudes (racial attitudes, feminism, political issues), for factual estimates and for numerical judgments such as mock-jury punishment ratings, where deliberation pushed awards beyond the median member's starting preference.[^s4][^s3] In a Colorado experiment by Hastie, Sunstein and Schkade, small groups from liberal Boulder grew more liberal and groups from conservative Colorado Springs more conservative after discussing climate change, affirmative action and civil unions; members' anonymous private views moved too, so the shift was not just public posturing.[^s3]

The effect has clear boundary conditions. Groups evenly split between committed sides, or lacking a shared identity, tend to depolarize.[^s1][^s4] Some groups weigh risks more carefully together; Abrams and colleagues (2006) found drinking groups monitoring each other's risk rather than escalating.[^s1] Sunstein noted that structured deliberative polls with balanced briefing materials generally did not polarize, and that much of the evidence comes from laboratory tasks.[^s4] Meeker (2007) attributes some failures to replicate to weak operationalisation.[^s1] There is debate about the relative weight of the mechanisms, since both persuasive-argument and social-comparison accounts have substantial support.[^s2] Polarization is not always harmful: a group that already leans toward the truth can become more accurate, but one sharing an error, such as an over-optimistic schedule, will amplify it.[^s3]

## How to counter it

Practical remedies follow from the mechanisms. Collect members' estimates independently before discussion, so the skew of the argument pool is visible; ensure the group includes, or deliberately assigns, people who argue the other side; give balanced briefing material; and keep the leader's own view back until others have spoken.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a pattern within group decision making, where interaction among members shapes the outcome.[^s3]
- **Studied in** social psychology, the field that turned Stoner's risky shift into a general account of group-induced attitude change.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Stoner's 1961 risky-shift study, the first report that group discussion shifted choices beyond members' individual positions.[^s1]
- **Explained by** persuasive arguments theory and social comparison theory, the two classic accounts, and by repeated attitude expression, the intrapersonal account Brauer and colleagues proposed.[^s2]
- **Amplifies** the planning fallacy and other errors that members share, since discussion entrenches a common lean rather than correcting it.[^s3]
- **Related to** groupthink, which stresses pressure toward unanimity in cohesive groups, whereas polarization can occur without any pressure; to the false consensus effect and myside bias; to the straw man, which caricatures the out-group's view; and to devil's advocacy, the nominal group technique, the Delphi method and the wisdom of crowds, which preserve independent judgment. Cass Sunstein extended the finding to juries, courts and online communities.[^s3][^s4]
