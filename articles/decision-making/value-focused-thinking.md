# Value-focused thinking

Value-focused thinking (VFT) is a way of structuring a decision that starts from what the decision maker wants to achieve rather than from the options already in view. The decision maker first writes down their fundamental objectives, then uses those objectives both to invent alternatives and to judge them.[^s1][^s2] Keeney contrasts it with *alternative-focused thinking*, the usual habit of listing the available options and only afterwards asking what criteria should separate them. He calls that habit reactive and backward, since alternatives are only means to the ends that values define.[^s1]

## Origin

The approach was developed by the decision analyst Ralph L. Keeney and set out in his book *Value-Focused Thinking: A Path to Creative Decisionmaking* (Harvard University Press, 1992). In an essay for *MIT Sloan Management Review* he summarised it under the slogan "values first".[^s1] In a 2008 paper Keeney named three main payoffs. VFT gives better objectives for evaluating options, produces alternatives beyond the obvious ones, and helps people spot decision opportunities rather than only reacting to problems imposed on them.[^s2]

## How it works

VFT reverses the usual order of a decision. Instead of asking "which of these options is best?", the decision maker first asks "what am I trying to achieve here, and why does it matter?" Keeney separates *fundamental* objectives, the ends a decision is meant to serve, from *means* objectives, which matter only because they help achieve those ends.[^s1][^s3] Fundamental objectives are then used to evaluate options. Means objectives are a prompt for new options: each one suggests a way of doing better on something that matters.[^s3] Keeney's example is air-pollution regulation. It is usually framed in terms of pollutant concentrations, but asking stakeholders what they care about brings out health effects, visibility and jobs. That wider set of objectives leads to a richer set of policies and a clearer way to compare them.[^s1]

A workplace example: a team lead must pick new project-tracking software. The alternative-focused route is to compare the three tools a colleague mentioned and take the first that fits the budget. With VFT, she first asks the team what they need the tool to achieve: fewer missed hand-offs, less time spent on status meetings, visibility for clients, and low onboarding effort. The "fewer missed hand-offs" objective suggests options that were not on the list at all. These include changing the hand-off process in the existing tool, or adding a shared checklist and keeping the current software. The full list of objectives then becomes the scoring sheet for every option.[^s1][^s4]

## How to apply it

1. **List objectives before options.** Write down everything you care about in the decision, ideally with several stakeholders. Then compare that list with a broader checklist or with colleagues' lists, because people working alone miss many of their own objectives.[^s3][^s5]
2. **Separate ends from means.** For each objective ask "why does this matter?" until you reach the fundamental ones, which become your evaluation criteria.[^s1][^s3]
3. **Create alternatives one objective at a time.** For each objective, ask what option would do especially well on it. Then try combining the best ideas.[^s4][^s5]
4. **Only then evaluate.** Score every option, old and new, against the fundamental objectives.[^s1][^s2]

The main pitfall is cost. VFT takes deliberate effort, and Bayreuth's summary of the 2015 studies stresses that simply giving people more time did not produce the same gains as structured reflection on goals.[^s5] For low-stakes, easily reversed choices a quick good-enough choice is usually wiser. VFT pays off in high-stakes decisions where the best option may not be on the table yet.[^s6]

## Evidence and limitations

The method rests on evidence that people represent their own decisions too narrowly. In three studies with MBA students and a field case at a software firm, Bond, Carlson and Keeney (2008) found that people left out nearly half the objectives they later recognised as relevant when shown a master list. In one study, 71% missed at least one of their own top five objectives. The omitted objectives were rated nearly as important as the ones people had produced.[^s3] Siebert and Keeney (2015) ran five studies on alternatives for important personal decisions. Unaided, people identified fewer than half of their alternatives, and the ones they missed were on average as good as the ones they found. Using objectives to prompt the search reliably increased both the number and the quality of alternatives.[^s4] In the internship study, students first came up with only 37% of the options they later judged relevant. Objectives plus instructions raised the number of alternatives by 53%.[^s5]

Field evidence points the same way. In Nutt's 1999 study of 356 organisational decisions, about half failed. Managers often adopted the first workable idea they came across, and fewer than 20% of decisions developed multiple options. Decisions that began by setting objectives were sustained 70% of the time, compared with 56% for those that started from a ready-made idea.[^s6] The evidence status is **mixed**. The laboratory studies come mostly from Keeney and his collaborators and use student samples and self-rated quality. Nutt's data are correlational. No large independent replication shows that VFT improves real outcomes.[^s3][^s4][^s6]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Ralph Keeney** in his 1992 book *Value-Focused Thinking*, which his *Sloan Management Review* essay summarises.[^s1]
- **Counteracts satisficing** where it goes wrong. Nutt found managers often settled on the first workable idea, whereas starting from objectives and developing several options went with more successful decisions. VFT makes the aspiration level explicit and widens the search before stopping.[^s6]
- **Supported by** Bond, Carlson and Keeney's 2008 objective-generation studies[^s3] and Siebert and Keeney's 2015 alternative-creation studies.[^s4]
- **Applied in decision analysis**, where Keeney presents it as a way to structure problems and modelling, including in military operations research.[^s2]
- **Related to** bounded rationality: Bond and colleagues treat narrow decision frames as an adaptive response to limited processing capacity, which objectives-first prompting is meant to offset.[^s3]
