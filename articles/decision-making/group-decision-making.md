# Group decision making

Group decision making is any process in which several people reach one choice or judgment together: a hiring panel picking a candidate, a jury reaching a verdict, a board approving a budget. Research on small groups shows that pooling people can produce *process gains*, where the group does better than its members would alone, or *process losses*, where it does worse, and that which one happens depends largely on the situation and the procedure the group follows.[^s2] For critical thinking the practical lesson is that "ask the team" is not a method in itself; how the team shares information and combines views decides whether the result is wiser or merely more confident.[^s3]

## Origin

There are two older traditions behind the topic. The formal one goes back to the Marquis de Condorcet, whose 1785 essay on majority decisions proved what is now called the jury theorem: if voters on a yes-or-no question are independent and each is more likely right than wrong, a majority becomes more reliable as the group grows.[^s4] The empirical one is mid-twentieth-century social psychology, which studied brainstorming, group polarization, groupthink and jury deliberation in the laboratory.[^s3] By 2004 Kerr and Tindale could describe decision research as focused on two problems: how members' preferences are combined, and how groups process the information their members hold.[^s2]

## How it works

Groups can gain over individuals because members bring different knowledge, catch one another's mistakes, remember more together and generate ideas no single person had.[^s3] They lose when the process wastes or distorts those resources.[^s3] Several losses are well documented:

- **Shared information bias.** Discussion tends to circle around facts everyone already knows and neglect facts only one member holds.[^s3] In Stasser and Titus's 1985 experiment, four-person groups of students chose among three hypothetical student-government candidates. When every member read the full profiles, 83% of groups picked the strongest candidate; when the favourable facts about that candidate were split among members, only 18% of groups did, and most groups simply went with whichever candidate a plurality had preferred before talking.[^s1] This split-information arrangement is now known as a *hidden profile*.[^s3]
- **Group polarization.** After discussion, members' views often become more extreme in the direction the group already leaned, partly because discussion supplies more arguments on the favoured side.[^s3]
- **Groupthink and production blocking.** Cohesive groups under a directive leader can suppress dissent, and face-to-face brainstorming groups produce fewer ideas than the same number of people working alone, mainly because waiting to speak makes people forget ideas.[^s3]

A workplace example: a five-person panel interviews three candidates for an analyst role. Each interviewer happens to have seen one different, impressive work sample from candidate A, while everyone has seen the same polished résumé from candidate B. If the panel opens by going around the table with gut preferences, B wins on the information everyone shares, and A's scattered strengths never surface. If each interviewer first writes down their evidence privately and the chair asks for anything only one person saw, the group can assemble A's full profile.[^s1]

On the aggregation side, the jury theorem explains why independent votes can be powerful, and why correlated ones are not: when people rely on the same evidence, the same sources or the same opinion leader, their errors line up and adding members adds little reliability.[^s4]

## How to apply it

- Collect individual judgments in writing before discussion, so early speakers do not set the agenda; nominal group procedures do this by having members generate ideas alone and then share them in turn.[^s3]
- Treat unique information as the group's main asset: ask explicitly what each person knows that others may not, and keep discussing after the group feels it has covered everything.[^s3]
- Frame the task as having a correct answer to find rather than a preference to settle, which encourages fuller sharing.[^s3]
- Protect independence when aggregating votes or estimates; competence above chance helps only if members are not all drawing on one source.[^s4]
- Seek out members whose views differ from yours and include diverse members, which lowers the risk of groupthink.[^s3]

## Evidence

Hidden-profile and shared-information effects have been reproduced in follow-up studies, and the bias persisted even when participants were told to review all the facts before stating preferences.[^s3] It weakens under some conditions, for example when members do not know the alternatives or one another's initial preferences, so the overall picture is of real but moderated effects rather than a fixed law.[^s3] Kerr and Tindale's review stresses this contingency: gains and losses are both possible, depending on context and procedure.[^s2] On the formal side, the Stanford entry concludes that the strong "asymptotic infallibility" reading of the jury theorem is untenable for large real groups, though the weaker claim that reliability can increase with size is defensible.[^s4] Many classic studies used students and artificial tasks, and laboratory groups often have more complete information than real teams, so effect sizes outside the lab are uncertain.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Studied in** social psychology, whose research on working groups covers information sharing, polarization, groupthink and juries.[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** Stasser and Titus's 1985 hidden-profile experiment, which showed groups failing to pool unshared information.[^s1]
- **Formalized by** the Condorcet jury theorem, the mathematical model of when majority voting improves accuracy.[^s4]
- **Has part** group polarization, one of the characteristic dynamics of deliberating groups.[^s3]
- Related to groupthink and the wisdom of crowds, which describe a group's failure and success modes; to the Delphi method, the nominal group technique and devil's advocacy, which are procedures for structuring group judgment; and to stasis theory, which helps a group locate where it actually disagrees.[^s3]
