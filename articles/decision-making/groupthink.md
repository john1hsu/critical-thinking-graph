# Groupthink

Groupthink is a failure mode of small, close-knit decision groups: members go along with what they take to be the group's consensus even when they privately doubt it is sound, and the group's problem-solving suffers as a result.[^s3] Irving Janis defined it as a way of thinking in a cohesive in-group in which the striving for unanimity overrides a realistic appraisal of the alternatives.[^s1] The term is now everyday vocabulary, but the scientific model behind it has a much weaker empirical record than its fame suggests.[^s4]

## Origin

The social psychologist Irving Janis set out the theory in *Victims of Groupthink: A Psychological Study of Foreign-Policy Decisions and Fiascoes* (1972), drawing on case studies of US policy failures including Pearl Harbor, the Bay of Pigs invasion and the escalation in Vietnam.[^s3] He contrasted these "fiascos" with decisions where he judged groupthink absent, the Marshall Plan and the Cuban missile crisis, and revised the work in 1982 as *Groupthink*, adding the Watergate cover-up.[^s1][^s4]

## How it works

Janis's model runs as a causal chain. **Antecedents** set the stage: high cohesion, structural faults such as insulation from outside experts and a directive leader without impartial procedures, and situational stress with little hope of a better option than the leader's.[^s1][^s4] These produce a tendency toward concurrence seeking, visible in **eight symptoms** that Janis sorted into three kinds:[^s1]

- *Overestimating the group:* an illusion of invulnerability and an unquestioned belief in the group's own morality.[^s1]
- *Closed-mindedness:* collective rationalization of warnings and stereotyped views of rivals or out-groups.[^s1]
- *Pressure toward uniformity:* self-censorship, an illusion of unanimity, direct pressure on dissenters and self-appointed "mindguards" who screen out unwelcome information.[^s1]

The result is **defective decision making**: an incomplete survey of alternatives and objectives, failure to examine the risks of the preferred option, poor information search and no contingency plan.[^s1][^s3]

*Workplace example.* A five-person product team has shipped three successes under a well-liked director, who opens a planning meeting by saying she is "really excited" about a new subscription tier. One engineer has data showing churn among similar customers but stays quiet because everyone else is nodding; another member mentions a competitor's failed launch and is told that "we're not them". Nobody asks what happens if uptake is half the forecast. Silence is read as agreement, the plan ships without a rollback option, and the doubts surface only after the numbers disappoint. The cues to notice are the leader stating a preference first, warnings being explained away rather than tested, and the absence of any recorded objection.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence

Groupthink is rated *contested*. Reviews agree that historical case studies have often fitted the model, while controlled tests have been few, weak and inconsistent.[^s1] By 1998 only around eleven empirical studies had tested it, partly because the model has many variables and can be read in strict, additive or particularistic ways; neither the strict nor the additive reading has found support.[^s4] Esser's 1998 review found that cohesion, defined as mutual attraction, was not supported as a cause, whereas structural faults such as insulation and directive leadership were generally predictive.[^s1] A 1994 meta-analysis by Mullen and colleagues likewise found no overall effect of cohesiveness on decision quality; cohesive groups decided worse only under directive leadership, as group size grew, or when cohesion meant interpersonal attraction rather than task commitment.[^s2]

The case base itself has been disputed. Kramer's 1998 reanalysis of the Bay of Pigs and Vietnam decisions concluded that cohesion was not a prominent cause and that political considerations explained more of what happened.[^s4][^s5] Fuller and Aldag called groupthink a "compelling myth" and urged researchers to move past it.[^s1] Turner and Pratkanis proposed a social identity maintenance account instead, and Robert Baron argued in 2005 that the evidence had largely failed to support the model's more ambitious predictions, and offered a revised "ubiquity" model in its place.[^s1][^s4] Britannica also notes that not every bad decision stems from groupthink and that concurrence does not always lead to failure.[^s3]

The practical upshot is narrower than the popular version: the parts with the best support, namely leader behaviour and insulation, are also the most practical to fix. Janis's own remedies included a leader who stays impartial at the outset, assigned devil's advocates, independent subgroups, outside experts and a "second-chance" meeting before committing, though these have been tested much less than the symptoms.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a pattern within group decision making, where members' interaction rather than individual judgment drives the error.[^s3]
- **Introduced by** Irving Janis, who coined the theoretical model and its symptom list.[^s1]
- **Introduced in** *Victims of Groupthink* (1972).[^s3]
- **Studied in** social psychology, the field in which Janis developed and others tested it.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** Fuller and Aldag's 1998 critique of the theory as a compelling myth.[^s1]
- **Challenged by** Kramer's 1998 reanalysis of the Bay of Pigs and Vietnam cases.[^s4][^s5]
- **Challenged by** the 1994 Mullen et al. meta-analysis, which found no main effect of cohesion, the model's central antecedent.[^s2]
- **Explained by** (on one account) the social identity maintenance model, which replaces cohesion with the group's need to protect a shared positive image.[^s1]
- **Related to** the premortem, devil's advocacy and authentic dissent, which put Janis's advice to legitimise criticism into structured form, and to actively open-minded thinking, group polarization, overconfidence and the wider structured analytic techniques.[^s1]
