# Devil's advocacy

Devil's advocacy is a deliberate, assigned challenge to a group's settled view. One person or a small team is given the job of building the best case they can for an alternative explanation or against a proposed course of action, so that the consensus is tested before anyone acts on it.[^s1] It differs from casual contrarianism in being a sanctioned role with a defined product: a critique of the mainline judgment's assumptions and evidence.[^s1]

## Origin

The name comes from the Roman Catholic Church. The *promotor fidei* (promoter of the faith), popularly called the devil's advocate, was an official whose task was to examine candidates for beatification or canonization critically and to put every unfavourable fact before the tribunal.[^s5] Pope Sixtus V formally established the office in 1587, and it lost most of its influence after Pope John Paul II reformed canonization procedure in 1983.[^s5]

As a management and analysis technique, devil's advocacy was studied in the 1980s strategic-planning literature, which compared it with dialectical inquiry (a structured debate between a plan and a fully developed counter-plan) and with plain consensus seeking.[^s2] US intelligence analysts later codified it as one of the "contrarian" structured analytic techniques in the CIA's 2009 *Tradecraft Primer*, alongside Team A/Team B, high-impact/low-probability analysis and "what if?" analysis.[^s1]

## How it works

The technique works by separating two jobs that a group normally merges: proposing a view and attacking it. Once someone is officially responsible for the attack, criticism stops being a social risk and becomes a task to do well. The *Tradecraft Primer* describes its main purpose as checking a dominant mindset that builds up when experienced people work an issue for a long time.[^s1] In laboratory studies of strategic decisions, groups that used devil's advocacy or dialectical inquiry produced higher-quality recommendations and assumptions than consensus groups,[^s2] and their recommendations beat the work of the average individual member, suggesting that structured conflict makes better use of what members know.[^s4]

*Workplace example.* A regional retailer's leadership team has agreed to close its three smallest stores, on the view that online sales will absorb their customers. The COO assigns a finance analyst to argue the other side for a week. The analyst lists the plan's key assumptions, finds that the online-migration figure came from a different market, and shows that the small stores handle most click-and-collect returns. The team keeps one store open and pilots the other two closures with a tracked customer-retention target. The core decision survived, but its weakest assumption was caught before it became expensive.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. **State the mainline view.** Write down the current judgment, its key assumptions and the evidence for it.[^s1]
2. **Pick the softest assumptions.** Choose the stated or unstated assumptions most open to challenge.[^s1]
3. **Re-examine the evidence.** Check sources for validity, gaps and possible deception, and collect anything that supports an alternative or contradicts the current line.[^s1]
4. **Present the case.** Report the flawed assumptions and weak evidence; if the flaws are serious, write a separate paper arguing a different conclusion.[^s1]
5. **Label it.** Mark the product clearly as a devil's advocacy exercise and restate the conventional view, so readers do not mistake it for the official position.[^s1]

**When to use it:** on high-stakes questions where an error would be costly, and especially when a firm consensus has formed and has not been questioned for some time.[^s1] **Pitfalls:** consensus groups in the 1986 study were more satisfied with their process and more accepting of the outcome, so devil's advocacy can cost some goodwill.[^s2] A role-played objection may also be easy to dismiss, which is why critics recommend finding or protecting real dissenters where possible (see Evidence).[^s6]

## Evidence

The empirical record is real but limited, mostly from lab tasks with student or manager groups in the 1980s, so the technique is rated *mixed*. Schweiger, Sandberg and Ragan's 1986 *Academy of Management Journal* study found that devil's advocacy and dialectical inquiry both beat consensus on recommendation and assumption quality, with dialectical inquiry producing better assumptions than devil's advocacy.[^s2] A 1990 meta-analysis by Charles Schwenk concluded that devil's advocacy generally outperformed a no-conflict "expert" approach, while neither devil's advocacy nor dialectical inquiry was shown to be better than the other at introducing productive conflict.[^s3]

The main critique comes from the social psychologist Charlan Nemeth, whose research on minority dissent argues that an assigned devil's advocate does not stimulate rethinking the way a sincere dissenter does, because the group knows the objection lacks conviction.[^s6] On this view, devil's advocacy is a useful substitute when no genuine dissent exists, and formats that make advocates build a serious, detailed case come closest to the real thing.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** one of the structured analytic techniques, specifically a contrarian technique, and a form of debiasing aimed at entrenched group mindsets.[^s1]
- **Applied in** intelligence analysis, where the *Tradecraft Primer* recommends it as a final check on an unquestioned consensus.[^s1]
- **Applied in** strategic decision making, where it was tested against consensus and dialectical inquiry.[^s2]
- **Confused with** Team A/Team B: the Primer notes that exercises billed as Team B are sometimes really devil's advocacy, although Team A/Team B pits two established views against each other rather than attacking one.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with** dialectical inquiry, which builds a full counter-plan from opposite assumptions instead of critiquing the existing plan.[^s2]
- **Supported by** Schwenk's 1990 meta-analysis showing an advantage over conflict-free expert approaches.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with** authentic dissent, which Nemeth argues is more effective at prompting genuine reconsideration.[^s6]
- **Related to** the key assumptions check and red-team analysis, fellow techniques in the Primer for loosening a well-developed mindset,[^s1] and to consider-the-opposite and the premortem, which likewise make the case against a current view explicit, and to groupthink, the premature consensus such techniques aim to prevent.
