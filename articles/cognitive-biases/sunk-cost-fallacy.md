# Sunk cost fallacy

The sunk cost fallacy, called the sunk cost effect in psychology, is the tendency to continue an endeavour once money, effort or time has gone into it, even though that past investment cannot be recovered and should not change what is best to do next.[^s1] Standard decision theory says a choice between options should depend only on their future costs and benefits; a cost already paid is the same whichever option is chosen, so it cancels out. People nevertheless let it tip the balance, typically toward persisting with a failing project or using a product they would otherwise skip.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer named and systematically tested the effect in a 1985 paper of ten experiments in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*; later work credits them with the term "sunk cost effect".[^s1][^s3] They described persistence driven by past spending as maladaptive and linked it to a wish not to appear wasteful.[^s1]

## How it works

In their best-known scenario, students imagined having paid $100 for one ski weekend and $50 for another on the same dates, then learning the cheaper trip would be more enjoyable. Only 46% picked the trip they expected to enjoy more; the rest chose the one that had cost more.[^s1] When the same trips were described as free prizes, choices split roughly evenly, which points to the money spent rather than the trips themselves.[^s1] In a field study, theatre-goers randomly sold season tickets at full price attended more plays in the first half of the season than those given a discount, a difference that faded after about six months.[^s1]

A workplace example follows their aircraft-project scenario. Respondents told that a firm had already spent $10 million of its budget on a radar-blank plane mostly said to finish it, while respondents asked whether to start the same plane from scratch mostly said no. Those with the sunk cost also rated the project's chances of success higher.[^s1] The everyday version is the stalled software rollout or the overdue renovation that keeps getting funded because "we've already put so much into it".

## Evidence

A 2015 meta-analysis pooled roughly 100 effect sizes from 45 studies and found a moderate overall effect (standardized mean difference about 0.5). It separated decisions about using something already paid for from decisions about continuing a project. Time since the investment weakened the effect for use decisions but strengthened it for continuation decisions. Older adults showed smaller effects in use decisions, and familiarity with economic decision making did not reduce the bias.[^s2] The authors note that most studies rely on hypothetical scenarios and student samples, which limits how far the results generalise to real organisations.[^s2]

Replications support the core effect but show it is smaller and more variable than early studies suggested. A 2023 registered replication with 821 US participants found sunk cost effects for both money and time, though with a smaller money-versus-time difference than the original study reported, and a lesson on opportunity cost did not reduce reliance on sunk time costs.[^s3] Because effect sizes depend on decision type, sample and materials, this article rates the evidence as mixed rather than robust.[^s2][^s3]

Two explanations are prominent. Arkes and Blumer drew on prospect theory: someone who has incurred a loss sits in a domain where a sure loss is especially aversive, so continuing on a long shot looks more attractive than writing off the investment.[^s1] They also proposed waste avoidance, the reluctance to admit that earlier spending was wasted.[^s1] A 2024 registered replication of Arkes's later work on perceived wastefulness reproduced its effect in two of three scenarios, with the third weakened by a failed manipulation check.[^s4]

## How to spot it

- The main reason offered for continuing is the amount already spent, not the value of what remains.[^s1]
- Estimates of success rise once money has been committed, without any new evidence.[^s1]
- You would decline the same option if offered it fresh today at its remaining cost.[^s1]

## How to counter it

Knowing the economic rule is not enough: in Arkes and Blumer's study, students who had taken economics, or were studying it at the time, showed the effect as much as those who had not, and the meta-analysis likewise found no protective effect of economic familiarity.[^s1][^s2] More promising is changing the question itself. Frame the decision as a fresh choice: given where things stand now, would we start this today for what it will still cost? Compare remaining costs and benefits of each option side by side, and set stopping rules before investing so the decision to quit is made before losses accumulate.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Hal Arkes, whose 1985 paper with Blumer is credited with the term "sunk cost effect".[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** the Arkes and Blumer 1985 experiments, including the ski-trip, theatre-ticket and aircraft-project studies.[^s1]
- **Explained by** prospect theory's account of risk seeking after losses, and by waste avoidance, the second mechanism Arkes and Blumer proposed and later replication work on wastefulness supports.[^s1][^s4]
- **Supported by** Roth, Robbert and Straus's 2015 meta-analysis and by Petrov and colleagues' 2023 registered replication.[^s2][^s3]
- **Related to** escalation of commitment and to reversible and irreversible decisions, since persistence after past investment is a central risk in long, costly projects.[^s1][^s2]
