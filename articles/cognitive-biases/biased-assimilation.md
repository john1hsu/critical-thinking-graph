# Biased assimilation

Biased assimilation is the tendency to rate evidence that agrees with what we already believe as sounder and more persuasive than comparable evidence that disagrees. The asymmetry is in the *evaluation*: the same research design looks rigorous when it delivers a welcome result and flawed when it delivers an unwelcome one.[^s1] Its practical consequence is that people on opposite sides of a question can read one body of mixed evidence and each come away convinced that it favours them.[^s1]

## Origin

The term comes from a 1979 study by Charles Lord, Lee Ross and Mark Lepper at Stanford, published in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. From 151 screened undergraduates they recruited 24 supporters and 24 opponents of capital punishment, all with firm views on whether it deters murder.[^s1] Each read two fictitious studies, one finding a deterrent effect and one finding none, with the research method (a before-and-after comparison within states, or a comparison of neighbouring states) counterbalanced against the result. Each study came first as a short result card, then as a full description with data, criticisms and rebuttals.[^s1] Supporters judged the pro-deterrence study better conducted and more convincing; opponents judged the reverse, even though the designs were swapped across conditions.[^s1] Participants also *reported* that their views had become more extreme, which the authors labelled attitude polarization.[^s1]

## How it works

Lord and colleagues stressed that giving less weight to surprising findings is not by itself irrational: a claim that clashes with well-founded knowledge deserves extra scrutiny. The error is the next step, in which evidence that was already read through the prior belief is then counted as fresh support for that same belief, so the belief becomes close to unfalsifiable.[^s1] Taber and Lodge later split the process into a *prior attitude effect*, where congenial arguments are rated stronger, and a *disconfirmation bias*, where people spend more time and effort counter-arguing uncongenial arguments while accepting congenial ones with little scrutiny.[^s2]

A workplace example: two managers are split over whether remote work lowers productivity. The company runs two internal analyses, one comparing teams before and after going remote and one comparing remote and office teams in the same quarter, and the two point in opposite directions. The manager who favours the office calls the first analysis solid and dismisses the second for ignoring team differences; the manager who favours remote work makes exactly the opposite criticisms. Each walks out of the review more confident than before, although neither analysis changed and the evidence as a whole is a draw.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

The biased-evaluation part has been reproduced with real political arguments. In two experiments with 126 and 136 Stony Brook undergraduates on gun control or affirmative action, Taber and Lodge (2006) found that participants rated attitude-consistent arguments as stronger and spent more effort denigrating opposing ones, even when told to be even-handed. The effects were strongest among people with strong prior attitudes and high political knowledge and were small or absent among those with weak attitudes and little knowledge.[^s2]

The polarization claim is much weaker. Taber and Lodge themselves noted that Lord and colleagues measured polarization by asking people whether they had changed, not by measuring attitudes before and after, and that replications with direct measures often failed.[^s2] Kuhn and Lao (1996) argued that much apparent polarization is people moving from a neutral position to a moderate one, and concluded that genuine polarization is real but uncommon.[^s3] Guess and Coppock's three large survey experiments (a 2016 national sample of 2,122 on gun control, and two online samples on the minimum wage and on capital punishment, the last using materials adapted from the 1979 study) found that supporters and opponents usually shifted in the *same* direction as the evidence; 23 of 24 subgroup estimates had the expected sign, and backlash was the exception.[^s4] The best current reading is that people discount unwelcome evidence, but usually still move a little toward it rather than away.[^s2][^s4]

## How to spot it

- You can list design flaws in the study you dislike but cannot name one in the study you like, though they use similar methods.[^s1]
- You spend far longer rebutting the opposing report than checking the supporting one.[^s2]
- After reviewing evidence that a neutral reader would call mixed, you feel more certain than before.[^s1]
- The issue is one you care about and know a lot about, which is where the effect is largest.[^s2]

## How to counter it

Judge the method before you see the result: agree in advance what a good test of the question would look like, then score each study against that standard with its conclusion hidden or swapped.[^s1] Apply your sharpest criticism symmetrically by asking whether you would accept the flaw you found if the study had come out your way.[^s1][^s2] Track how far your view actually moved by writing it down before and after reviewing evidence, rather than relying on your sense that the evidence "confirmed" it.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by** Lord, Ross and Lepper's 1979 capital-punishment study, the canonical demonstration of opposite evaluations of identical designs.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Lee Ross, with Charles Lord and Mark Lepper, who named biased assimilation in that paper.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Taber and Lodge's 2006 experiments, which found the prior attitude effect and disconfirmation bias with real political arguments.[^s2]
- **Explained by** motivated reasoning: Taber and Lodge frame the pattern as motivated skepticism, driven by the wish to defend existing attitudes.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** Kuhn and Lao's 1996 study, which found genuine polarization from mixed evidence to be infrequent,[^s3] and by Guess and Coppock's survey experiments, which found parallel updating rather than backlash.[^s4]
- **Narrower than** confirmation bias, being its evaluative form, and **related to** myside bias, belief bias and belief perseverance; consider-the-opposite and self-affirmation are remedies already linked to it in this graph.[^s1][^s2]
