# Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is the habit of seeking or interpreting evidence in ways that favour a belief, expectation or hypothesis already in hand. Raymond Nickerson's 1998 review treats it as an umbrella term for several related tendencies, and stresses that it usually describes unwitting selectivity, not the deliberate case-building of a lawyer or debater.[^s1] It appears both when people want a belief to be true and when they have no stake in the answer at all, which is why it is hard to explain by motivation alone.[^s1]

## Origin

Observers noticed the pattern long before psychologists named it. Nickerson cites Francis Bacon's 1620 remark that once the mind adopts an opinion it draws everything else in to support it.[^s1] The experimental study began with Peter Wason's 1960 rule-discovery task. Wason told 29 undergraduates that the triple 2-4-6 fitted a simple rule and asked them to find it by proposing new triples and hearing whether each fitted. The rule was simply "any three increasing numbers", but only 6 of the 29 announced it correctly on their first attempt.[^s2] Most announced narrower rules such as "increasing by two", having proposed only triples that fitted their own guess. Wason, drawing on Karl Popper, concluded that people tend to collect confirming instances instead of trying to eliminate hypotheses.[^s2]

## How it works

Nickerson groups the experimental findings into several forms: considering only one favoured hypothesis; recalling or generating reasons for one's own side and discounting the other side's; testing only cases the hypothesis predicts will come out positive; demanding less evidence to accept a hypothesis than to reject it; seeing what one expects to see, including illusory correlations; and holding on to beliefs after the evidence for them has been discredited.[^s1] Proposed explanations range from failing to ask how likely the evidence would be if an alternative were true, to memory search that stops once it finds support, to the comfort of keeping one's beliefs consistent.[^s1]

A workplace example: a product manager believes a new onboarding flow caused a rise in sign-ups. She pulls the weeks when sign-ups rose after the launch and quotes three enthusiastic customer emails. She does not check whether sign-ups also rose in a region that never got the new flow, or whether a marketing campaign ran the same month. Every check she runs could only agree with her, so her confidence grows without the hypothesis ever being at risk.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

The core tendency is well supported. A 2009 meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* by Hart and colleagues found a moderate overall preference for information that supports one's existing attitudes, beliefs or behaviours (d = 0.36). The preference was weaker when the belief was weakly held or not tied to values, when the supporting information was low in quality, and when people were less closed-minded; it reversed when challenging information was useful for a current goal.[^s4]

Two qualifications matter. First, Klayman and Ha (1987) argued that much of what had been labelled confirmation bias is a positive test strategy: testing the cases where you expect the property to appear. That strategy can falsify a hypothesis, and it is often efficient when the thing being sought is rare; it fails badly in tasks like Wason's, where the guessed rule sits inside the true one.[^s3] Second, a strong claim often attached to the bias, that counter-evidence makes people dig in harder, has not held up well. Guess and Coppock's three large survey experiments found no evidence of such backlash, even under conditions designed to favour it.[^s5] Nickerson also notes that a lean toward confirmation can be adaptive, for example by keeping beliefs stable or avoiding costly errors.[^s1]

## How to spot it

- Every test you have run could only confirm your view; none could have shown it wrong.[^s2][^s3]
- You have one explanation on the table and have not written down any rival.[^s1]
- Supporting evidence gets accepted at face value while contrary evidence gets audited for flaws.[^s1]
- Your confidence has risen, but the evidence would look much the same if you were wrong.[^s1]

## How to counter it

Ask what result would prove you wrong, then look for it, as the successful solvers in Wason's study did when they proposed triples designed to break their own rule.[^s2] Generate reasons why your judgment might be mistaken; Nickerson reports this improves how well confidence tracks accuracy, though it does not remove the bias.[^s1] Put rival hypotheses on paper early and ask how likely each piece of evidence would be under each one.[^s1] Choose tests by the structure of the problem: when your hypothesis may be too narrow, test cases it says should fail.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by** Wason's 1960 rule-discovery study, the first laboratory demonstration of people failing to seek disconfirming instances.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with** falsifiability: Wason explicitly framed his participants' enumerative strategy against Popper's emphasis on trying to refute hypotheses.[^s2]
- **Explained by** the positive test strategy, which Klayman and Ha argue accounts for many confirmation-bias findings without any built-in desire to confirm.[^s3]
- **Supported by** Hart and colleagues' 2009 meta-analysis of selective exposure to congenial information.[^s4]
- **Challenged by** Guess and Coppock's survey experiments, which found no backlash from counter-attitudinal information and so cut against the strongest claims about how people respond to disconfirming evidence.[^s5]
- **Related to** myside bias, biased assimilation and belief perseverance, which Nickerson lists among the guises of the same tendency, and to consider-the-opposite, hypothesis testing and analysis of competing hypotheses, which build the remedies above into routine practice.[^s1]
