# Belief perseverance

Belief perseverance is the tendency for a belief or impression to survive the collapse of the evidence it was built on. Once someone has formed a view from some piece of information, being told plainly that the information was false often reduces the view only partway; the person keeps leaning in the direction the discredited evidence pointed.[^s1][^s2] It matters for critical thinking because good reasoning requires beliefs to be proportioned to the evidence that currently stands, not to evidence that has already been withdrawn.

## Origin

The effect was established by Lee Ross, Mark Lepper and Michael Hubbard in a 1975 paper in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. Participants tried to tell genuine suicide notes from fake ones and were given false feedback saying they had done well or badly. They were then thoroughly debriefed: told that the feedback had been assigned at random and said nothing about their skill. Even so, both the participants and observers who had watched them kept impressions that matched the false feedback.[^s1] Ross and colleagues proposed that people build causal explanations (attributions) around the feedback, and that those explanations stay standing after the evidence is removed.[^s1]

## How it works

Imagine a team lead who is told in a new hire's first week that the hire missed a client deadline. The lead mentally fills in reasons: maybe the hire is disorganised, maybe careless with email. Two days later it emerges that the deadline was missed by someone else entirely. The lead accepts the correction, yet in the next planning meeting still hesitates to give the new hire the time-critical project. The original report is gone; the explanations it prompted are not. This is the pattern the debriefing experiments capture: an impression formed from false feedback, plus self-generated reasons for it, outlasting the retraction.[^s1][^s3]

Two accounts of the mechanism are on record. The attributional account says that the explanations people generate become independent support for the belief.[^s1] Wegner, Coulton and Wenzlaff found that telling participants in advance that feedback would be false still left their performance estimates biased, which led them to argue that the denied information and the denial are stored side by side, with the denial only sometimes retrieved.[^s2] Recent misinformation research describes a similar picture: a retraction can leave a gap in a person's causal model of events, and the false information can be retrieved without its "false" tag.[^s4]

## Evidence

The original debriefing finding has been reproduced. Wegner and colleagues followed Ross's methods closely and obtained debriefing-condition results within about one and a half estimated trials of the 1975 numbers, although they disputed the attributional explanation.[^s2] A 2017 meta-analysis of debunking studies (52 samples, 6,878 participants) found that corrections reduced belief in misinformation substantially but that a sizeable residue persisted after correction; persistence was stronger when people had generated their own reasons supporting the original claim.[^s3] A 2022 review in *Nature Reviews Psychology* treats this continued reliance on corrected information as a well-documented effect, while noting that fears of corrections "backfiring" and strengthening the false belief are largely overstated.[^s4]

There are limits. In Ross's study, a process debriefing that explained the perseverance mechanism itself was generally enough to remove the false self-perceptions.[^s1] A preregistered 2024 study found that a standard debriefing after a false-childhood-memory experiment cut false belief from about half of participants to under one in ten, suggesting perseverance depends on how vivid the belief is and how well the correction is delivered.[^s5] The effect is best read as reliable but partial and moderated, not as proof that corrections are futile.

## How to spot it

- You can no longer name the evidence for a view, or you know it was retracted, yet the view still feels right.[^s1]
- The reasons you would now give for a belief are explanations you constructed after first hearing it, not independent evidence.[^s1][^s3]
- In a team, a correction is acknowledged in the meeting notes but decisions continue to track the original report.[^s4]

## How to counter it

- Replace, don't just retract: give or seek an alternative causal account of what happened, which reviews identify as the most important element of an effective correction.[^s4]
- Prefer detailed corrections that explain why the claim was wrong over bare labels of "false".[^s3]
- Ask people to argue against the discredited claim themselves; meta-analytic evidence shows correction works better when the audience generates counterarguments.[^s3]
- Explain the perseverance process itself, as Ross's process debriefing did.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by** the Ross, Lepper and Hubbard 1975 debriefing experiments, and **introduced by** Lee Ross with his co-authors in that paper.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Wegner, Coulton and Wenzlaff's 1985 replication of the debriefing results, which also proposed the denial-transparency mechanism,[^s2] and by Chan and colleagues' 2017 debunking meta-analysis.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** Greene and colleagues' 2024 study, where careful debriefing largely removed implanted false beliefs, a boundary condition on the effect.[^s5]
- **Related to** the continued influence effect, the memory-based reliance on corrected misinformation discussed in the misinformation literature, and to debunking and misinformation more broadly.[^s4] It is also related to confirmation bias and biased assimilation, which describe how beliefs shape the gathering and weighing of new evidence, whereas belief perseverance concerns what happens when the old evidence is withdrawn. Consider-the-opposite is a natural remedy, since generating counterarguments improves correction.[^s3]
