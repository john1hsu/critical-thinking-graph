# Continued influence effect

The continued influence effect is the persistence of misinformation in people's reasoning after it has been retracted. Someone hears a claim, then hears a clear correction, can repeat the correction back accurately, and still draws inferences that only make sense if the original claim were true.[^s3] A correction typically reduces this reliance but rarely removes it.[^s2]

## Origin

Early work by Wilkes and Leatherbarrow in 1988 showed that retracted details in a news-style story kept appearing in readers' answers, and in 1994 H. M. Johnson and Colleen Seifert named the pattern the continued influence effect and investigated its sources.[^s3] Their experiments used a short series of reports about a warehouse fire in which an early message suggests a cause of the fire and a later message withdraws that claim.[^s3] Johnson and Seifert found that an immediate correction did not help more than a delayed one, which argued against the idea that people simply fail to edit inferences they had already made.[^s1]

## How it works

The leading account is about mental models of events. People build a causal story of what happened; when a key cause is retracted and nothing replaces it, the story has a hole, and people prefer a complete but discredited model to an incomplete one.[^s2] Johnson and Seifert found that discredited information persisted when it supplied causal structure, but not when it was only mentioned incidentally.[^s1] A second account stresses memory retrieval: the false claim stays active and competes with the correction, and people may retrieve it without also retrieving the fact that it was withdrawn.[^s3]

A workplace example: an early incident note says a payments outage was caused by a vendor's faulty update. An hour later the note is corrected: the vendor was not involved, and the cause is still under investigation. At the review meeting, the team accepts that the vendor was cleared, yet proposes renegotiating the vendor contract and adding vendor-update checks. With no alternative cause on offer, the retracted explanation still fills the gap in their account.[^s2]

## Evidence

In a 2010 warehouse-fire study, a retraction alone cut participants' references to the misinformation only slightly and not significantly compared with no retraction, whereas supplying a plausible alternative cause or a specific warning about the effect roughly halved them; combining both helped further but still did not eliminate reliance.[^s3] A 2012 review in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* summarised a large body of laboratory work in which retractions at best halved references to misinformation, and described the effect as highly persistent across procedures and laboratories.[^s2] A meta-analysis by Walter and Tukachinsky (accepted 2019, published 2020) pooled 32 studies with 6,527 participants and found that corrections do not fully eliminate misinformation's effect; corrections worked better when coherent, consistent with the audience's worldview and issued by the original source, and worse when the misinformation came from a credible source, had been repeated, or was corrected after a delay.[^s4] The residual effect is consistent and replicates, but its size varies with these conditions, and much of the evidence uses short laboratory narratives rather than long-term, real-world beliefs.[^s4][^s2]

## How to spot it

- You accept that a claim was withdrawn but still reason from its implications, for example in planning or blame.[^s3]
- An explanation was retracted and nothing has replaced it, leaving a causal gap.[^s1]
- The original claim came from a trusted source or was repeated several times before the correction.[^s4]

## How to counter it

Replace rather than just negate: give a plausible alternative explanation that fills the causal role the misinformation played.[^s1][^s3] Warn people in advance, specifically, that corrected information tends to linger.[^s3] Have the original source issue the correction, correct promptly, and keep the correction coherent.[^s4] Avoid repeating the myth more than needed, since repetition can raise its familiarity.[^s2] As a personal check, ask: "If the retracted claim is gone, what is my evidence for this conclusion?"[^s2]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a cognitive bias in which retracted information keeps steering judgement.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by Johnson and Seifert (1994):** the experiments that named the effect and showed that alternative causal explanations reduce it.[^s1]
- **Introduced by Colleen Seifert:** with H. M. Johnson, she coined the term in the 1994 study.[^s3]
- **Supported by Walter and Tukachinsky's meta-analysis:** pooled 32 studies and found corrections leave a residual effect.[^s4]
- **Contrasts with the illusory truth effect:** repetition-driven familiarity makes false claims seem true, whereas the continued influence effect concerns reliance on claims people know were retracted; the two interact when corrections repeat the myth.[^s2]
- **Related:** belief perseverance is the analogous persistence of beliefs after their evidence is discredited, and debunking, prebunking and process debriefing are remedies aimed at it.[^s2][^s3]
