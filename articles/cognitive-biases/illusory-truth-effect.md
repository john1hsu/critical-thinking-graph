# Illusory truth effect

The illusory truth effect is the tendency to rate a statement as more believable simply because one has met it before. A claim seen or heard a second time tends to feel truer than an equally plausible claim seen for the first time, and this happens for false statements as well as true ones.[^s5] Researchers also call it the truth effect or the repetition-induced truth effect; its core is that familiarity gets mistaken for evidence.[^s1]

## Origin

The effect was first reported by Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino in 1977. Their participants rated the truth of plausible general-knowledge statements, some true and some false, across several sessions; statements repeated from an earlier session were rated as increasingly true, regardless of whether they were actually correct.[^s5][^s4] Later work gave the finding its current name and connected it to research on misinformation, advertising and propaganda.[^s5]

## How it works

The leading explanation is processing fluency. A statement that has been encountered before is easier to read and understand the next time, and people use that ease as a cue that the claim is accurate. Dechêne and colleagues framed their 2010 meta-analysis within this fluency account.[^s1] Because the feeling of ease is available whether or not one remembers where the statement came from, the boost can occur even when people do not consciously recall the earlier exposure.[^s3]

A workplace example: a rumour that a competitor is about to cut prices by 30% is mentioned in a Monday stand-up, repeated in a team chat thread on Wednesday and echoed by a sales rep on Friday. No one has checked it, and each mention traces back to the same unverified remark, yet by Friday the team treats it as established and starts drafting a price response. Three repetitions of one source have been counted as if they were three pieces of evidence.[^s3]

Repetition has diminishing returns. In a 2021 study in which statements were shown up to 27 times, truth ratings rose most from the first to the second exposure and then by smaller amounts with each further repetition, a roughly logarithmic pattern.[^s4]

## Evidence

A 2010 meta-analysis of 51 studies found a reliable effect of medium size, larger when repeated statements are compared with new ones than when the same statement is rated before and after repetition.[^s1][^s6] Later experiments extended it beyond trivia. A 2018 series found that one prior exposure made fabricated news headlines seem more accurate, that the boost persisted after a week and grew with a second exposure, and that it appeared even for headlines that conflicted with participants' political views and for headlines flagged as disputed by fact-checkers.[^s3] Knowledge is a weaker shield than expected: a 2015 study found that repetition increased belief in false statements even when participants knew the correct answer, a pattern the authors called knowledge neglect.[^s2]

There are limits. Repeating extremely implausible statements did not raise their rated accuracy in the 2018 work.[^s3] A 2022 systematic map of 181 studies cautioned that the meta-analysis is dated and imputed many missing variances, that about three-quarters of studies used trivia with student samples, that long delays and real-world content are rarely tested, that only three studies were external replications, and that the near-universal rate of positive results may partly reflect publication bias.[^s6] The basic effect replicates well; how large it is in everyday settings and over long periods is less certain.

## How to spot it

- You feel sure a claim is true but cannot name where you first learned it or what supports it.[^s3]
- Several mentions of a claim turn out to trace back to one original source.[^s6]
- The claim is plausible but has never been checked against an independent record.[^s2]

## How to counter it

Evaluating accuracy at the moment of first exposure helps. When participants rated headlines for truthfulness rather than for interest on first reading, the later boost from repetition shrank or vanished; earlier work by Brashier and colleagues found the same, mainly when people knew the relevant facts.[^s5] In practice, ask "is this true, and how do I know?" the first time you meet a claim, separate how familiar it feels from what evidence supports it, and trace repeated claims to their original source before treating them as corroborated.[^s5][^s2] Warning labels alone are not enough, since flagged headlines still gained credibility from repetition.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a specific cognitive bias in which a feeling of familiarity is mistaken for evidence of truth.[^s1]
- **Explained by processing fluency:** the meta-analysis interprets the effect as people using ease of processing as a cue to truth.[^s1]
- **Supported by the Dechêne et al. (2010) meta-analysis:** it pooled 51 studies and found a reliable effect.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by Hasher, Goldstein and Toppino (1977), introduced by Lynn Hasher:** the first report that repetition raises perceived truth of both true and false statements.[^s5]
- **Demonstrated by Pennycook, Cannon and Rand (2018):** showed the effect for fake news headlines after a single exposure.[^s3]
- **Related:** it is a key reason misinformation spreads,[^s3] it sits near source monitoring because people forget where a familiar claim came from,[^s3] and techniques such as prebunking and lateral reading aim to check a claim before familiarity does the work.[^s5]
