# Motivated reasoning

Motivated reasoning is the influence of what we *want* to be true on how we reason about what *is* true. Ziva Kunda's classic review distinguished two kinds of goal: an accuracy goal, the wish to get the answer right whatever it is, and a directional goal, the wish to arrive at one particular answer. Directional goals do not simply install the preferred belief; they steer which memories are searched, which rules are applied and which evidence is scrutinised, so the preferred conclusion comes out looking well supported.[^s1] The bias is that this steering is invisible to the reasoner, who experiences the result as an honest reading of the evidence.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea that wishes shape belief is old, but the modern cognitive account comes from Kunda's 1990 article in *Psychological Bulletin*, written at Princeton. At the time many researchers argued that apparently self-serving judgments could be explained by purely cognitive factors such as prior expectations. Kunda argued instead that motivation does bias reasoning, but only by working through ordinary cognitive processes rather than around them.[^s1] Since then the concept has been applied widely, most prominently to political judgment, where it is studied as partisan or politically motivated reasoning.[^s2][^s3]

## How it works

Kunda identified two main mechanisms: a biased search of memory, in which people more readily retrieve beliefs and episodes that favour the desired conclusion, and biased belief construction, in which they assemble new reasons or theories that justify it.[^s1] Crucially, the process is constrained. People maintain an "illusion of objectivity": they reach the desired conclusion only if they can build a justification that would persuade a neutral observer, and they accept unwelcome conclusions when the evidence is strong enough.[^s1] That is why motivated reasoning usually bends judgments rather than breaking them.

Her best-known demonstration concerned caffeine. In Kunda's 1987 study, women who drank a lot of caffeine found an article linking caffeine to a health risk for women less convincing than light drinkers did, and the effect appeared only when the risk was described as serious.[^s1] In related work with Sherman, participants with a personal stake in a study's conclusion listed fewer of its methodological strengths and rated its methods as weaker, though they still recognised its strong points.[^s1]

**Workplace example.** A product manager has championed a new onboarding flow. When an A/B test shows it underperforming, she spots that the test ran during a holiday week and asks for a rerun. When an earlier test in an equally unusual week had favoured the flow, she had not questioned the timing. Each objection is reasonable on its own; the bias lies in which results get the extra scrutiny, the same asymmetry Kunda describes in people's critiques of unwelcome studies.[^s1]

## Evidence

The core claim that people judge identical information more favourably when it supports a conclusion they prefer is well supported. A 2019 meta-analysis by Ditto and colleagues pooled 51 experiments with more than 18,000 participants on partisan bias, defined as rating the same information more favourably when it supports one's political views. It found a moderate overall effect (r ≈ .25) and nearly identical effects on both sides of the political spectrum.[^s2]

Whether such effects show *motivation* at work is disputed. Tappin, Pennycook and Rand argue that the two most common designs, switching a study's outcome or switching which party endorses a message, also change other things people use to reason, such as what they already believe, so the effect cannot be cleanly attributed to wanting a conclusion.[^s3] In their own experiments with about 1,500 US adults, they compared belief updates on political statements with a Bayesian benchmark that builds in each person's prior belief, and found little politically biased departure from it; people high in analytic thinking, measured by the cognitive reflection test, came closer to the benchmark.[^s4] The authors note that their statements were not the most entrenched issues and that bias may be stronger there.[^s4] The evidence is therefore marked *mixed*: the behaviour is robust, but how much of it is motivated rather than belief-driven depends on the domain and the design.

## How to spot it

- You apply stricter standards of evidence to unwelcome findings than to welcome ones, as in the critique of methods only when results disappoint.[^s1]
- You can easily list reasons for the conclusion you want but struggle to state the strongest case against it.[^s1]
- The issue touches your stake: money, reputation, a project you proposed, or a group you belong to.[^s2]
- Reverse the result in your head: if the same data pointed the other way, would you still accept the method?[^s2]

## How to counter it

Kunda's review found that accuracy goals, such as being told one's judgment will be checked or must be justified, led people to process information more carefully and reduced several biases, but only when the goal was set *before* they saw the information.[^s1] Accuracy goals are not a cure-all: they did not remove every bias, and combined with a directional goal they can supply more material for rationalisation.[^s1] Practical steps follow from this. Decide what evidence would change your mind before you look; apply the same scrutiny checklist to every result regardless of direction; and ask a colleague without a stake to review the reasoning. Distinguish motivation from honest prior belief before accusing anyone, since updating that looks biased can be reasonable given different starting beliefs.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by Kunda's 1987 caffeine study**, which Kunda's review presents as direct evidence that personal stakes lower the persuasiveness of threatening information.[^s1]
- **Supported by the Ditto et al. (2019) meta-analysis**, which finds a moderate and symmetrical partisan bias across 51 studies.[^s2]
- **Challenged by Tappin, Pennycook and Rand (2020)**, whose critique of paradigmatic designs questions whether such effects can be causally attributed to motivation,[^s3] and by their Bayesian updating experiments, which found little politically biased updating once prior beliefs were taken into account.[^s4]
- **Related to confirmation bias, biased assimilation and myside bias**, which describe similar patterns of evidence evaluation without necessarily positing a goal; Kunda's mechanisms of biased search and harsher scrutiny of uncongenial studies overlap with them.[^s1] It also relates to the **bias blind spot**, since the illusion of objectivity hides the bias from the reasoner,[^s1] and to the **cognitive reflection test**, whose high scorers updated closer to the Bayesian benchmark.[^s4]
