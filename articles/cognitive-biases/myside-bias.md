# Myside bias

Myside bias is the tendency to evaluate evidence, generate evidence and test hypotheses in ways that lean toward the opinions and attitudes we already hold.[^s1] Keith Stanovich, Richard West and Maggie Toplak treat it as a subclass of confirmation bias: the general habit of seeking and favouring confirming information becomes myside bias when the hypothesis being "confirmed" is one's own stance on a contested question.[^s1] Its signature is asymmetry. People write more arguments for their position than against it, rate one-sided arguments for their side as stronger, and apply a rule to the other side that they would not accept for their own.[^s1]

## Origin

The phenomenon was studied before it had a settled label. Stanovich and colleagues credit David Perkins and co-workers (1985, 1991), whose studies of informal reasoning found that intelligence was only weakly related to how many arguments against their own position people could produce.[^s1] The concept sits close to Jonathan Baron's notion of actively open-minded thinking, and it became central to the research of Stanovich, now professor emeritus at the University of Toronto, culminating in his 2021 MIT Press book *The Bias That Divides Us*.[^s1][^s5] There Stanovich argues that ours is less a "post-truth" society than a "myside" one: the dispute is over which facts are true, with each side confident that its own reading is the impartial one.[^s5]

## How it works

A classic demonstration uses a between-subjects design so that no participant sees both versions. In one version, American students read that a German car had been found to be much more dangerous than ordinary cars in crashes and were asked whether it should be banned from U.S. roads; in the other, they read the same facts about the Ford Explorer and were asked whether Germany should ban it.[^s1] About 78% favoured banning the German car in the U.S., but only about 51% favoured Germany banning the American car, even though the safety data were identical.[^s1]

A workplace version: a product manager champions a new onboarding flow. When a small pilot shows a rise in sign-ups, she calls the result decisive; when a similar-sized pilot of a rival team's flow shows the same rise, she points out that the sample was too small and the period too short. Both criticisms are reasonable, but she applies them only to the side she does not own, the same evaluative asymmetry the argument-evaluation studies measure.[^s1]

## Evidence

The bias itself replicates widely. In Stanovich's argument-generation and argument-evaluation studies, participants consistently favoured their own side on issues such as organ sales and abortion.[^s1] A 2019 meta-analysis by Peter Ditto and colleagues pooled 51 experiments with more than 18,000 participants in which people judged otherwise identical information that either did or did not suit their politics; partisan bias appeared with an overall effect of about r = .245, and was of similar size in liberals (r = .235) and conservatives (r = .255).[^s2]

A striking feature is how weakly the bias tracks cognitive ability. Across informal reasoning tasks, Stanovich, West and Toplak report correlations with intelligence near zero.[^s1] By contrast, belief bias in formal syllogism tasks does decline with ability. Their explanation is that formal tasks explicitly tell people to set aside their beliefs, which brighter participants can do. Everyday tasks give no such warning.[^s1] They note one caveat: their samples were university students, whose restricted range of ability may have damped the correlations.[^s1] Stanovich adds that a person's myside bias on one topic is a poor predictor of their bias on another, so it behaves less like a stable trait than most biases.[^s5]

The evidence status here is *mixed* because the interpretation is disputed. Jonathan Baron and John Jost argued that the studies in the Ditto meta-analysis were selected in a way that favoured finding symmetry, and that many measured reasonable Bayesian updating from prior beliefs rather than bias.[^s3] Ditto's team replied that they chose designs least open to a Bayesian reading and covered a wide range of issues, while recommending that future work attend to time and context instead of broad generalisations.[^s4] The disagreement shows a deeper difficulty: when a prior belief is itself well founded, giving it weight is not obviously an error.[^s3][^s4]

## How to spot it

- Ask whether you would accept the same evidence, sample size or standard of proof if it pointed the other way.[^s1]
- Count the arguments you can give for and against your view; a lopsided list is the bias Perkins first measured.[^s1]
- Notice confidence that "my side sees the facts clearly" while the other side is distorting them; Stanovich treats this as the bias hiding itself.[^s5]

## How to counter it

Because intelligence offers little protection, the remedies are procedural rather than a matter of thinking harder.[^s1] Swap the sides in a judgment, as the car study did, and check whether your verdict changes.[^s1] Deliberately generate the strongest case against your position before deciding, and state explicitly that prior opinions should be set aside, since such instructions are exactly what let people override belief bias in formal tasks.[^s1] Recognising that the bias is domain-specific also helps: vigilance on one topic does not transfer to another.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Broader:** confirmation bias, of which myside bias is the opinion-directed subclass.[^s1]
- **Supported by** the 2019 Ditto et al. meta-analysis of partisan bias across 51 studies.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** the Stanovich and West (2008) dangerous-car study, where identical safety data led to different ban verdicts depending on whose car it was.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with** belief bias, which concerns judging logical validity by a conclusion's believability and, unlike myside bias, correlates with cognitive ability.[^s1]
- **Challenged by** Baron and Jost's (2019) critique that much of the evidence reflects rational updating and selective study choice.[^s3]
- **Studied in** the psychology of reasoning, as part of Stanovich's account of rational thinking.[^s1]
- **Related to** biased assimilation, motivated reasoning, the bias blind spot, actively open-minded thinking and consider-the-opposite, which describe, explain or remedy overlapping patterns.[^s1][^s5]
