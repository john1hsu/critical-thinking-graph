# Endowment effect

The endowment effect is the tendency to value something more once it is yours. Its usual signature is a gap between willingness to accept (WTA), the least an owner will take to part with a good, and willingness to pay (WTP), the most a non-owner will pay for the identical good.[^s1] Standard economic theory expects these two numbers to be close for everyday goods, so a large and stable gap is evidence that ownership itself shifts valuation.[^s1][^s5] For a critical thinker the effect matters because it quietly inflates the worth of whatever is already on one's side of the table: a plan, a tool, a team member, an asset.

## Origin

Richard Thaler named the effect in 1980 to describe the extra value a good acquires when it becomes part of a person's holdings.[^s1] Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Thaler gave it its best-known experimental test in a 1990 *Journal of Political Economy* paper, which pitted it against the Coase theorem's prediction that, with low transaction costs, goods flow to whoever values them most regardless of who starts out owning them.[^s1] They interpreted the effect as a manifestation of loss aversion: giving up an owned item is coded as a loss, which weighs more than the equivalent gain of acquiring it.[^s1]

## How it works

In the 1990 study, 44 Cornell law-and-economics students took part in repeated markets. Half were randomly handed a university coffee mug sold in the campus bookstore for $6. Because the mugs were assigned at random, about half should have changed hands. Instead, owners' median asking price stayed at $5.25 while buyers' median offer was only $2.25 to $2.75, and trading reached about a fifth of the predicted volume.[^s1] The same market run with tokens of fixed cash value traded as predicted, which suggested the shortfall was not caused by transaction costs.[^s1] A related exchange study by Knetsch found that 89% of students given a mug kept it rather than swap for a chocolate bar, while only 10% of those given the chocolate swapped for the mug.[^s1]

A workplace parallel: a department that built an internal reporting tool is asked what it would accept to retire it in favour of a vendor product, and names a figure far above what it would pay to acquire that same tool if it belonged to another team. Nothing about the tool differs between the two questions; only ownership does.[^s1][^s5]

## Evidence

The effect has been widely reproduced, but its interpretation is contested. Charles Plott and Kathryn Zeiler (2005) argued that WTA-WTP gaps may reflect participants' misconceptions about elicitation procedures. Using a modified elicitation mechanism and extensive pre-valuation training, they found no gap for mugs or lotteries and concluded that gaps should not be read as clear proof of loss aversion without further evidence.[^s2] Andrea Isoni, Graham Loomes and Robert Sugden (2011) replied that Plott and Zeiler's own unpublished lottery data had shown a clear, lasting gap; new experiments with the same procedures again found no gap for mugs but a persistent one for lotteries, implying the effect depends on more than misconceptions.[^s3]

At the level of the whole literature, a 2014 meta-analysis by Tuba Tunçel and James Hammitt found that the disparity is real and not merely an artifact of hypothetical questions or weak methods, but is smaller for ordinary private goods than for public and non-market goods, smaller when subjects have market experience or repeated trials, smaller with incentive-compatible mechanisms, and smaller in more recent studies.[^s4] Keith Ericson and Andreas Fuster's 2014 review judged loss aversion still the leading explanation, while noting that expectation-based reference points and psychological accounts may be needed to reconcile conflicting results.[^s5] The fair reading is that the effect is common but conditional, not a constant multiplier on every owned good.[^s4][^s5]

## How to spot it

- An asking price or "cost to replace" estimate far exceeds what anyone, including the owner, would pay to acquire the same thing.[^s1]
- Trade or reallocation falls well below what random initial assignment would predict, for example a team keeps equipment or headcount it would not request today.[^s1]
- The gap is largest for unfamiliar, hard-to-price or non-market goods, where valuation leans most on how the question is framed.[^s4]

## How to counter it

- Ask both questions: what would I pay to get this if I did not have it, and what would I accept to give it up? A large difference signals ownership is doing the work.[^s1][^s5]
- Get a market benchmark or a valuation from someone with no stake; experience with real market prices narrows the gap.[^s4]
- Repeat decisions where possible and give clear procedures: training and repeated trials shrank or removed the gap in some settings.[^s2][^s4]
