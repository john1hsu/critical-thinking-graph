# Fundamental attribution error

The fundamental attribution error is the habit of explaining what other people do by what kind of people they are, while giving too little weight to the situation they were in.[^s3] Social psychologists often call the same pattern the correspondence bias: drawing conclusions about someone's stable dispositions from behaviour that the circumstances could fully explain.[^s1] Gilbert and Malone describe it as a logical lapse, because when a situation is sufficient to explain an action, that action tells you little about the actor.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea is older than its name. Kurt Lewin argued in 1931 that psychology, like pre-Galilean physics, explained behaviour through properties of the actor rather than the field around it, and Gustav Ichheiser wrote in 1949 of a "social blindness" that credits personality for what circumstances produce.[^s1] Jones and Davis formalised the logic of inferring dispositions from acts in 1965.[^s1] Two years later, Edward Jones and Victor Harris gave the first clean demonstration: people read an essay for or against Fidel Castro and still judged the writer to hold the essay's view even when told a debate coach had assigned the position.[^s1][^s2] In 1977 Lee Ross argued that underestimating the power of situations was so pervasive and consequential that it deserved the label "fundamental attribution error".[^s1]

## How it works

Gilbert and Malone identify four routes to the error.[^s1] Observers may not see the situational pressure at all, because it is invisible or because the actor construes the situation differently. They may misjudge how strongly the situation pushes a typical person, often by imagining what they themselves would do. Knowing the situation can make the behaviour look more extreme than it was. Finally, people seem to draw a trait inference quickly and then correct it for the situation, and that second, effortful step is easily skipped.[^s1] In Gilbert, Pelham and Krull's 1988 experiments, observers who were busy rehearsing words rated a nervous-looking woman as an anxious person regardless of whether she was discussing anxiety-provoking topics, while undistracted observers adjusted for the topic.[^s1]

A workplace example: a support agent's customer ratings drop for a quarter, and a manager concludes the agent has "lost motivation". The rating sheet does not show that the agent was moved to the queue handling a botched product recall, where every caller arrived angry. Scopelliti and colleagues found that people who score high on a measure of this tendency gave similar unwarranted blame in performance evaluations, promotion decisions and investment choices.[^s2]

## Evidence

The core effect has been reproduced many times in the attitude-attribution design Jones and Harris introduced.[^s2] A 2018 study by Scopelliti and colleagues, with samples of up to about 1,000 people, found that the tendency to make correspondent inferences across attitudes, ability, emotion and morality forms a single, stable individual difference that is separate from intelligence and decision-making competence.[^s2] In the large multi-site Many Labs 2 project (2018), a version of the essay paradigm was recorded as a successful replication: participants again inferred the writer's attitude from constrained behaviour.[^s4]

Its universality is less settled. Comparative studies found weaker bias among Indian, Korean and Japanese participants than among Americans, especially when situational causes were made salient.[^s3][^s2] Many Labs 2 found no difference between WEIRD and less-WEIRD samples, but Miyamoto and Kitayama reanalysed its data and reported a larger bias among Americans than among Japanese participants (d = 0.63), arguing that coarse groupings hide such differences.[^s5] Gilbert and Malone also caution that laboratory studies cannot say how often the error occurs in daily life, and that it often costs little when people choose their situations or are always seen in the same one.[^s1] The related claim that we explain our own actions situationally and others' dispositionally (the actor-observer asymmetry) is weaker than the basic error, according to Malle's 2006 meta-analysis.[^s3]

## How to spot it

- An explanation of someone's behaviour names a trait ("lazy", "careless", "aggressive") but no circumstance.[^s3]
- The judgment would change if you learned the person had been assigned the task, role or script.[^s1][^s2]
- You would explain the same behaviour differently if you had done it yourself.[^s3]
- You formed the impression while rushed, tired or multitasking, when the correction step is most likely to be skipped.[^s1][^s3]

## How to counter it

Before settling on a trait explanation, list the situational forces acting on the person, and ask whether you would accept the trait label if the roles were reversed.[^s3] Make situational information easy to see: Scopelliti and colleagues found that showing fund performance against the market in one clear figure removed the bias among the most susceptible participants, who otherwise ignored the same numbers.[^s2] Slow down judgments of people when you are busy or distracted.[^s1][^s3] Training interventions also show promise, though the authors note the tendency is mitigable rather than eliminated.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Lee Ross, who named it in 1977, and **demonstrated by** Jones and Harris's 1967 attitude-attribution study.[^s1]
- **Explained by** the characterization-correction account, in which a quick trait inference is followed by an effortful situational correction that cognitive load disrupts.[^s1]
- **Supported by** the Many Labs 2 replication project, which reproduced the attitude-attribution effect.[^s4]
- **Confused with** the actor-observer asymmetry, a narrower self-versus-other claim with weaker support.[^s3]
- **Studied in** social psychology, where attribution is a core topic.[^s3]
- **Related to** the availability heuristic, which Gilbert and Malone link to unrealistic expectations about how others would act; to the principle of charity and debiasing training games as remedies; to Richard Nisbett's work on culture and reasoning; and to dual-process theory.[^s1][^s3][^s2]
