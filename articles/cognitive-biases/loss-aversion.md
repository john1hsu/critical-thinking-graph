# Loss aversion

Loss aversion is the tendency for losses and disadvantages to count for more in a decision than gains and advantages of the same size.[^s1] What counts as a loss or a gain is set by a reference point, usually the status quo, so moving that reference point can flip which option a person prefers even though the options themselves have not changed.[^s1] For a critical thinker it matters because it quietly tilts comparisons toward keeping what one already has.

## Origin

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky built loss aversion into their account of choice under risk, and in a 1991 *Quarterly Journal of Economics* paper Tversky and Kahneman extended it to riskless consumer choice. They reviewed experiments showing that preferences depend on the reference level and proposed a reference-dependent model whose central assumption is that losses weigh more than comparable gains.[^s1] The same year, Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler used loss aversion in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* to tie together two anomalies for standard economics: the endowment effect and status quo bias.[^s2]

## How it works

Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler open with an economist who bought fine wine cheaply years earlier. At its current auction value he will neither sell a bottle nor buy another one, because giving up a bottle he owns registers as a loss, while paying the same price for a new one only registers as a forgone gain.[^s2] A workplace version: a team is offered a new scheduling tool that is better on three features and worse on one. If the current tool is the reference point, the one lost feature is felt as a loss and can outweigh three gains of similar size, so the team stays put.[^s1][^s2] Describe the same choice from the new tool's point of view and the asymmetry runs the other way, which is the preference reversal the reference-dependent model predicts.[^s1]

## Evidence

Loss aversion is usually summarised by a coefficient, the ratio of how much a loss hurts to how much an equal gain pleases. A 2024 meta-analysis by Alexander Brown, Taisuke Imai, Ferdinand Vieider and Colin Camerer pooled 607 estimates from 150 articles published between 1992 and 2017 and found a mean of about 1.96, with a 95% credible range of roughly 1.8 to 2.1; few features of study design shifted the estimates much.[^s3] A 2020 set of five studies with 17,720 participants (Kellen Mrkva and colleagues) found loss aversion at every level of knowledge and experience, though it was weaker among people who knew more about the specific attribute at stake and stronger among older consumers.[^s4]

The effect is still disputed. David Gal and Derek Rucker argued in 2018 that the evidence does not show losses to be, on balance, more impactful than gains, and that many findings attributed to loss aversion have other explanations; they asked why the idea has nonetheless been so widely accepted.[^s5] Mrkva and colleagues replied directly that claims of its demise, or that status quo bias or unrepresentative samples fully explain it, are not supported by their data.[^s4] The fair summary is that a gain-loss asymmetry is common and sizeable on average, but its strength depends on the person, the stakes and the attribute, so a fixed "losses count twice" rule overstates it.[^s3][^s4][^s5]

## How to spot it

- A proposal is rejected mainly because of what would be given up, while its gains are listed but barely discussed.[^s2]
- Willingness to sell something is far above willingness to buy the same thing, a sign that ownership has shifted the reference point.[^s2]
- Preferences change when only the description changes, for example when the same outcome is framed as avoiding a loss rather than securing a gain.[^s1]

## How to counter it

- Name the reference point explicitly and re-evaluate the options from a neutral one, or from the alternative's side, to see whether the preference survives.[^s1]
- Ask whether you would choose the current option if you did not already have it; the asymmetry between keeping and acquiring is exactly what the endowment effect exposes.[^s2]
- Build attribute-specific knowledge before deciding: in the 2020 studies, people who understood an attribute well showed less loss aversion for that attribute.[^s4]
- Treat the effect as a tendency to check for, not a law; the debate over its size means assumptions in models or policies should be tested locally.[^s3][^s5]
