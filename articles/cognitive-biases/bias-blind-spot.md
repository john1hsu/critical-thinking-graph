# Bias blind spot

The bias blind spot is the tendency to recognise biases in other people's thinking while failing to see the same biases in one's own. People readily accept that judgment in general is distorted by self-interest, prior beliefs, anchors or hindsight, and they often overestimate how much those forces sway others, yet they rate themselves as less affected than the people around them.[^s1][^s2] Scopelliti and colleagues call it a "metabias": a bias in how people perceive bias itself.[^s4]

## Origin

Emily Pronin, Daniel Lin and Lee Ross named and documented the effect in a 2002 paper in *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, linking it to the idea of naive realism.[^s1] Across three surveys, people rated themselves as less susceptible to a list of described biases than the average American, their seminar classmates and fellow travellers at an airport. A follow-up showed that people who displayed a better-than-average pattern held to their self-assessments even after reading how that very bias could have shaped them, and a final study found participants judged a peer's self-serving explanation of a test result as biased while seeing their own matching explanation as fair.[^s1]

## How it works

Two mechanisms are usually proposed. The first is naive realism: people assume they see the world as it is, so when others disagree, bias in those others becomes the natural explanation.[^s2][^s4] The second is the introspection illusion. Most biases work outside awareness, so when people look inward for signs of bias they find none and conclude they are objective; when they judge others, they cannot look inside, and instead apply general theories of bias to what the other person concluded and stands to gain.[^s2][^s3] Ehrlinger, Gilovich and Ross found that ratings of another person's bias tracked how far that person's judgment departed from what would be expected, while people's ratings of their own bias did not.[^s3] They also found that people treat their own group's personal stake in an issue as a source of insight, but the other side's stake as a source of distortion.[^s3]

A workplace example: two managers on a hiring panel disagree about a candidate. One went to the same university as the candidate; the other used to work at the candidate's previous employer. Each sees how the other's background could tilt the assessment, but treats their own background as giving useful inside knowledge. Each asks themselves "am I being unfair?", finds no feeling of unfairness, and concludes the problem lies with the colleague.[^s3][^s2]

## Evidence

The core asymmetry has replicated. In three preregistered experiments with 969 participants, Chandrashekar and colleagues (2021) reproduced two of Pronin, Lin and Ross's studies and found a large self-other gap in perceived susceptibility to biases (d = -1.00). Unlike the original, they also found a smaller gap for personal shortcomings such as procrastination (d = -0.34), but the gap remained clearly larger for biases than for shortcomings.[^s5] A replication database records this as broad support for the core effect with one deviation from the original.[^s6]

Scopelliti and colleagues (2015) built a scale measuring individual differences in the blind spot. Most people showed it, the scale was stable over time, and scores were distinct from measures of intelligence, decision-making ability, self-esteem and self-presentation. Higher scores predicted ignoring advice from others and getting less benefit from training aimed at a different bias.[^s4] Limitations remain: much of the evidence uses self-ratings of described biases rather than observed biased behaviour, and samples are mostly Western students or online panels.[^s5][^s4]

## How to spot it

- You can list the biases likely to affect a colleague, a rival team or the other side of a dispute, but struggle to name any that apply to you on the same question.[^s1][^s3]
- Your evidence that you are unbiased is that you looked inward and felt fair.[^s2]
- You attribute disagreement to the other party's interests or background while treating your own background as expertise.[^s3]
- You accept that you are biased "in general" but not in this particular judgment.[^s3]

## How to counter it

Pronin's 2007 review reports that teaching people that bias operates without awareness, and that introspection is therefore a poor test for it, removed the blind spot in laboratory studies; simple warnings about bias did little.[^s2] In practice, judge your own decisions by the same external tests you apply to others: does the conclusion favour my interests, and does it depart from what a neutral person would expect?[^s3] Use procedures rather than self-scrutiny, such as blind review of work samples, independent scoring before discussion, and seeking outside advice, since people high in the blind spot are the ones most likely to discount advice.[^s4] Whether such awareness reliably leads people to correct their judgments, and not merely to admit they might be biased, is still an open question.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Emily Pronin, with Lin and Ross, and **demonstrated by** their 2002 survey studies.[^s1]
- **Explained by** naive realism, the assumption that one's own perceptions are objective, which Scopelliti and colleagues identify as the root of the effect.[^s4]
- **Explained by** the introspection illusion, the overweighting of one's own thoughts and feelings as evidence of being unbiased.[^s2]
- **Supported by** Chandrashekar and colleagues' 2021 preregistered replications.[^s5]
- **Related to** debiasing, because people who do not think they are biased have little reason to correct their judgment; Pronin lists anchoring among the biases people see in others but not themselves.[^s2]
