# Framing effect

The framing effect is the finding that people make different choices, or form different evaluations, when the same information is worded differently. Describing a medical programme by the lives it saves rather than the lives it loses, or a product by its success rate rather than its failure rate, changes what people prefer even though nothing about the outcomes has changed.[^s1][^s2] Tversky and Kahneman argued that this sensitivity to wording is a serious problem for rational-choice theory, which assumes that preferences should not depend on how equivalent options are described.[^s1]

## Origin

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman introduced the term in a 1981 *Science* article. They defined a decision frame as a person's picture of the acts, outcomes and contingencies in a choice, shaped partly by how the problem is worded and partly by the person's own habits and norms.[^s1] The paper showed preference reversals in choices about money, both hypothetical and real, and about human lives, and compared changing a frame to changing one's visual perspective on a scene.[^s1] It also catalogued related cases, such as people who would buy a theatre ticket after losing a cash note of the same value but not after losing the ticket itself.[^s1]

## How it works

In the best-known demonstration, often called the Asian disease problem, respondents chose between two programmes against an outbreak expected to kill 600 people. When the options were described in terms of lives saved, 72% of 152 respondents chose the certain option (200 saved) over a gamble with the same expected value. When a second group of 155 saw the same outcomes described as deaths (400 will die), 78% chose the gamble.[^s1] Tversky and Kahneman explained the reversal with prospect theory: outcomes are judged as gains or losses from a reference point, the value function is concave for gains and convex for losses, so people tend to avoid risk when gains are in view and seek risk when losses are.[^s1]

Levin, Schneider and Gaeth later argued that "framing" covers three different effects. Risky-choice framing is the Asian disease type. Attribute framing changes how people evaluate a feature of an object or event depending on whether it is described positively or negatively. Goal framing changes how persuasive a message is depending on whether it stresses the benefits of acting or the costs of not acting.[^s2]

*Workplace example.* A project sponsor is told that a migration plan "keeps 90% of customer accounts running without interruption" and approves it. Another sponsor hears that the same plan "will interrupt service for 10% of accounts" and asks for a riskier alternative that might avoid any outage. The data are identical; only the reference point has moved.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

The core risky-choice effect replicates well. Kühberger's 1998 meta-analysis put the average effect across about 80 Asian-disease-type studies at d = 0.57, a small-to-moderate size.[^s3] The FORRT Replication Atlas records the 1981 problem as successfully replicated in the first Many Labs project (2014, d = 0.60) and in Many Labs 2 (2018, about 7,200 participants, d = 0.40), though the latter estimate was well below the original.[^s5] Mandel and Kapler's 2018 experiments with 506 participants found a combined effect of d = 0.42 even when outcomes were stated as exact values.[^s3]

Findings vary with the type of frame, which was the reason Levin and colleagues proposed their typology: studies using different operational definitions tap different processes and so produce apparently inconsistent results.[^s2] In Mandel and Kapler's studies, cognitive reflection, numeracy, actively open-minded thinking and need for cognition did not predict who was swayed by the frame.[^s3]

What remains disputed is how to interpret the effect. Mandel's 2014 experiments found that many participants read "200 people will be saved" as a lower bound (at least 200), in which case the two frames are not truly equivalent; one condition of 76 participants showed no significant framing effect, although Mandel and Kapler later argued that this null result reflected low power, since their larger samples did show a small effect.[^s3] In a 2022 commentary Mandel argued that if the assumption of equivalence between frames fails, framing experiments cannot show either that people are irrational or that they are rational.[^s4]

## How to spot it

- A decision or rating changes when a figure is restated in the opposite direction (survival versus mortality, pass rate versus defect rate).[^s2]
- The options are presented as gains, and the sure thing looks obviously best; or as losses, and the gamble suddenly looks attractive.[^s1]
- Only one description has been considered. Tversky and Kahneman noted that people rarely notice alternative frames on their own.[^s1]

## How to counter it

The most direct check is to restate the problem in the opposite frame and see whether your preference survives; if it flips, the wording rather than the outcomes was doing the work.[^s1][^s2] State numbers as exact values to remove the at-least reading.[^s3] Distance from the problem may also help: in Keysar and colleagues' 2012 experiments, framing effects disappeared when people made the choice in a second language, which the authors attributed to reduced emotional reactivity and more deliberate processing.[^s6] Tversky and Kahneman cautioned that even when a reversal is exposed, it is not always clear which preference to keep.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Tversky and Kahneman, and **demonstrated by** their 1981 Asian disease problem.[^s1]
- **Explained by** prospect theory's reference-dependent, S-shaped value function.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Kühberger's 1998 meta-analysis and by the Many Labs 2 replication project.[^s3][^s5]
- **Challenged by** Mandel's 2014 quantifier experiments and his 2022 commentary on the equivalence assumption; both question the interpretation more than the existence of the effect.[^s3][^s4]
- **Related to** attribute framing and goal framing, the two other members of Levin and colleagues' typology.[^s2]
- **Related to** the foreign-language effect, in which choosing in a second language removed the framing effect.[^s6]
- **Related to** numeracy and the cognitive reflection test; in Mandel and Kapler's Asian-disease experiments neither measure predicted who was swayed by the frame.[^s3]
