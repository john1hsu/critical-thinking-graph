# Halo effect

The halo effect is the tendency for a general impression of someone to leak into judgments of their particular qualities. A person who seems likeable, competent or attractive overall gets rated higher on traits that have little to do with that impression, and someone who makes a poor first impression gets marked down across the board.[^s1][^s2] The same pattern applies to products, brands and organisations: one striking strength or flaw tints the assessment of everything else.[^s1]

## Origin

The psychologist Edward L. Thorndike of Teachers College, Columbia University, named the effect in a short 1920 paper in the *Journal of Applied Psychology*. He examined ratings that supervisors gave to army officers, aviation cadets, industrial workers and teachers, and found that the separate trait scores were correlated far more highly and evenly than the traits themselves plausibly could be.[^s1] He called this a "halo": raters seemed to form a single good-or-poor view of each person and let it suffuse every rating.[^s1]

## How it works

Thorndike's evidence was statistical. In one set of cadet ratings, intelligence correlated about .5 to .6 with physique, leadership and character alike, though he expected it to relate much more to some of these than to others. Across eight raters, technical flying ability correlated with general officer quality at about .67 on average, where Thorndike thought the true figure could hardly exceed about .25.[^s1]

Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson's 1977 experiment showed the mechanism directly. In it, 118 undergraduates watched one of two videotaped interviews with the same college instructor, who spoke with a European accent. In one he was warm and friendly; in the other, cold and distant. Viewers of the warm version found his appearance, mannerisms and accent appealing, while viewers of the cold version found the very same features irritating.[^s2] The global liking changed how unchanged, directly observable traits were perceived.[^s2] Participants also misread the direction of influence: those who saw the cold lecturer denied that disliking him had affected their trait ratings, and claimed instead that his traits had made them dislike him.[^s2]

A workplace example: in an annual review, a manager rates an engineer who recently rescued a high-profile launch. The rescue is real evidence of skill under pressure, but the same manager also rates the engineer highly on documentation, mentoring and punctuality, areas where the record is average. Those inflated scores are the pattern Thorndike flagged: one vivid impression standing in for separate evidence on each dimension.[^s1]

## Evidence

The physical-attractiveness halo is the best-tested modern form. A 2023 secondary analysis of Psychological Science Accelerator data had 11,570 participants in 45 countries rate 120 standardised faces, each participant rating one trait.[^s4] In all 11 world regions, more attractive faces were rated more confident, emotionally stable, intelligent, responsible, sociable and trustworthy. Links with caring, dominance, aggression, meanness and unhappiness held in only some regions.[^s4] The study used one face database and averaged ratings per face, so it shows a consistent rating pattern rather than its size in real decisions.[^s4]

The original rating-error version is more disputed. In 1993 Kevin Murphy, Robert Jako and Rebecca Anhalt argued that the traditional view rested on four assumptions: that halo is common, that it is a rater error, that it inflates correlations among dimensions, and that it is harmful. They reviewed evidence that each is wrong or doubtful.[^s3] Their central point is that real traits often do correlate ("true halo"), so high inter-trait correlations cannot by themselves show error. They recommended that researchers stop using halo indices as outcome measures.[^s3] Thorndike himself admitted he had no objective way to measure how big the error was.[^s1] Because the effect is clear in experiments and cross-cultural ratings but hard to measure in field ratings, this article rates the evidence as mixed.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## How to spot it

- Ratings of distinct qualities move together, all high or all low, with no dimension standing out.[^s1]
- One salient feature (charm, a famous success, good looks, a bad first meeting) keeps coming up as the reason for judgments about unrelated traits.[^s1][^s4]
- You feel sure a trait caused your overall opinion; Nisbett and Wilson's participants felt the same and were wrong about the direction.[^s2]

## How to counter it

Thorndike's own advice still applies. Record the evidence for each quality rather than just a score, and rate each quality separately, ideally without seeing the evidence for the person's other qualities.[^s1] In practice this means scoring one criterion across all candidates before moving to the next, and using different assessors for different dimensions where possible. Do not treat every correlation between traits as bias, because some qualities really do go together.[^s3] Because people cannot reliably detect the influence by introspection, procedural safeguards work better than resolving to "be objective".[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Edward Thorndike, whose 1920 analysis of supervisors' ratings named the "halo".[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Nisbett and Wilson's 1977 warm-versus-cold lecturer experiment, which isolated the effect of global liking on ratings of unchanged traits.[^s2]
- **Supported by** Batres and Shiramizu's 2023 cross-cultural analysis of the attractiveness halo.[^s4]
- **Challenged by** Murphy, Jako and Anhalt's 1993 critique of halo error as a measurable, harmful rater error.[^s3]
- **Applied in** performance appraisal and personnel rating, the setting of Thorndike's original data.[^s1]
- **Related to** the introspection illusion and the bias blind spot: Nisbett and Wilson's participants could not see the influence on their own judgments and misattributed its cause.[^s2]
