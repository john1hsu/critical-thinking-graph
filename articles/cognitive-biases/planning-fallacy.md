# Planning fallacy

The planning fallacy is the tendency to believe that one's own project will go as planned, and so to underestimate how long it will take, even when one knows that most comparable projects have overrun.[^s1] Its signature is a gap between knowledge and prediction: people can remember accurately that past tasks took longer than expected yet still forecast an optimistic finish for the next one.[^s2] The bias concerns predictions about one's own work; observers forecasting someone else's task tend to be more cautious.[^s1]

## Origin

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky coined the term in 1979, in work on why intuitive forecasts go wrong.[^s2] Their chapter on intuitive prediction separates two kinds of information a forecaster has: singular information about the case at hand, and distributional (base-rate) information about how similar cases have turned out.[^s3] The planning fallacy is what happens when a forecaster leans on the singular case and neglects the distribution.[^s3] Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross gave the idea its main experimental footing in a 1994 paper in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.[^s1]

## How it works

Planning naturally adopts what Kahneman and Tversky called the "inside view": the planner focuses on the specifics of the task and mentally rehearses the steps to completion.[^s2] That scenario tends to be idealized and success-focused, so interruptions, competing demands and unexpected obstacles are left out.[^s4] The "outside view" (how long did similar jobs actually take?) would correct the forecast, but people often treat past overruns as irrelevant to the new, apparently different, task.[^s2] Buehler and colleagues found that when participants did recall past lateness, they blamed it on external, one-off and specific causes, which made those failures seem not to apply this time.[^s1]

A workplace example: a team lead estimates that migrating a reporting system will take three weeks, building the estimate by listing the steps and sizing each one. The last two migrations each took about seven weeks, but each had "special" problems (a vendor delay, a staff absence) that the lead does not expect to recur. The plan has no line for the next unspecified problem, so the three-week date slips.[^s2] Completion times are especially exposed because they include waiting, interruptions and procrastination, not just hands-on work.[^s4]

## Evidence

In the 1994 thesis study, 37 honours students predicted on average that they would finish in about 34 days; the actual average was about 56 days, and fewer than a third finished by their own best-guess date.[^s1] Even their "worst case" predictions were met by fewer than half.[^s1] Across studies, few participants spontaneously mentioned past experience while predicting, and simply asking them to recall past tasks did not help; asking them to link those memories to the current task did reduce the optimistic bias.[^s1] Buehler's 2019 overview describes the effect as robust across small chores and large construction projects, across cultures, and in individual and group work.[^s2] A 2016 series of four studies again found significant underestimation among people planning forward through a real project.[^s4]

The main dispute is about mechanism rather than existence. Roy and Christenfeld (2007) argue that people partly underestimate future durations because they also misremember past durations as shorter; in their experiments, bias in memory tracked bias in prediction, and underestimation vanished for novel tasks.[^s5] On that account, the error lies partly in memory, not only in neglecting it, and the effect may depend on task type and familiarity.[^s5]

## How to spot it

- An estimate is built only from the plan's steps, with no reference to how long comparable jobs took.[^s2]
- Past overruns are explained away as one-off bad luck.[^s1]
- The forecaster's own estimate is markedly shorter than a neutral colleague's estimate of the same job.[^s1]
- The schedule has no allowance for interruptions or waiting, only for working time.[^s4]

## How to counter it

- **Take the outside view:** anchor on the distribution of outcomes for a reference class of similar projects, then adjust.[^s2]
- **Connect the past to the present:** explicitly ask how earlier, similar tasks went and why this one would differ.[^s1]
- **Plan backward** from the deadline; in a 2016 study, backward planners predicted later finishes and showed no significant bias, though the mechanism is unclear.[^s4]
- **Unpack the task** into subtasks, adopt an observer's perspective, and commit to specific times for specific steps; these tactics help in some studies but not reliably.[^s2] [^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a cognitive bias in prediction about one's own plans.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, who coined the term in 1979.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** Buehler, Griffin and Ross's 1994 thesis-completion study.[^s1]
- **Explained by** the inside view, the case-specific planning perspective that crowds out distributional data.[^s2] [^s3]
- **Supported by** Wiese, Buehler and Griffin's 2016 backward-planning studies, in which forward planners again underestimated completion times.[^s4]
- **Challenged by** Roy and Christenfeld's 2007 memory-bias study, which locates part of the error in biased recall of past durations.[^s5]
- **Related to** taking the outside view (the standard remedy), the overconfidence effect, the premortem, optimism bias and base-rate neglect, which share the habit of discounting how things usually go.[^s2] [^s3]
