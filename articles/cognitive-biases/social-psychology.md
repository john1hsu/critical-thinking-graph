# Social psychology

Social psychology is the branch of psychology that studies how people think about, influence and relate to one another. The most widely cited textbook definition, Gordon Allport's from 1954, describes it as the study of how an individual's thoughts, feelings and behaviour are shaped by the actual, imagined or implied presence of others; Chryssochoou notes that this definition keeps the individual at the centre and leaves many collective processes out of view.[^s2] Britannica frames it more broadly as the scientific study of individual behaviour in its social and cultural setting.[^s4] For critical thinking the field matters because many of the best-known errors in everyday judgment, such as over-blaming people's character for what their circumstances caused, conforming to a confident group, or being persuaded by a speaker's status rather than the argument, were first identified and measured by social psychologists.[^s1]

## Origin

Early experiments on how the presence of others changes performance, such as Norman Triplett's 1898 studies of competition, are usually counted as the field's beginning.[^s1] Theories of crowds and imitation (Le Bon, Tarde) and Durkheim's ideas about collective representations supplied a parallel, more sociological strand, and before the Second World War a split opened between a "psychological" and a "sociological" social psychology.[^s2] Floyd Allport (1924) pushed the psychological side, arguing that any psychology of groups reduces to the psychology of individuals.[^s2]

Kurt Lewin, often called the father of social psychology, reshaped the field in the 1940s and 1950s by insisting on experiments and by summarising its core idea as behaviour being a function of both the person and the social situation.[^s1] The postwar decades produced its landmark programmes: Sherif's and Asch's conformity studies, Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory (1957) and Milgram's obedience experiments.[^s1] From the 1970s European researchers, notably Tajfel on social identity and Moscovici on minority influence and social representations, criticised American social cognition for individualising the social and tried to restore group and societal levels of explanation.[^s2]

## How it works

The field's standard tool is the experiment: researchers manipulate a feature of the social situation, measure its effect on judgment or behaviour, and use the result to test a theory.[^s1] Britannica notes that laboratory experiments, often with student volunteers, are sometimes criticised as artificial, so field studies in realistic settings are used alongside them.[^s4] Doise's four levels of analysis (processes within the person, interactions between people, people's positions as group members, and shared ideologies and norms) are a common map for deciding where an explanation should sit.[^s2]

A workplace illustration: a manager notices that in design reviews junior engineers rarely object once a senior colleague has spoken. A social psychologist would not stop at "the juniors lack confidence" (a person-level story) but would test the situation: run matched review sessions in which the senior speaks first or last, or in which objections are written down anonymously before discussion, and compare how many problems are raised. This is Lewin's person-and-situation idea put to work, and it is the reason the field so often finds that circumstances explain more of what people do than observers expect.[^s1]

## Evidence and limitations

Social psychology has been at the centre of psychology's replication debate. The Open Science Collaboration's 2015 attempt to repeat 100 studies published in 2008 found that only 36% of replications reached significance and that replication effects were on average about half the size of the originals.[^s3] Social psychology fared worse than cognitive psychology on this measure: 14 of 55 social effects (25%) replicated at p < .05, against 21 of 42 cognitive effects (50%), although the drop in effect sizes was similar across the two fields.[^s3] A reader should therefore treat a single striking social-psychology finding as provisional until it has been independently repeated.[^s3]

Critics have also questioned the field's scope. Chryssochoou records arguments that mainstream social cognition reduces the social to processes inside individual heads, and cross-cultural work has challenged how far findings from Western student samples generalise.[^s2] Britannica's survey likewise notes the long-running worry about artificial laboratory settings.[^s4]

## Connections

Social psychology is a subfield of psychology and is often compared with its neighbour cognitive psychology, for instance in large replication projects that sampled both.[^s3] It was **influenced by** Kurt Lewin, whose experimental approach and person-situation formula shaped the modern discipline,[^s1] and, through Moscovici's theory of social representations, was **influenced by** Émile Durkheim.[^s2] It is **challenged by** the replication crisis, in which social-psychology effects replicated at a notably low rate.[^s3] Its central themes of conformity, attribution and influence link it to graph nodes such as the fundamental attribution error, groupthink and the elaboration likelihood model of persuasion.[^s1]
