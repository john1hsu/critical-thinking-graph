# Red herring

A red herring is a move that pulls a discussion away from the question actually at issue and onto a different topic, frequently one that stirs emotion, so that the audience follows the new trail and the original question goes unanswered.[^s1][^s2] Douglas Walton describes the characteristic pattern as a line of reasoning that starts toward the proper conclusion and then veers off without arriving at any definite alternative conclusion.[^s1] Introductory logic courses file it among the fallacies of relevance: the material offered may be psychologically persuasive, but it is logically unconnected to the point being argued.[^s3]

## Origin

The name is a hunting image: a strong-smelling smoked fish dragged across a trail could send hounds off after the wrong scent.[^s3] Curtis's Fallacy Files records this fox-hunting account and a second story, reported by the logic textbook author S. Morris Engel, in which escaping prisoners used a herring to throw tracking dogs off.[^s5] The underlying error is much older than the label. Curtis and Archie both connect it to Aristotle's *ignoratio elenchi* in *On Sophistical Refutations*, and Archie calls the red herring essentially a dialectical fallacy that can be subsumed under that broader heading.[^s5][^s2]

## How it works

The fallacy has three parts: an open question, a switch to a second topic that does not bear on it, and an exchange that ends with the second topic having replaced the first.[^s1] In the argument map above, a release meeting is weighing whether failing payment tests justify a delay. The product lead switches to a competitor's outage and then to the competitor's general incompetence. The new material may be true and even interesting, but its truth would not make shipping on schedule any safer, so it throws no weight onto the conclusion.[^s1] Walton distinguishes two subtypes: *diversionary irrelevance*, where the digression is a vivid, identifiable side issue, and *pettifogging*, where the speaker rambles without settling on any one diversion.[^s1] He also reads the red herring as typically a tactic rather than an error: the wrong-conclusion fallacy looks like a bad shot at the target, while the red herring looks like a deliberate attempt to lead the audience elsewhere.[^s1]

## How to spot it

Restate the question at issue in one sentence and ask whether the new remark, if true, would make any answer to that question more or less likely; Curtis gives this as the core test of relevance.[^s5] Walton's criteria for calling something a red herring are that no specific wrong conclusion can be identified, that there is real textual evidence of a strategic diversion, and that the digression adds no probative weight to the conclusion.[^s1] Typical carriers are attacks on the speaker, accusations of hypocrisy and guilt by association, which a Florida International University course presents as varieties of the same diversionary pattern.[^s3]

## How to counter it

Name the original question and bring the discussion back to it, acknowledging the side issue as a separate topic if it deserves attention later.[^s3] Check the classification before calling a foul. If the arguer reaches a definite but different conclusion, Walton would call it a wrong conclusion (*ignoratio elenchi*) rather than a red herring.[^s1] If a remark only distracts and offers no argument, Archie suggests treating it as a rhetorical distraction rather than a fallacy.[^s2] Curtis warns that relevance can be surprising: a point that seems off-topic may turn out to bear on the question, so the test is logical connection, not whether the link is obvious.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Contrasts with *ignoratio elenchi*.** Walton separates the two in his 2004 classification. The wrong-conclusion fallacy proves a specific, different claim, while the red herring diverts without reaching any definite conclusion. He also notes that textbooks disagree on whether red herring is a subspecies of irrelevant conclusion (Copi and Cohen) or a separate, closely associated fallacy (Hurley).[^s1]
- **Confused with the straw man fallacy.** An Indiana University course text notes that the two are often confused. A straw man misrepresents the opponent's position, whereas a red herring changes the subject.[^s4] Archie puts the same contrast this way: in a red herring the opponent's argument is not distorted but simply abandoned.[^s2]
- **An instance of the fallacies of relevance.** The FIU course treats red herrings as the general family of relevance fallacies, with ad hominem, tu quoque and guilt by association as examples. Curtis likewise treats red herring as the most general fallacy of irrelevance.[^s3][^s5]
- **Studied in argumentation theory.** Walton's dialectical analysis, using probative-weight diagrams and criteria for diversion, is the main scholarly treatment of how to classify the fallacy.[^s1]
