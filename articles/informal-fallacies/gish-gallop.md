# Gish gallop

The Gish gallop is a debating tactic in which a speaker buries an opponent under a large volume of claims, whether accurate or not, so that the opponent cannot answer them all in the time allowed.[^s1] A University of Maryland course on argumentation describes it as trying to overwhelm the other side with as many arguments as possible while disregarding their accuracy, validity or strength, and then declaring victory because some of them went unanswered.[^s2] It is classed here as an informal fallacy because the conclusion is presented as established by the sheer number of points and by the opponent's failure to rebut each one, not by the quality of any of them.[^s2]

## Origin

The name was coined by Eugenie C. Scott, the founding executive director of the National Center for Science Education (NCSE), after Duane T. Gish, the Institute for Creation Research's best-known public debater.[^s1] A 2024 *Scientific American* opinion piece dates the coinage to 1994 and notes that Gish regularly challenged biologists to staged debates about evolution.[^s3] Scott's own essay on creation-evolution debates, reproduced in the TalkOrigins Archive, makes the underlying point: creationists offered many simple, plausible-sounding arguments, each of which took far longer to refute than to state, and the short debate format made it hard to explain the science.[^s5] Scott called the gallop effective but shallow and misleading, and argued that debate is a sport rather than a way of informing the public about which view is accurate.[^s1] The Maryland course stresses that the technique did not originate with Gish or with science denial; he only lent it a memorable name.[^s2]

## How it works

The tactic exploits an asymmetry of effort. Stating a false or misleading claim takes seconds, while showing why it is wrong can take minutes of context and evidence.[^s5][^s3] The Nature columnist Phil Williamson cites Brandolini's "bullshit asymmetry principle" for the general version: refuting nonsense takes far more energy than producing it.[^s4] In a timed exchange that gap becomes decisive. In the argument map above, an account manager lists fourteen problems with a rival product in a ten-minute review. The analyst has three minutes, debunks two, and the account manager presents the remaining twelve as standing objections. Nothing about the unanswered twelve has been shown to be true; they survive only because there was no time to check them.[^s2] The *Scientific American* piece describes the delivery as fast and confident, mixing falsehoods, non sequiturs and cherry-picked facts, with the aim of discrediting the opponent rather than reaching the truth.[^s3]

## How to spot it

Count the claims and compare that number with the time the other side has to respond; a list far longer than anyone could check is the first sign.[^s2] Look for the closing move, in which unanswered points are treated as conceded. In effect, that move shifts the burden of proof onto the respondent, and it resembles the argument from ignorance, since absence of rebuttal is offered as evidence of truth.[^s2] Watch also for claims that are recycled word for word across debates, which the Maryland course identifies as a sign of a standard script rather than engagement with the opponent.[^s2]

## How to counter it

Do not try to answer every claim. The *Scientific American* piece recommends naming the tactic and its dishonesty to the audience, then returning to your own case.[^s3] The Maryland course's description suggests why this works: the gallop's force lies in the claim that unanswered points count as wins, so exposing that inference matters more than chasing each point.[^s2] Scott and the *Scientific American* author both caution scientists against accepting staged debates whose format rewards volume over accuracy.[^s1][^s3] Williamson's advice for the wider information environment is complementary: keep correcting falsehoods even when the effect is slow, because open-minded readers may still find the correction.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Eugenie Scott.** NCSE credits Scott with coining the term and naming it after Duane Gish.[^s1]
- **Amplifies misinformation.** The *Scientific American* piece argues that a gallop can leave the audience less informed than before, because false claims go out faster than they can be corrected.[^s3]
- **Related to the argument from ignorance and the burden of proof.** Treating unrebutted claims as proven reverses who must support what, so absence of refutation stands in for evidence.[^s2]
- **Related to Brandolini's law.** The bullshit asymmetry principle Williamson cites names the effort gap that the gallop exploits in timed exchanges.[^s4][^s5]
- **Related to the firehose of falsehood.** The Maryland course notes that the same flooding approach, used as propaganda, is called firehosing.[^s2]
