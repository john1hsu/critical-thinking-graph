# Ad verecundiam

*Ad verecundiam* is the standard label for a fallacious appeal to authority. The usual textbook account says it occurs when someone's pronouncement is accepted as evidence although that person is not really an authority, or not a relevant one; celebrity product endorsements and relying on only some experts when experts disagree are the stock cases.[^s1] The label matters because appealing to authority is not wrong in itself: modern argumentation theory treats a well-made appeal to expertise as a reasonable but defeasible argument. The fallacy is the misuse of that pattern, not the pattern.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The Latin name comes from John Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690), which listed *ad verecundiam* among several kinds of argument people use to win assent or silence opponents. Locke did not call it a fallacy, and his wording about eminence, dignity and breaches of modesty points to deference to social standing more than to expertise; *verecundia* means something like modesty or shame.[^s1] Earlier, the Port-Royal *Logic* of Arnauld and Nicole (1662) had described a "sophism of authority", warning against deferring to age or noble birth on questions that turn on insight, and Jeremy Bentham later set out conditions an appeal to authority must meet, any one of which, if it fails, cancels the appeal's force.[^s1] Richard Whately distinguished authority that rests on knowledge, which earns a degree of deference, from authority that rests on office, which demands obedience. In his *Rhetoric* he treated deference as creating a defeasible presumption in an authority's favour, open to counter-presumptions such as the vanity or self-interest of the learned.[^s3] Jean Goodwin (1998) later sorted appeals to authority into appeals grounded in command, in expertise and in dignity, and traced Locke's term back to the rhetorical practice of ancient Rome.[^s4]

## How it works

Douglas Walton's analysis, summarised by Hansen, treats the appeal to expertise as a scheme: E is an expert in field S, E asserts p on the basis of that knowledge, so p is presumably true. If critical questions about E's expertise and the circumstances of the statement get good answers, the argument creates a presumption; if any answer is no, the presumption is cancelled. It becomes the fallacy when the bad appeal still looks correct in context and seriously obstructs the goal of the discussion.[^s1] In the argument map above, a chief financial officer's seniority is used to settle an ergonomics question and to shut down a safety review. Her standing is real, but it is the wrong kind for this claim and this field.[^s1][^s2]

Walton and Koszowy argue that many real cases turn on exactly that mismatch. Epistemic authority rests on knowledge; deontic or administrative authority rests on institutional power, as with a judge, a commander or a manager. The fallacy arises when an appeal to one is passed off as the other, or when an administrative appeal is presented as more conclusive than it is, so that the listener feels intimidated and stops asking questions.[^s2]

## How to spot it

Ask the critical questions listed above and watch for answers that are missing rather than negative. Typical signs are a source cited for fame or rank rather than relevant training, an expert speaking outside their field, one side of a live expert disagreement presented as the consensus, and a tone that treats questioning the authority as rude.[^s1][^s2] That last sign is the one Locke's term names: the pressure comes from modesty or deference, not from evidence.[^s1][^s3]

## How to counter it

Separate the authority's standing from the question being asked. Accept the appeal for what it can carry, a presumption that a qualified source is probably right inside their field, and ask for the evidence behind the claim when the stakes justify it.[^s1][^s3] Where rank is doing the work, name the kind of authority involved: a manager can decide what the team will do, but that decision does not make a factual claim true.[^s2] Aberdein's virtue approach, also summarised by Hansen, places the healthy attitude between two vices, too little respect for reliable authorities and too much deference to them.[^s1]

## Connections

Ad verecundiam **misuses the argument-from-expert-opinion scheme**: it is what that scheme becomes when a required condition fails while the argument still seems sound.[^s1] The name was **introduced by John Locke**, whose treatment is the classic starting point.[^s1] The modern analysis was **influenced by Richard Whately**, whose split between knowledge-based and office-based authority anticipates current distinctions.[^s3] Walton and Koszowy **explain** the fallacy through the confusion of **epistemic and deontic authority**, work that sits within **argumentation theory**.[^s2] Goodwin's three forms of authority extend the same line of analysis.[^s4] The fallacy is closely tied to **source credibility** and to how a fair appeal shifts the **burden of proof**, and Whately modelled his logical account of ad verecundiam on the **ad hominem**, as a charge that an arguer is inconsistent with their own reverence for an authority.[^s1][^s3]
