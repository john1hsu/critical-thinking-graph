# False dilemma

A false dilemma unfairly presents too few choices and implies that one of the offered choices must be taken.[^s1] When the menu is cut down to exactly two, textbooks often call it the black-white (or black-or-white) fallacy, a special case of the broader error.[^s1] The familiar shape is "either X or Y; not X; so Y", where the trouble lies not in the inference but in the either-or premise that hides other possibilities. Taxonomies of online manipulation list "false dichotomies or dilemmas" among the techniques that argumentation research consistently flags as epistemically dubious.[^s3]

## Origin

A systematic treatment appears in a 2013 paper in *Argumentation*, in which T. Tomić set out to explain the fallacy with six general criteria that any good account of a fallacy should meet, citing earlier work such as Charles Hamblin's 1970 *Fallacies*.[^s2] His starting puzzle is that a false dilemma is fallacious even though it relies on a deductively valid argument form, so the error cannot be located in the logic alone.[^s2] Introductory textbooks, meanwhile, work with short definitions that stress the unfair narrowing of choices and keep the black-white label for the two-option case.[^s1]

## How it works

The argument map above shows a typical workplace version. A manager argues that the team must either hire two agents or watch satisfaction collapse, then rules out hiring. If the either-or premise were true, the conclusion would follow; the form is the valid pattern of eliminating one alternative.[^s2] The premise is false, though: self-service help, ticket triage, a part-time hire or fixing the defects that generate tickets are all left out, and the meeting ends up debating the wrong question.[^s1] Tomić distinguishes four types, the false quandary, the defeasible sound quandary, the false obstruction and the defeasible sound obstruction, and suggests a fitting kind of criticism for each.[^s2] The presence of "defeasible sound" types is a useful reminder that not every either-or argument is a fallacy: some dilemmas are real, and the work lies in checking whether this one is.[^s2]

## How to spot it

The basic signal is a demand to choose from a short list, often just two items, presented as if it were complete.[^s1] Ask whether the options are exhaustive and whether they really exclude each other; the critical questions above turn those checks into prompts.[^s2] In headlines and posts, a common form pairs two unrelated policy goals as rivals, as in the test item "we either need to improve our education system or deal with crime on the streets" used in a 2022 study.[^s3]

## How to counter it

The classic response is to escape between the horns: point out, fairly, that there is another choice that should have been on the table.[^s1] Tomić adds that spotting a false dilemma is not only a defensive skill; uncovering suppressed alternatives, or showing how apparently opposed options can be combined, is also a route to creative problem solving.[^s2] At population scale, prebunking has been tested directly. In Roozenbeek and colleagues' 2022 experiments, a roughly 90-second video warning about false dichotomies improved people's ability to recognise the technique in later posts, with the largest recognition effect of the five videos tested (Cohen's d = 0.68, n = 1,095), and smaller gains in confidence, trust judgements and sharing intentions.[^s3] Run as a YouTube advertisement, the same video raised correct identification of false-dichotomy headlines within about a day of viewing, a small effect (h = 0.13) that cleared the authors' preregistered threshold; how long the effect lasts was not measured.[^s3]

## Connections

False dilemma is a standard topic of **argumentation theory**, where Tomić's account treats it as a case study in how fallacies should be explained.[^s2] It **contrasts with disjunctive syllogism**: the false dilemma borrows that valid form, so the fault lies in the disjunctive premise rather than the inference.[^s2] It is **used in misinformation**, which is why inoculation research picked it as one of five target techniques, alongside ad hominem attacks, scapegoating, incoherence and emotionally manipulative language.[^s3] The **prebunking** experiments of 2022 supply the main evidence that people can be trained to notice it quickly.[^s3]
