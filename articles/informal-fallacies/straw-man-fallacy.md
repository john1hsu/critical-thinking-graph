# Straw man fallacy

The straw man fallacy is committed when someone attacks a distorted version of an opponent's position, one that is easier to knock down, and then treats the win as a refutation of the view the opponent actually holds.[^s1][^s3] Because the attack lands on a position nobody defended, it is irrelevant to the real dispute and draws attention away from the reasons that bear on it.[^s3] It is an informal fallacy: the problem lies not in the logical shape of the argument but in the mismatch between what is refuted and what was claimed.[^s1]

## Origin

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats the straw man as a variety of *ignoratio elenchi*, Aristotle's "ignorance of refutation", in which the conclusion proved is not the one in dispute.[^s1] Hansen's entry suggests the modern name may trace to Isaac Watts's *Logick* (1724), whose discussion of *ignoratio elenchi* describes disputants who dress up an opponent's view as an image of straw and then celebrate defeating it.[^s1] Later fallacy theory, following Hamblin and Walton, analyses it as a failure of dialogue: a move that misstates the other party's commitments.[^s1][^s4]

## How it works

Aikin and Casey distinguish three forms.[^s2] The **representational straw man** restates the opponent's view or argument in a weaker form, often by turning a qualified claim into an absolute one. The **weak man** picks the opponent's weakest actual argument, refutes it and implies the whole case has fallen, which works like a hasty generalisation. The **hollow man** invents a position and assigns it to a vague "some people", so it bears no relation to anything anyone argued.[^s2][^s6]

The argument map above is a representational straw man at work. A colleague proposes one remote day a week; the reply attacks a company with nobody ever in the office. The criticism may be correct about full remote work, but it does not touch the proposal on the table.[^s3] Dowden's textbook gives the parallel case of a lobbyist asking to log selectively on a limited area and being answered as if he had called for wholesale deforestation.[^s3]

## How to spot it

Compare the criticism with what the other side actually said. Signs include quantifiers that have grown ("some" into "all", "one day" into "always"), qualifications that have vanished, and attributions to unnamed critics.[^s2][^s3] Drawing a consequence the opponent would reject is not by itself a straw man; the fallacy needs a misstatement of what was said.[^s3] Walton and Macagno add that quotations cut from their context are a common vehicle: the words are genuine, but the commitment imputed to the speaker is not.[^s4] The tactic depends on the audience not knowing the accurate version of the view, so spotting it requires knowing that version.[^s6]

## How to counter it

Restate the position accurately and ask the critic to engage it, ideally in terms the original speaker would accept.[^s3] Ask for clarification of ambiguous terms before responding, so a vague claim cannot be stretched into an extreme one.[^s3] When one weak argument has been answered, ask whether the stronger arguments for the same conclusion have been addressed.[^s2] Aikin and Casey note that misrepresentation is not always fallacious: correcting a common misconception or clearing away a bad argument can be legitimate, provided it is not presented as defeating the opponent's best case.[^s2] They also warn against the opposite excess, the "iron man", in which a weak view is strengthened beyond what its holders argue and given more attention than it deserves.[^s5]

## Evidence

The straw man is defined by a norm of fair representation rather than discovered by experiment, so its status here is normative.[^s1] Aikin and Casey argue that straw-man arguments persuade best among audiences who are not motivated to check them and can backfire with audiences who know the real view; because they usually reinforce what a group already thinks, they can feed group polarisation.[^s2] These are argued claims, not measured effects.

## Connections

- **Variety of *ignoratio elenchi*.** The SEP classes it with arguments that prove a conclusion other than the one at issue.[^s1]
- **Possibly named by Isaac Watts.** The term may come from Watts's 1724 *Logick*; the attribution is tentative.[^s1]
- **Confused with wrenching from context.** Walton and Macagno separate distortion through selective quotation from the broader straw man.[^s4]
- **Amplifies group polarisation.** By confirming an audience's existing picture of the other side, straw men can push groups further apart.[^s2]
- **Contrasts with steelmanning.** Building the strongest version of an opponent's case, which Aikin and Casey call iron-manning, is the reverse move and has its own risks.[^s5]
