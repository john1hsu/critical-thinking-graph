# Informal fallacy

An informal fallacy is a kind of argument that looks better than it is, where the trouble cannot be located just by writing the argument in symbols and checking its form. The flaw sits instead in what the premises say, how words are used, what is quietly assumed, or how the move fits the conversation it is part of.[^s1] Formal fallacies such as undistributed middle or denying the antecedent are instances of identifiable invalid forms; informal fallacies are the remainder, which logicians have found more productive to analyse without a formal language.[^s1] The label therefore covers a "mixed bag": some informal fallacies are logical failures, while others, such as begging the question, can be deductively valid yet still fail to prove anything.[^s1]

## Origin

The catalogue is ancient. In *Sophistical Refutations* Aristotle listed thirteen ways a refutation can be merely apparent, six that depend on language (equivocation and amphiboly among them) and seven that do not (including begging the question, many questions and *ignoratio elenchi*).[^s1] John Locke's *Essay* (1690) added the *ad* arguments (*ad verecundiam*, *ad ignorantiam*, *ad hominem*), which he contrasted with *ad judicium* arguments resting on real evidence; he did not call them fallacies.[^s1] Richard Whately (1826) split fallacies into logical and "material" ones, and J. S. Mill (1843) relocated much of the topic into inductive reasoning.[^s1] The modern textbook core comes from Irving Copi (1961), whose eighteen informal fallacies, sorted into fallacies of relevance and of ambiguity, trace eleven items to Aristotle and seven to the post-Lockean tradition.[^s1]

In 1970 C. L. Hamblin's *Fallacies* attacked this "standard treatment" as dogmatic and historically ungrounded, and named the standard definition: an argument that seems valid but is not.[^s1][^s2] His book is usually treated as the start of modern fallacy theory and pushed the study toward the rules of dialogue.[^s1]

## How it works

Three post-Hamblin accounts explain what makes an informal fallacy bad. The informal-logic account of Johnson and Blair (1977) says a cogent argument needs acceptable, relevant and sufficient premises, and a fallacy is a frequent violation of one of those criteria.[^s1] Pragma-dialectics treats every fallacy as the breach of a rule of critical discussion, so a threat (*ad baculum*) fails because it stops the other side from voicing a view.[^s1] Douglas Walton's pragmatic theory judges an argument by its use within a goal-directed dialogue, and argues that many textbook fallacies are misused versions of reasonable but defeasible presumptive arguments.[^s3] On this view a fallacy is a deceptively bad move that impedes the dialogue's goal, often an argumentation scheme pressed past its critical questions.[^s1]

A workplace example shows why form alone does not settle the matter. Two engineers argue for a vendor: "Our principal architect says this database will scale, so it will." As an appeal to expert opinion this can be reasonable. It becomes fallacious when the architect has no database expertise, when other experts disagree, or when the appeal is used to shut down questions in a design review. The same sentence can be acceptable in one context and fallacious in another, which is exactly what a purely formal test misses.[^s3][^s1]

Trudy Govier makes the general point directly: deductive invalidity is neither necessary nor sufficient for fallaciousness, since begging the question and straw man can occur in valid arguments, while many cogent arguments are not deductively valid.[^s2]

## How to spot it

Read for the dialogue, not just the inference. Ask whether the premises are acceptable, relevant and sufficient; whether a key word shifts meaning; whether the conclusion actually answers the point at issue; and whether a move pressures, evades or misrepresents rather than gives reasons.[^s1] Apply moderate charity first, reconstructing the best plausible reading, but do not stretch charity so far that no argument can ever be faulted.[^s2] Govier shows with a chain letter that promises luck to those who forward it: even a fair reading leaves only anecdotes of earlier events "causing" later ones, so post hoc reasoning and hasty generalization remain.[^s2]

## Evidence and limitations

Psychologists Ulrike Hahn and Mike Oaksford (2007) argued that arguments from ignorance, circular arguments and slippery slopes are better seen as argument forms whose strength varies with their content, and modelled that strength in Bayesian terms; their experiments tested whether people's strength judgments track the factors the model predicts.[^s4] The result supports the context-sensitive view: a fallacy name marks a pattern worth checking, not an automatic verdict.[^s4][^s1]

Scholars still dispute the definition. Many reject the "appearance" condition as too subjective, others keep it to separate seductive errors from mere carelessness, and frequency has been proposed as a substitute.[^s1] Teaching by fallacy lists is also contested: critics note that no list is complete and that drilling labels can make students overly critical, and a 2023 review of six long-running textbooks found little improvement since Hamblin's critique.[^s1] Mill's suggestion that biases predispose people to fallacies has been developed by later authors who link particular cognitive biases to particular fallacies.[^s1]

## Connections

Informal fallacy **contrasts with formal fallacy**, the family defined by invalid logical form.[^s1] The concept was **influenced by *Sophistical Refutations***, whose thirteen sophistical refutations still supply much of the standard list.[^s1] It is **studied in informal logic**, whose acceptability-relevance-sufficiency criteria define fallacies as violations,[^s1] and in **cognitive psychology**, where Hahn and Oaksford tested how people judge such arguments.[^s4] It is **explained by pragma-dialectics**, which recasts each fallacy as a rule violation in critical discussion,[^s1] and by **Bayesian reasoning**, which accounts for why the same pattern can be weak or strong depending on content.[^s4] Specific informal fallacies in this graph include the false dilemma, the straw man and tu quoque, a variety of ad hominem. Walton's account ties the topic to argumentation schemes and their critical questions, and Govier's analysis to the principle of charity.[^s3][^s2] Charles Hamblin's critique marks the turn to modern fallacy theory.[^s1]
