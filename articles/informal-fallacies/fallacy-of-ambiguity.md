# Fallacy of ambiguity

A fallacy of ambiguity is any argument that succeeds only by trading on two meanings of the same words.[^s2] Ambiguity itself is just the condition of an expression having more than one interpretation; it becomes a fallacy when one reading makes the premises acceptable and a different reading is needed to reach the conclusion.[^s3] The label names a family rather than a single error. Its core members are equivocation (a single word shifts sense), amphiboly (a sentence's grammar allows two readings) and accent (stress or emphasis changes what is said).[^s2][^s3] Many textbooks add composition, division and hypostatization, which also turn on how words are read.[^s3]

## Origin

The family goes back to Aristotle's *Sophistical Refutations*, which divides apparent refutations into those that depend on language and those that do not.[^s1][^s4] He traces the language-dependent kind to a mismatch between words and things: names are finite while things are unlimited, so one name must cover several meanings, and people who are unskilled with words are misled when they treat names as stand-ins for things.[^s4] He lists six such fallacies: ambiguity of a word, amphiboly, combination, division, accent and form of expression. His examples include "learn" meaning both to understand and to acquire knowledge, and "5 is 2 and 3" read as making five both even and odd.[^s4]

Plantin's dictionary of argumentation records the Latin names (*aequivocatio*, *fallacia compositionis*, *fallacia divisionis*, *fallacia accentis*, *fallacia figurae dictionis*). It also notes that this list anchors what Charles Hamblin in 1970 called the standard treatment of fallacies.[^s5] Hansen's SEP entry observes that only equivocation and amphiboly have survived largely as Aristotle meant them. His combination and division differ from the modern part-whole fallacies, and form of expression is closer to what we now call a category mistake.[^s1]

## How it works

Every fallacy of ambiguity has the same skeleton. An expression appears in the reasoning with two available senses. Sense A makes a premise true or plausible, sense B is what the conclusion requires, and the argument is valid only if A and B are treated as one.[^s3] In a syllogism, a shifted term in effect gives four terms instead of three and breaks the chain of inference.[^s3]

The argument map shows a workplace case. A handbook allows "a reasonable amount" of unpaid leave, and an employee argues that a month off after a long launch is reasonable, so the handbook entitles them to a month. In the policy, "reasonable" is measured against the employer's needs. In the second premise, it means understandable from the employee's side. Each premise is fine in its own sense, but the conclusion needs one sense throughout.[^s2][^s3]

The other members vary the source of the double reading. In amphiboly it is structure, as in a sign promising "clean and decent dancing, every night except Sunday".[^s3] In accent it is stress: "Did *you* steal the butter?" and "Did you *steal* the butter?" ask different questions.[^s3] The IEP adds the written case of "invalid", which reads differently depending on which syllable is stressed.[^s2] In composition and division the slide is between a predicate applied to each member and one applied to the group.[^s3]

## How to spot it

- Look for a key term that occurs more than once, especially a relative, theoretical or figurative word, and ask whether it keeps the same meaning each time.[^s2][^s3]
- Reread suspect sentences with different grouping or emphasis and see whether the claim changes.[^s2][^s3]
- When a property moves between parts and wholes, check whether it applies distributively (to each one) or collectively (to the group).[^s3]
- Watch for abstractions such as "the market" or "Nature" doing concrete things; that is hypostatization.[^s3]

## How to counter it

Disambiguate, then re-test. Replace the suspect expression with a precise paraphrase, use that paraphrase in every premise and in the conclusion, and ask whether the argument still works.[^s2] Usually one premise turns false or the inference collapses. Remedies differ by member: define key terms for equivocation, rewrite the sentence for amphiboly, and supply the missing emphasis or context for accent.[^s2][^s3] Writers should give readers enough context, because a reader cannot ask what was meant.[^s3]

Two limits apply. Classifications vary: the IEP groups only amphiboly, equivocation and accent under ambiguity and treats composition and division separately, while textbook chapters often include them.[^s2][^s3] And naming a fallacy is no substitute for examining the passage. The IEP stresses that whoever alleges a fallacy carries the burden of showing it, so a critic should first look for a single fair reading under which the argument holds.[^s2]

## Connections

It is an *informal fallacy* because the defect lies in meaning, not logical form.[^s1] It was *introduced* by *Aristotle* in the *Sophistical Refutations*, whose list of language-dependent fallacies *influenced* the later standard treatment.[^s1][^s4][^s5] It is *explained by* the linguistic property of *ambiguity*: the fallacy is the misuse of that property in reasoning.[^s3] *Equivocation*, *amphiboly*, *fallacy of accent*, *fallacy of composition*, *fallacy of division* and *hypostatization* are its members, and the *fallacy of four terms* is equivocation inside a syllogism.[^s2][^s3] The *distributive-collective distinction* is the test for composition and division, and *precising definitions* and the *principle of charity* are the remedies for the family as a whole.[^s2][^s3]
