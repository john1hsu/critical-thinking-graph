# Genetic fallacy

The genetic fallacy is the error of evaluating a claim by its genesis: rejecting (or accepting) an idea because of who first proposed it, how it was discovered or what motive lay behind it, when that history does not bear on whether the idea is true.[^s3] Dowden's textbook describes it as giving too much weight to where an idea came from and too little to its content and the justification offered for it.[^s3] Kevin Klement notes that formulations vary: some textbooks count any evaluation based on origin, positive or negative, as fallacious, while others reserve the label for using origin to reject a belief.[^s1] Either way it is usually filed as a fallacy of relevance, on the reasoning that a statement's truth depends on its content rather than its source.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The term appears to date from the 1920s or 1930s. Klement traces its earliest use to philosophy of science, where it named the mistake of mixing up the logical analysis of a theory with the story of how the theory came about; he cites Morris Cohen and Ernest Nagel's 1934 logic textbook as an example of that original use, and notes that the meaning has widened since.[^s1] Erol's open textbook chapter reports that the term is generally believed to have been coined by Cohen and Nagel.[^s4] The idea leans on the distinction between the context of discovery and the context of justification, which Andrew Ward dates at least to Hans Reichenbach's *Experience and Prediction* (1938) and traces further back to Frege's rejection of psychologism; Wesley Salmon later defined the genetic fallacy as treating items from the discovery context as if they belonged to the justification context.[^s2] Ward also records Charles Hamblin's description of the fallacy as confusing historical origin with logical nature.[^s2]

## How it works

The fallacy substitutes a story about a claim's past for an assessment of its present support. In the argument map above, a proposal for shorter sprints is dismissed because the colleague heard the idea on a podcast. Both premises might be true, yet whether four-day sprints suit the team is a question about the team's work, which a trial or a look at past delivery data could settle; the podcast has dropped out of the picture once the idea is on the table.[^s3][^s4] Dowden's examples follow the same pattern: rejecting a legislator's bill because she got the idea from a dream, or Freud's argument that a patient's religious belief must be faulty because it springs from a need for a father figure.[^s3] The positive version is equally weak; Klement points out that accepting a politician's position because of the courage it took to adopt it is as much an error as rejecting it because of her motives.[^s1]

## How to spot it

Watch for replies that answer "is this true?" with "here is where it came from". Typical forms are "that idea started with our competitor", "he only says that because he was trained there" and "that's just a politically motivated complaint". Dowden shows that one such reply can be labelled several ways at once: an attack on the critic's character (ad hominem), a change of subject, and a genetic fallacy, because it attacks the origin of a complaint instead of the complaint.[^s3] A useful test is whether the claim's evidence would still stand if the story about its source were true.[^s4]

## How to counter it

Separate two questions: how did the idea arise, and what supports it now? Grant whatever is fair about the first, then ask for the evidence on the second.[^s3][^s4] Not every appeal to origins is fallacious, though, so check what the origin is being used to show. Klement argues that the causal history of a belief can be relevant to its epistemic status: if a belief came from a reliable process, or from a causal chain involving the very fact that makes it true, that is good reason to think it true.[^s1] Negative uses are harder to justify. Learning that an answer came from a magic eight-ball shows the belief was unjustified, but it does not show the answer was false.[^s1] So an unreliable source undercuts one reason to believe a claim; it does not establish the opposite.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

As a normative category, the genetic fallacy is defended by argument rather than experiment, and its scope is disputed. Klement (2002) identifies several valid or inductively strong forms of genetic reasoning, including arguments from reliable processes and "self-referential" abductive arguments in which the best explanation of a belief is a truth-tracking process.[^s1] He also argues that rebutting such arguments with a better causal explanation, such as wishful thinking or social conformity, comes close to the textbook fallacy without committing it.[^s1] Ward (2010) goes further, arguing that genealogical critiques are not automatically fallacious: the fallacy is assuming that an account in one context automatically settles the other, and a belief's causal history can bear on its justification when that role is established independently.[^s2] His worked example, a paranoia-based genealogical argument against a belief, comes out deductively invalid and usually inductively weak, yet he maintains that such critiques can still be valuable in argument.[^s2]

## Connections

The genetic fallacy is an **instance of the fallacies of relevance**, because the source of a belief is standardly taken to be irrelevant to its truth.[^s1] It is often **confused with ad hominem**: when the origin in question is a person, the same reply can be read as either.[^s3] It is **explained by the distinction between the context of discovery and the context of justification**, whose conflation was the fallacy's original meaning.[^s2] Ward's paper **challenges** the standard view by defending a limited value for genealogical critique.[^s2] The term is usually credited to **Morris Cohen** (with Ernest Nagel), though that attribution is reported with some hedging.[^s4] Klement's defence of self-referential abductive arguments ties it to **inference to the best explanation**, and its legitimate cousin is the assessment of **source credibility**, where a source's track record does bear on how much weight its say-so deserves.[^s1]
