# Fallacies of relevance

Fallacies of relevance are arguments whose premises do not bear on the truth of their conclusion, whether or not those premises are themselves true.[^s4] What makes them fallacies rather than simply weak arguments is that the gap is disguised: the premises seem connected to the conclusion through emotion, social pressure or a shared topic, but supply no logical support.[^s5] The label names a family, not a single error. Textbook lists usually include attacks on the person (ad hominem), appeals to popularity (ad populum), to force (ad baculum) and to pity, the genetic fallacy, straw man, missing the point (irrelevant conclusion) and red herring.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

The core idea is old. Aristotle counted *ignoratio elenchi*, proving something other than the point at issue, among the sophistical refutations, and Walton traces the modern category back to it.[^s2] Hansen's SEP entry describes that fallacy as misdirection in argumentation rather than a failed inference. It notes that Richard Whately (1826) placed the irrelevant-conclusion fallacies among the non-logical "material" fallacies and treated the Latin *ad*-arguments as variants of *ignoratio elenchi*, fallacious only when used unfairly.[^s1] Locke had grouped *ad verecundiam*, *ad ignorantiam* and *ad hominem* arguments against arguments *ad judicium* (from the evidence itself) in 1690, without calling them fallacies.[^s1]

The heading "fallacies of relevance" became standard through mid-20th-century logic textbooks. Irving Copi's 1961 *Introduction to Logic* listed ad hominem, ad populum, ad baculum and others under it, and the SEP notes that such fallacies work by exploiting psychological associations.[^s1] Walton reports that Copi and Cohen frame the family as confusing psychological with logical relevance, while Hurley's textbook splits the pure cases into missing the point and red herring.[^s2] In informal logic, Johnson and Blair (1977) built relevance into their test of a cogent argument: premises must be acceptable, relevant and jointly sufficient. Fallacies of irrelevant premises give no support, whereas fallacies of insufficient premises give some, but not enough.[^s1]

## How it works

The argument map shows a workplace case. A team lead rejects a proposal for shorter sprints because it came from an analyst who joined three months ago. Both premises are true, but neither says anything about cycle times, review load or trial results, the things that would decide whether the plan works. The conclusion feels supported because doubts about a newcomer's judgment spill over onto her idea.[^s4][^s5]

Different members supply the false sense of connection in different ways. Ad hominem swaps the person for the claim, ad populum swaps social approval for evidence, and the genetic fallacy swaps a claim's history for its current justification.[^s4] Appeals to force and pity let a threat or sympathy do the work of a reason.[^s5] Straw man and red herring change the subject: one attacks a weaker version of the opponent's position, the other diverts the discussion to a different issue.[^s5]

Walton argues that relevance should be judged as probative, not merely topical. Two statements can share a subject yet neither raises nor lowers the probability of the other. An argument is relevant when it moves the discussion toward proving the designated conclusion in dispute.[^s2] On this dialectical view relevance depends on the type of dialogue and its stage, so the same remark can be relevant in one exchange and a diversion in another.[^s2]

## How to spot it

- Grant every premise for the sake of argument and ask whether the conclusion is now more likely. If nothing changes, the support is missing.[^s5]
- Ask what the reason is about. Reasons about the speaker, the audience's feelings, popularity, origins or penalties are warning signs.[^s4][^s5]
- Restate the claim in dispute and compare it with the conclusion actually argued for. A mismatch signals missing the point or a straw man.[^s2][^s5]
- Track the topic. If the discussion has shifted and the original question is left hanging, look for a red herring.[^s2][^s5]

## How to counter it

Name the claim that is actually at issue and ask for reasons that bear on it, such as data, a test or a mechanism. Then set the irrelevant consideration aside without dismissing the person who raised it.[^s4][^s5] In the sprint example, a fair response is to propose a two-week trial and judge the plan by its results.

Two cautions apply. First, relevance depends on context: a witness's character can matter when the question is whether to believe their testimony, so an appeal to character is not automatically fallacious.[^s4] Second, classification is harder than textbooks suggest. Walton found that short examples often fit both "wrong conclusion" and "red herring", and that diagnosing a red herring may require evidence that the diversion was deliberate.[^s2] In an earlier paper he examined the traditional "gang of eighteen" fallacies and concluded that only four are fallacies of relevance in the strict sense; for the rest, irrelevance is a peripheral or tangential feature.[^s3] So it is better to show why a premise fails to bear on the conclusion than simply to attach a label.[^s2]

## Connections

It is an *informal fallacy* because the defect lies in the content of the reasons, not in logical form.[^s5] It was *influenced by* the *Sophistical Refutations*, whose *ignoratio elenchi* is its ancestor, and by *Richard Whately*, who treated the *ad*-arguments as forms of that fallacy.[^s1][^s2] It is *explained by* *dialectical relevance*, the account of relevance as progress toward the conclusion in dispute within a given dialogue.[^s2] It is *studied in* *informal logic*, whose RSA criteria make relevance one of three tests of a good argument, and in *argumentation theory*, where Walton and pragma-dialecticians have reworked the category.[^s1][^s3] *Red herring*, *straw man*, *ad hominem*, *ad populum*, *appeal to force*, *appeal to pity*, the *genetic fallacy* and *accident* are its usual members.[^s4][^s5] It contrasts with the *fallacy of ambiguity*, a family whose arguments fail because a term shifts meaning rather than because a premise is off the point.[^s1]
