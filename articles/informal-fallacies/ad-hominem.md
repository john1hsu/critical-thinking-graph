# Ad hominem

An *ad hominem* ("to the person") is a reply that targets the arguer instead of the argument: it points to someone's character, motives, circumstances or behaviour and treats that as grounds for rejecting what they said. Textbooks usually file it as a fallacy of relevance, on the assumption that a personal attack has no bearing on the merits of a position.[^s5] Douglas Walton's book-length treatment sorts personal attacks into five subtypes: direct (abusive), circumstantial, bias, poisoning the well, and tu quoque, and gives each its own argument form and test for when it goes wrong.[^s2]

## Origin

The label began as the name of a legitimate move. In its older sense, which Hitchcock traces back to Aristotle and finds in John Locke, an argument *ad hominem* pressed opponents with consequences of their own principles or concessions: a fair way to win their assent, though weak as proof that a claim is true.[^s5] Richard Whately (1826) recast the fallacy as presenting such a concession-based argument as if it settled the question absolutely; Hitchcock accepts that this is a real error but argues it is a misdescription of a sound argument, not a personal attack.[^s5] The modern sense of attacking the person grew out of this tradition, and Walton later gave the family its most detailed normative analysis.[^s2][^s1]

## How it works

In the argument map above, a procurement analyst recommends a cheaper storage vendor and supports it with a cost comparison. A colleague answers that the analyst once worked for that vendor, so the recommendation is wrong. The premise may be true and is not irrelevant: it is a reasonable signal to double-check the numbers. The error lies in the leap from "this person may be biased" to "the claim is false".[^s5] Walton models the circumstantial version as a defeasible scheme: a person argues for A, their actions or situation commit them to something at odds with A, so their credibility as a sincere advocate is questioned and the argument loses plausibility.[^s1] The scheme licenses at most a reduction in how much weight we give the arguer; it becomes fallacious when it is presented as a refutation of the argument itself.[^s1]

## How to spot it

Ask what work the remark about the person is doing. Walton's critical questions for the direct and circumstantial schemes check whether the allegation is supported by evidence, whether character is relevant in this type of dialogue, whether an alleged inconsistency could be explained if the person were allowed to answer, and whether the conclusion drawn is the modest one (trust this speaker less) or the strong one (the argument is wrong).[^s1] Warning signs include an attack that would leave every premise of the original argument standing, and an attack offered where the claim does not rest on the speaker's word at all, as with a calculation anyone can check.[^s5]

## How to counter it

Separate two questions the attack runs together: should we rely on this person, and is the argument good? If the claim depends on testimony or expert judgment, a documented conflict of interest is a fair reason for caution, and the remedy is to look for independent confirmation.[^s5] If the claim rests on evidence or reasoning that can be checked directly, return the discussion to that evidence. Van Eemeren, Garssen and Meuffels study personal attacks as breaches of the pragma-dialectical freedom rule, which forbids keeping the other side from advancing or questioning a standpoint; on that view the repair is to let the attacked party back into the discussion and deal with the standpoint itself.[^s4]

## Evidence

Ordinary arguers do recognise blatant personal attacks as unreasonable. In experiments reported by van Eemeren, Garssen and Meuffels (2009), respondents rated personal attacks as less reasonable than non-fallacious moves. The gap was largest for direct attacks, smaller for indirect ones, and small for tu quoque, which in one study was judged no less reasonable than a legitimate reply.[^s4] Yet such attacks still move opinion. Barnes and colleagues (2018) showed 439 undergraduates, then 199 online adults, claims attributed to scientists. Allegations of past data fabrication or of a financial conflict of interest lowered acceptance of a claim about as much as a criticism of the study's methods, while attacks on the scientist's education or reputation for sloppiness had no reliable effect.[^s3] The authors caution that a conflict-of-interest charge may sway people almost as much as an allegation of fraud.[^s3]

Whether "ad hominem" names a single fallacy at all is disputed. Hitchcock argues that relevant attacks on a speaker's ethos are legitimate, purely diversionary insults contain no reasoning to be fallacious, and the genuine errors are better described as other fallacies, such as straw man or overclaiming.[^s5] Walton's approach is similar in spirit: personal attack arguments are defeasible schemes that are sometimes reasonable and sometimes fallacious, to be judged case by case.[^s2][^s1]

## Connections

Ad hominem is a central **informal fallacy**, and **tu quoque** is one of its subtypes.[^s2] Its pre-modern sense is credited to **John Locke**, whose usage Hitchcock reconstructs, and **Richard Whately** reshaped it into a fallacy.[^s5] **Douglas Walton** **formalized** the circumstantial version as an argumentation scheme with critical questions, linking the fallacy to **argumentation schemes** generally.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** the Barnes et al. (2018) experiments on attacks against scientists, which show the move's persuasive power,[^s3] and by the van Eemeren, Garssen and Meuffels studies of the **pragma-dialectical** freedom rule, which show people judge it unreasonable.[^s4] It is **challenged by** Hitchcock's essay denying that there is a distinct ad hominem fallacy.[^s5] Because legitimate uses turn on whether we must trust the speaker, it borders on **source credibility** and the **argument from expert opinion**, and like the **straw man** and **red herring** it can divert a discussion from the issue.[^s5][^s1]
