# Fallacy of composition

The fallacy of composition is the error of concluding that a whole has some property because its parts have it, or that a group has a property because each of its members has it, when the property does not actually transfer.[^s1][^s7] Hansen's textbook-style example is a research team: every member is an excellent researcher, so the team must be excellent, yet the team can still fail if its members cannot work together.[^s1] Its mirror image, the fallacy of division, runs the other way, from a whole's properties to those of its parts.[^s1][^s7] The two are easily mixed up because they differ only in direction.[^s5]

## Origin

Aristotle's *On Sophistical Refutations* already listed "combination" and "division" of words among the fallacies that depend on language, but Hansen notes that his versions concern meaning changes produced by grouping or separating words, which differ considerably from the modern part-whole fallacies.[^s1] Richard Whately later called composition and division "semi-logical" fallacies, logical failures whose detection needs knowledge of the terms and subject matter, and in 1826 he counted composition among the commonest and most deceptive errors.[^s1][^s4] J. S. Mill grouped them with fallacies of confusion, and Irving Copi's *Introduction to Logic* placed them among the fallacies of ambiguity alongside equivocation, amphiboly and accent, the classification most later textbooks follow.[^s1]

Economists took up the term independently. Paul Samuelson's *Economics* (1955) defined the fallacy and used it to explain paradoxes such as the paradox of thrift, and a widespread view credits Keynesian economics with exposing it.[^s3]

## How it works

The inference has the shape "every part has F, so the whole has F". Mack's textbook shows that the same shape can be valid or fallacious depending on the property: if every part of a car is plastic, the car is plastic; but if every gymnast on a team weighs under 110 lbs, the team does not.[^s5] Likewise, every team member being on the plane means the team was on the plane, while every member having visited Paris does not mean the team went to Paris.[^s5] Because form alone cannot settle the matter, it is classed as an informal fallacy.[^s5]

The worked example above is a common workplace version. Individual ratings measure what each engineer did in a different setting; team performance also depends on coordination and role coverage, so the premises can be true while the conclusion is false.[^s1]

The best-known economic case is the paradox of thrift. One household that spends less and saves more raises its own saving, but if everyone does so at once, each person's lower spending is someone else's lower income, so total saving may not rise at all. Treating the whole economy like one household is a fallacy of composition.[^s6]

## How to spot it

- Look for a move from "each", "every" or "all the parts" to a claim about the group, system, team or economy as a unit.[^s7]
- Ask whether the property is additive or structural. Van Eemeren and Grootendorst proposed that a property transfers from parts to whole only if it is absolute rather than relative and the whole is unstructured; Finocchiaro reads this as a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.[^s4]
- Separate the three things the label often covers: a term used distributively in the premises and collectively in the conclusion (Copi's claim that because a bus uses more fuel than a car, all buses use more than all cars), a part-to-whole property inference, and a member-to-group inference.[^s4][^s3]
- Distinguish it from hasty generalization: composition reasons from parts to the whole they form, while hasty generalization reasons from a few instances to all members of a class; the AU Press text sets composition and division beside hasty and sweeping generalization in a dedicated comparison.[^s7]

## How to counter it

Name the property and ask for evidence that it carries over: is there a reason the arrangement or interaction of the parts preserves it?[^s7] Look for interaction effects such as crowding, competition or offsetting flows of money, which are what make micro-level truths fail at the macro level.[^s6] Where possible, look for direct evidence about the whole (how the team actually performs, how aggregate saving actually moves) instead of inferring it from the parts.

## Limitations and debate

Not every part-to-whole argument is fallacious, and logicians disagree about how often the fallacy really occurs; Kahane suggested real cases are uncommon even as economists treat it as a major pitfall.[^s4] Finocchiaro argues that compositional arguments span deductive, inductive, analogical and explanatory forms, so the "fallacy of composition" is neither a distinctive kind of argument nor a distinctive kind of error, and he proposes that it may be a special case of oversimplification.[^s3] His 2015 study of historical cases, including Hume's critique of the design argument and critiques of Michels's iron law of oligarchy, treats charges of composition as meta-arguments that need to be argued for case by case.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Confused with** the fallacy of division, its whole-to-part mirror image; Finocchiaro notes the two are frequently conflated.[^s2]
- **Instance of** the fallacies of ambiguity in Copi's widely used classification, though Hansen observes that composition behaves more like a logical failure than an ambiguity.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with** hasty generalization, which generalizes from members of a class rather than from parts of a whole.[^s7]
- **Applied in** economics, where the paradox of thrift is the standard illustration of micro reasoning failing at the macro level.[^s6]
- **Studied in** argumentation theory, where Finocchiaro's historical cases and research problems frame current work on it.[^s2]
- **Related** to Aristotle's *Sophistical Refutations*, Whately's classification, equivocation, amphiboly and the fallacy of accident.[^s1]
