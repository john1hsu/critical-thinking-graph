# Tu quoque

*Tu quoque* (Latin for "you too") is the move of answering a claim or a piece of advice by pointing out that the speaker does not live up to it, and then treating that inconsistency as a reason to reject the claim. It is usually classed as one of the three standard forms of the ad hominem, alongside the abusive and circumstantial versions; the textbook case is dismissing a neighbour's advice to exercise because she never exercises herself, which is fallacious because the value of the advice does not depend on the advisor's integrity.[^s1] Scott Aikin narrows the term to charges of practical inconsistency, that is, hypocrisy, and notes that most introductory textbooks file it under ad hominem.[^s2]

## Origin

The modern tu quoque descends from an older, non-fallacious idea. John Locke described the argument *ad hominem* as pressing someone with consequences drawn from their own principles or concessions, a legitimate way to win agreement even if it does not establish truth; the abusive, circumstantial and tu quoque forms are its later descendants.[^s1] Richard Whately treated ad hominem moves as fallacious only when used unfairly,[^s1] and his example of a sportsman who answers a critic of hunting by asking why the critic eats meat became a classic tu quoque illustration.[^s5] Charles Hamblin's 1970 treatment set the standard modern account, and in 1976 John Woods and Douglas Walton split tu quoque into logical, assertional and practical (praxiological) varieties.[^s2]

## How it works

The fallacy shifts attention from what was said to how the speaker behaves. In the argument map above, a team lead's request to log hours by Friday is met with "but you log yours late". The premises may be true, yet the conclusion that the rule is pointless does not follow: whether prompt logging keeps invoices accurate is a question about invoicing, not about the team lead.[^s1][^s4] What the retort can legitimately do is narrower. Dowden's textbook gives the case of a patient who answers a doctor's smoking warning with "you smoke too" and observes that the patient has a point: actions reveal belief, so a doctor ignoring his own advice is some reason to hesitate, unless other authorities back the advice.[^s4]

## How to spot it

Look for a reply that changes the subject from the claim to the claimant's conduct and then treats the claim as settled. Aikin's analysis supplies the usual weak spots: the two situations are not really alike (an adult telling a child to go to bed early is not a hypocrite, since adults need less sleep), the lapse was a single failure of will rather than a habit, the conduct is long past, or the "hypocrisy" lies in an unrelated area.[^s2] A second sign is a retort that would leave the speaker's independent evidence untouched; the charge is aimed at the person, while the argument's premises stand.[^s1][^s5]

## How to counter it

Separate the two questions the retort runs together: is the claim true, and is the speaker consistent? Concede the second where it is fair and return to the first.[^s5] Aikin describes a "tu quoque judo": when a speaker cannot follow her own advice because of structural constraints, the inconsistency can be reframed as evidence of how much needs to change.[^s2] Admitting the lapse openly also blunts the attack. In five experiments, Jordan and colleagues (2017) found that people judged hypocrites more harshly than people who lied directly about their behaviour, because condemning a wrong falsely signals that one avoids it, while "honest hypocrites" who admitted the lapse were not judged negatively.[^s3]

## Evidence

Whether tu quoque deserves the name "fallacy" is itself debated. David Hitchcock argues that real tu quoque arguments are legitimate. In his view they place a reasonable burden on critics to explain a gap between their words and deeds, and the actual errors involved are better named straw man, false allegation of inconsistency or overreaching from an ad hominem refutation to an absolute one.[^s5] Aikin takes a middle line: hypocrisy cannot falsify a claim, but it can undermine a speaker's authority or show that a proposal is impractical, so some hypocrisy charges are relevant indirectly.[^s2] The psychological work on hypocrisy explains why the retort persuades, not whether it is valid; false-signaling theory is one account, tested in online studies in a single 2017 paper.[^s3]

## Connections

Tu quoque is an instance of the broader **ad hominem** family, distinguished by targeting the gap between a person's words and conduct.[^s1] It is easily **confused with the straw man fallacy**: Hitchcock argues that many supposed tu quoque errors are really misdescriptions of what an opponent did or said.[^s5] His essay **challenges** the standard textbook classification by denying there is a distinct ad hominem fallacy at all.[^s5] The **false-signaling theory of hypocrisy** offers a tentative explanation of why the retort lands so hard with audiences.[^s3] Because the retort often attacks a speaker's trustworthiness rather than the evidence, it overlaps with questions of **source credibility**, and like the **red herring** it diverts attention from the issue; Hamblin's account remains the standard modern starting point.[^s2][^s4]
